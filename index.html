      1. Tokens: the model never sees your letters

      Your text has to become numbers before the model can do anything with it. The part that does this is the tokenizer, and it is the dumbest component in the system. It holds a fixed vocabulary, usually 30,000 to 200,000 entries, and chops your string into the longest pieces it can find in that list. Each piece is a token with an integer id.

      The model never sees your characters. It sees a list of ids.

      Tokens are not words and not letters. They are fragments, chosen before training even began by a blunt rule: whichever byte sequences turned up often in the training text earned their own entry. Common English words got one token each, usually with the leading space attached, so ·the and the are two different tokens with two different ids. Rare words get shredded into four or six pieces.

      Four consequences you will use constantly.

      Pricing and context limits are counted in tokens, not words. English prose runs about four characters per token, so 1,000 tokens is roughly 750 words. Treat that as a planning rule of thumb, never a number to quote. What is stable is the shape: input and output are billed separately, and output costs several times more per token, because writing is serial work while reading is parallel.

      Non-English text costs more for the same meaning. Vocabularies are fit to data that skews heavily English, so a Portuguese, Turkish, or Thai sentence fragments into more tokens than its English translation, sometimes two or three times more. You pay more, and less of it fits in the window. A multilingual product does not have one set of unit economics. It has one per language.

      Letter work fights the representation. Ask the model to count the letter r in a word and it is working over a handful of opaque fragments, not over letters. Same for reversing a string, and for rhyme.

      Digit work fights it too. Long numbers split into chunks that ignore place value, so digit arithmetic is unreliable by construction. Neither weakness is a reasoning failure, so neither is fixed by prompting harder. Give the model a calculator instead. That is a tool call, and it is Chapter 5.

      Common misconception. “The model reads text like we do.” It does not. It reads a sequence of vocabulary ids. Every character-level weakness you have ever seen in a chatbot traces back to this one design choice.

      2. Embeddings: meaning becomes geometry

      An id carries no meaning. Token 2415 is not “more” than token 254. So the first thing the model does is look each id up in a big table called the embedding matrix. Row 2415 is a list of numbers, usually a few hundred to a few thousand of them. That list is the token’s embedding, and you can read it as a position in space. A 100,000-token vocabulary with 4,096 numbers per row is a lookup table holding roughly 400 million learned numbers.

      Those numbers start out random. Training nudges them until tokens that behave alike end up pointing in similar directions.

      Nothing in the model knows what a dog is. It knows that dog sits near puppy, cat, and bark, and nowhere near mortgage. Meaning is stored as position.

      Near is measured with cosine similarity, the cosine of the angle between two vectors. Same direction scores 1, right angles score 0, opposite directions score −1. It ignores length and looks only at direction, which is what you want, because length mostly tracks how often a token appeared rather than what it means.

      Token embeddings are not the embeddings your search index uses

      This one trips people up, and Chapter 4 depends on it. The embeddings above live inside the model, one per token, and they are only a starting point. The layers above rewrite each token’s vector using context, which is how one bank can come out meaning riverbank in one sentence and lender in the next.

      A search index uses a different animal: a sentence or document embedding, one vector for a whole chunk of text, produced by a separate model whose only job is to place similar passages near each other.

      3. Next-token prediction is the whole engine

      In plain words. At the end of every step the model gives a score to every token it knows, then turns those scores into percentages. It picks one and starts over.

      Here is the whole job description. The model takes a sequence of tokens and pushes their vectors through many layers of arithmetic. At the end it takes the vector sitting at the last position, multiplies it by one final matrix, and gets a raw score, a logit, for every single entry in the vocabulary. All 100,000 of them. Then softmax turns those scores into probabilities: every value between 0 and 1, all of them adding to 1.

      That distribution is the model’s entire output. Not a sentence. Not an answer. One probability for each possible next token.

      A sampler picks one. The chosen token is appended to the sequence, and the whole thing runs again on the longer sequence. That loop is called autoregression, and it is why answers arrive one piece at a time.

      The idea in one line. There is no plan, no goal, and no lookahead. The model does not decide what the paragraph will say and then write it. Fluency and apparent intent are side effects of one very good conditional probability, applied a token at a time.

      Hold onto that, because it explains most of the sheet. Hallucination is what happens when the most probable continuation is not the true one. Prompt engineering works because the prompt is the condition in the conditional distribution. Chain-of-thought helps because the intermediate tokens become context for the next prediction. The model is thinking out loud into its own input.

      4. Transformers and attention

      Read this sentence: “The cat drank the milk because it was thirsty.” You knew instantly that it means the cat and not the milk. Nothing in the words themselves says so.

      Attention is the machinery that lets a model do the same thing. It is also the topic that ends interviews when a candidate freezes, so go slowly. It is not actually hard.

      Why the architecture exists

      Before 2017, sequence models were mostly recurrent neural networks. An RNN reads token one, updates a hidden state, reads token two, updates the state, and on down the line. Two problems with that. It is serial, so you cannot compute step 500 before step 499, and a GPU’s whole advantage is doing thousands of things at once. And information from token 3 reaches token 400 only by surviving 397 rounds of being squeezed through one fixed-size state, which it usually does not.

      The transformer drops the recurrence. Every token works out its relationship to every other token in one parallel step. Long-range links become direct instead of relayed, and the layer becomes one big matrix multiplication, which is the thing GPUs do best.

      That is the real reason transformers won. Not smarter per parameter. Cheaper to train on the whole internet.

      What attention actually computes

      In plain words. Every token writes two short notes: what I am looking for, and what I have to offer. Each token then reads everyone else’s offer and takes a blend of the contents, weighted by how well each offer matched.

      Back to the pronoun. To process it, the model needs information from cat. Here is how it gets it. Every token’s vector is multiplied by three separate learned matrices, producing three new vectors:

      • a query (Q) — what this token is looking for;
      • a key (K) — what this token offers to anyone looking;
      • a value (V) — the content this token will hand over if selected.

      Now take the query for it and compare it against the key of every token in the sequence, itself included. The comparison is a dot product, an operation that boils two vectors down to a single number, large when they point in similar directions. So each score answers one question: how relevant is that token to what I am looking for?

      Softmax turns those scores into weights that are all positive and add up to exactly 1. Multiply each token’s value vector by its weight, add them together, and that sum is the new vector for it. It is now mostly made of cat.

      Now the analogy, and only now. A hard lookup table takes a key and returns exactly one value. Attention takes a query, scores every key, and returns a mixture of all the values, weighted by relevance. It is a soft lookup table whose matching rules were learned rather than written. Mechanism first, then that line. Saying only the line is what gets a candidate marked down.

      Many heads, many layers

      One attention operation can express one notion of relevance. So transformers run several at once, typically 8 to 128, each with its own Q, K, and V matrices. That is multi-head attention. Each head starts from different random numbers and gets its own gradients, so heads end up specializing. Researchers have found heads that resolve pronouns, heads that only ever look at the previous token, and plenty of heads nobody has cleanly characterized. Their outputs are stitched together and mixed by one more learned matrix.

      That whole assembly is one transformer block: multi-head attention, a small feed-forward network applied to each position on its own, plus normalization and residual connections that let the original vector pass through alongside the update. A large model stacks dozens of these, sometimes well over a hundred.

      Depth is where the sophistication comes from. Layer 2 can only relate raw tokens. Layer 40 relates representations that already carry whatever layers 1 through 39 found. Composition, not magic.

      Causal masking, and why generation only goes forward

      In plain words. Each position has to guess the next word without peeking at it. The mask is the blindfold.

      In a generative model, position 5 is forbidden from attending to position 6 and anything after it, enforced with a causal mask: before softmax, scores for future positions are set to negative infinity, so their weights come out exactly zero. Without it, training would be useless, because the model would predict token 6 by looking at token 6. The mask is what makes “predict the next token” a real problem, and why autoregression runs left to right.

      Why long context costs more

      With n tokens, attention builds an n × n grid of scores in every head of every layer, so ten times the context is roughly a hundred times the attention arithmetic. Engineers have softened that a great deal, and windows have grown from a couple of thousand tokens to a million in a few years. The scaling has not been repealed. It is why long prompts cost more and answer slower, why “put the whole database in the prompt” is not a strategy, and why retrieval exists.

      The idea in one line. A transformer is a tall stack of blocks; each block lets every token gather a weighted blend of information from every earlier token, using relevance scores it learned. Attention is that gathering step, expressed as query · key → softmax → weighted sum of values.

      5. Weights, pre-training, and post-training

      Every matrix in this chapter is made of weights: the embedding table, the Q, K, and V projections in every head of every layer, the feed-forward networks, the final output matrix. Weights are just numbers. A model described as “70 billion parameters” has 70 billion of them.

      Everything the model can do is stored in those numbers and nowhere else.

      Common misconception, and the important one. “The model learned from our conversation.” It did not. During a conversation the weights are read-only, literally the same file serving thousands of users at once. What feels like learning is your earlier messages still sitting in the context window, being re-read on every turn. Start a new chat and it is gone. If a product genuinely remembers you, that is a database plus retrieval bolted on the outside, not a changed model.

      Weights change only in training, which comes in two phases. Knowing which phase owns a problem is one of the genuinely useful things a PM can hold in their head.

      Pre-training is next-token prediction over an enormous pile of text and code. No labels, no instructions, no human feedback. Just “predict what comes next,” trillions of times. This is where capability comes from: grammar, world knowledge, code, translation, whatever latent reasoning the model has. It is also where nearly all the money goes, months on very large GPU clusters, budgets in the tens or hundreds of millions of dollars at the frontier.

      A raw pre-trained model is a spectacular text continuer and a terrible assistant. Ask it a question and it may hand you five more questions, because that is a plausible continuation.

      Post-training turns it into something usable, in two stages. First, instruction tuning (also called supervised fine-tuning, or SFT): train on curated instructions paired with good responses, so the model learns that a question should be followed by an answer. Then preference-based methods: collect judgments about which of two responses is better and use them to push the model toward the preferred behavior. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is the classic implementation; direct preference optimization and AI feedback against a written constitution are relatives.

      The method is a detail. The category is the point. Post-training installs helpfulness, tone, format-following, refusals, and safety behavior, and it is a rounding error on the pre-training bill.

      6. Why an LLM is not a database you query

      A database stores records. Each row exists somewhere, byte for byte. You can point at it, prove it is there, update it, and delete it. Retrieval is exact by construction.

      A model stores weights. During pre-training, far more text is pushed through the parameters than the parameters could ever hold. That is lossy compression, and it is statistical: what survives is regularities and associations, not copies.

      So when you ask a question, nothing is looked up. The forward pass rebuilds a plausible answer, one token at a time. Where the patterns are strong and consistent — the capital of France appeared identically in a million documents — the rebuild is reliable. Where they are thin or conflicting, as with a citation or your company’s internal policy, the answer comes out just as fluent, because fluency comes from the same machinery. It is simply wrong.

      A database retrieves; a model reconstructs
      Property Database LLM weights
      Storage Exact records Lossy statistical compression
      Access Look up an address Reconstruct by generation

      Recall guarantee Yes, or an explicit error None; a confident guess instead of an error

      Delete one fact DELETE, provable No clean mechanism; an open research area

      Common misconception. “It knows the answer, it just needs a better prompt.” Sometimes true. Often not. If a fact was rare in training, changed after the training cutoff, or lives only in your private systems, there is nothing in the weights to surface and no wording will conjure it. That is an architectural gap, and the fix is to put the fact in the context window with retrieval or a tool call.

      Two later chapters exist because of this. Chapter 3 covers hallucination, which should now be unsurprising: a reconstruction engine with no notion of “no record found” produces something plausible rather than nothing. Chapter 4 covers retrieval-augmented generation. Keep the facts in a real database, fetch the relevant ones at query time, and put them in the prompt so the model is reading rather than remembering.

      The idea in one line. A database retrieves. A model reconstructs. Every difference that matters — provenance, deletability, guaranteed recall, reproducibility — follows from that one distinction.

      Questions this chapter answers

      Answer each one out loud before you open it. The gap between what you said and what is written below is the part you have not learned yet. That gap is the whole point of this section.

      “How does an LLM actually work?”

      Text is split into tokens, each token becomes a vector, a stack of transformer layers lets every token gather information from every earlier token, and the model outputs a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary for the next token. It samples one, appends it, and repeats.

      If they want more, walk the pipeline in order: tokenizer to ids; ids to embeddings via a learned lookup table; dozens of transformer blocks, each one multi-head attention plus a per-position feed-forward network, wrapped in residual connections and normalization; a final projection to one logit per vocabulary entry; softmax; sample; loop. Then name the thing that makes it coherent. The model is not executing a plan. It is repeatedly answering “what token is most likely next, given everything so far.” The weights do not move while you talk to it.

      Going deeper: saying what the model outputs, a distribution over the entire vocabulary rather than a sentence, and naming where the cost lives, since attention is quadratic in sequence length and generation is serial. That is the difference between understanding the mechanism and reciting the vocabulary.

      “What is next-token prediction?”

      The model reads a sequence and produces a probability for every possible next token. One is sampled, appended to the sequence, and the process repeats — that loop is autoregression, and it is the model’s only real operation.

      Two details worth adding. The output is a full distribution over tens or hundreds of thousands of entries, produced by softmax over raw logits, not a single guess, which is what makes sampling controls like temperature meaningful. And there is no lookahead: the model does not choose the ending of a sentence before writing its beginning. Coherence emerges because each token is conditioned on all previous tokens, including the ones the model just wrote. That one fact explains why prompt wording matters so much, why chain-of-thought helps, and why long generations drift.

      Going deeper: explicitly killing the intentionality framing. “It has no plan, no goal, and no representation of the answer it is heading toward — fluency is a side effect of a very good conditional distribution.” Then note the cost consequence: output tokens are serial, so they are slower and priced higher than input tokens.

      “Explain how transformers work”

      A transformer is a deep stack of identical blocks. Each block runs multi-head attention, which lets every token gather a weighted blend of information from every other token in one parallel step, followed by a small feed-forward network applied to each position, wrapped in residual connections and normalization.

      Give the motivation, because it shows you know why the design exists. RNNs processed tokens strictly in order, so they could not be parallelized on GPUs, and long-range information had to survive hundreds of sequential updates through one fixed-size state. Attention replaces that with direct, all-to-all connections computed as one large matrix multiplication, which is what made training on internet-scale data affordable. Then add depth: early layers relate raw tokens, later layers relate representations that already encode earlier relationships. In a generative model a causal mask zeroes out attention to future positions, which is what makes next-token prediction a real task and forces generation to run left to right.

      Going deeper: naming the cost. The attention grid is n × n per head per layer, so context is quadratic, and a key-value cache grows with sequence length on top of that. This is why context windows are finite and expensive, and it connects the architecture directly to your unit economics.

      “What is attention?”

      Every token produces a query, a key, and a value vector. A token’s query is dot-producted with every key to get relevance scores; softmax turns those into weights that sum to 1; the output is the weighted sum of the value vectors. It is a soft, learned lookup table.

      Make it concrete. In “The cat drank the milk because it was thirsty,” the token it issues a query. Its dot product with the key of cat is large, with the it is small. Scores are scaled by the square root of the head dimension, softmax turns them into weights, and most of the weight lands on cat, so the new vector for it is mostly built out of cat’s value vector. That is coreference resolved by arithmetic. Multi-head attention runs many of these in parallel with separate learned projections, so heads can specialize, and stacking layers lets later heads work on relationships that earlier ones already found.

      Going deeper: giving the mechanism before the metaphor, and knowing why it is soft. A hard lookup returns one value and is not differentiable; a weighted blend of all values is, which is the only reason the matching rules can be learned by gradient descent at all.

      “What are tokens and why do they matter?”

      Tokens are the subword chunks a tokenizer splits text into — the only units the model ever sees. They matter because they are the unit of billing, the unit of the context limit, and the reason the model is weak at character-level and digit-level work.

      The vocabulary is fixed before training and typically holds tens of thousands to a couple hundred thousand entries, chosen so that frequent byte sequences get their own token. English prose runs about four characters per token, so 1,000 tokens is roughly 750 words; treat that as an order of magnitude, not a fact. Then the four consequences: pricing and context are token-denominated, with output priced well above input; non-English text fragments into more tokens for the same meaning, so margins differ by market; letter-level tasks such as counting characters fight the representation; and long numbers split in ways that ignore place value, so arithmetic belongs in a tool rather than in a better prompt.

      Going deeper: connecting tokenization to a real decision. A multilingual launch changes both cost per request and effective context length, so the same feature can be profitable in English and unprofitable in Thai. Measure that before you price, not after.

      “What are embeddings?”

      An embedding is a vector of numbers representing a piece of text, learned so that similar things sit close together in the space. Meaning becomes geometry, and similarity becomes a measurable angle — usually cosine similarity.

      Then draw the distinction that matters in practice. Token embeddings live inside the model: each token id indexes a row of a learned matrix, and that vector is the input to the first transformer layer. It starts out context-free, and the layers above rewrite it using attention, which is how one bank token can end up meaning riverbank in one sentence and lender in another. Sentence or document embeddings are a different thing entirely: one vector for a whole passage, produced by a separate and usually much smaller model, stored in a vector database, and compared by cosine similarity to answer “which chunks are most like this query.” That is the retrieval half of a RAG system, and it does not need the generating model at all.

      Going deeper: being unambiguous about which kind you mean, and noting that cosine similarity measures direction and deliberately ignores magnitude. Bonus: semantic similarity is not the same as relevance or correctness, which is exactly why naive vector search retrieves passages that look related and answer nothing.

      “What are model weights and when do they change?”

      Weights are the billions of learned numbers that make up the model — the embedding table, the query, key and value projections in every head, the feed-forward layers, the output matrix. They change only during training or fine-tuning. They never change during a conversation.

      Everything the model can do is stored in those values and nowhere else. At inference the weights are read-only, and the same file serves many users at once. What people mistake for learning is context: your earlier turns are still in the window and are re-read on every request, so the model appears to remember. Open a new chat and it is gone. Products that genuinely remember a user do it with external storage plus retrieval, not by modifying the model. Changing weights means a deliberate offline job, and it costs real money and needs evaluation before and after.

      Going deeper: stating the security and compliance implication out loud. “Sending data in a prompt does not train the model by default — whether your data is retained or used for training is a contractual and configuration question about the provider, not a property of the architecture.” That is the version of this answer a legal or enterprise stakeholder needs.

      “What is the difference between pre-training and post-training?”

      Pre-training is next-token prediction over a vast corpus and produces raw capability at enormous cost. Post-training is instruction tuning plus preference-based methods, and produces helpfulness, format-following, tone, and refusals. Pre-training builds the engine; post-training builds the car around it.

      Pre-training uses no labels and no human feedback, just prediction repeated trillions of times, and it is where grammar, world knowledge, code, translation, and latent reasoning come from. It also carries nearly all of the compute bill. The output is a model that continues documents beautifully and takes instructions badly. Post-training fixes that in two stages: supervised fine-tuning on curated instruction-and-response pairs, then preference optimization, where human or AI judgments about which response is better are used to shift behavior. RLHF is the classic recipe; direct preference optimization and constitution-style AI feedback are relatives. The algorithm is a detail. The category is the point.

      Going deeper: using it as a triage rule. Most behavior a PM complains about, verbosity, over-refusal, ignoring the output schema, wrong tone, is post-training, and is often addressable with prompting or fine-tuning. Most capability a PM wants, can it reason about this domain at all, is pre-training, and fine-tuning will not install a capability the base model never had.

      “Why isn’t an LLM a database you query?”

      Because a database retrieves and a model reconstructs. Weights are a lossy, statistical compression of the training data, not a set of records, so there is no row to look up, no pointer to a source, and no guarantee of recall.

      Far more text goes into pre-training than the model has parameters to hold, so what survives is regularities and associations rather than copies. A forward pass regenerates a plausible answer from those patterns token by token. When a fact was frequent and consistent, the reconstruction is reliable; when it was rare, contested, or absent, the output is equally fluent and possibly false, because fluency and accuracy come from the same machinery. The consequences: no provenance, so you cannot cite; no “record not found” error, so failure looks like confidence; no clean way to update or delete a single fact, which is a real problem under data-deletion obligations; and no guarantee of identical output for identical input once sampling is involved.

      Going deeper: going straight to the architectural fix. If you need provenance, freshness, access control, or auditable deletion, keep the facts in a system that has those properties and put the relevant ones in the context window at query time. That is retrieval-augmented generation, and framing it as “a database for facts, the model for language” is what a senior candidate says.

      The model does not choose a word

      Chapter 1 stopped at a specific moment. The forward pass finished, and what came out was not a word. It was a logit vector: one raw, unbounded score for every token in the vocabulary, usually 50,000 to 200,000 of them. Softmax turned those scores into a probability distribution, every number between 0 and 1, all of them adding to 1.

      That is the entire output of the model. A ranked opinion about what could come next. Not a decision.

      Something else has to turn that ranking into one concrete token to append to the text. That something is the sampler, and the rules it follows are called the decoding strategy. The sampler is a few lines of ordinary code sitting outside the neural network. It has no weights. It learned nothing. It reads the probabilities and returns an index.

      Hold on to that boundary and the temperature question answers itself. Temperature is not a setting inside the model’s thinking. It is a parameter of the code that picks.

      The idea in one line. The model produces a distribution; the decoder produces a token. Everything you tune with temperature, top_p, or top_k lives in the decoder, not in the model.

      Temperature, mechanically

      In plain words. Temperature is a volume knob on the model’s confidence. Turn it down and the favorite wins every time; turn it up and the also-rans get a real chance.

      Mechanically it is one division. Call the raw scores z and the temperature T. Every score is divided by T before softmax runs, so the model computes softmax(z / T) instead of softmax(z). That is the whole mechanism.

      Divide by a number below 1 and every score gets bigger, and so does the gap between scores. Softmax exponentiates, so a wider gap becomes a dramatically wider probability ratio. The leader runs away with everything. Divide by a number above 1 and the scores squeeze toward each other, the gaps shrink, and the distribution slides toward uniform, toward every token being equally likely.

      A concrete case. The prompt is “The capital of France is” and suppose only four tokens have scores worth anything: logits of 3.0, 1.0, 0.5, and 0.0.

      Four tokens understate the effect badly. A real vocabulary runs to something like 100,000 tokens, and almost all of them are nonsense in any given position. Flattening the distribution moves probability mass out of the handful of sensible tokens and into that enormous tail of garbage. High temperature does not buy you interesting writing. It buys you text that falls apart.

      Common misconception. Temperature is not a creativity dial, a confidence dial, or an effort dial. It controls exactly one thing: how flat the probability distribution is before a token is drawn from it. The varied phrasing, the extra invention, the incoherence — all of it is downstream of that flatness. A model given a low temperature does not think harder. It has just stopped taking chances on its own second-choice tokens.

      Zero, two, and the other knob

      At T = 0 the formula would divide by zero, so implementations special-case it. They skip sampling and take the argmax, the single highest-probability token, at every step. This is greedy decoding. Set T = 0 and you have asked for the model’s most likely continuation and nothing else. Run it twice on the same input and you should get the same text. You often will. You are not guaranteed to, for reasons in the next section that have nothing to do with sampling.

      At T = 2 you are at the top of the range most APIs expose, and the distribution is flat enough that the model’s ranking barely constrains the output. The text starts well, drifts, then degrades into invented words, broken syntax, abandoned sentences. Once one low-probability token lands, it is in the context, and every token after it is conditioned on nonsense. The loop feeds its own output back in, so errors compound instead of averaging out.

      Temperature is not the only knob. Top-p, also called nucleus sampling, cuts the tail off instead of reshaping the whole distribution. Sort the tokens by probability and add them up from the top, stopping as soon as the running total reaches p. Everything below the cut is thrown away, what is left is renormalized, and the draw happens there. In the T = 1.0 example, top-p = 0.9 keeps Paris, Lyon, and Nice, whose probabilities add to 96%, and deletes “Tokyo” outright. Not low probability. Zero. Top-k is the blunter cousin: keep the k highest-scoring tokens regardless of what their probabilities are.

      Temperature changes how much the model’s preferences matter. Top-p sets a floor on how bad a token is allowed to be. They compose, which is why tuning both at once is a good way to confuse yourself. Move one, and leave the other at its default.

      Where on the range each kind of task belongs. These are starting points to measure against your own evaluation set, not settings to memorize.
      Task Range Why
      Extraction, classification, routing, structured output 0 to 0.2 There is one right answer. Variation is pure downside, and reproducibility makes debugging possible.
      Summarization, rewriting, code generation 0.2 to 0.6 Slight variation is harmless and sometimes escapes a bad phrasing, but the content has to stay faithful.
      Ideation, name generation, drafting several options 0.7 to 1.0 Variety is the product. Generating five samples and picking one only works if the five actually differ.
      Anything at all 1.5 to 2.0 Effectively nothing. Reach for it to demonstrate degradation, or to sample a deliberately weird distribution for research.

      Why the same prompt gives you a different answer

      Most people answer this with “sampling is random” and stop. That is one cause out of four, and the only one you can fix from your side by changing a parameter. Naming the rest is what signals you have debugged a production system.

      1. The sampler drew a different token. Above T = 0 it is drawing from a distribution. Different draw, different token, and one different token early on sends the whole continuation somewhere else. Some providers accept a seed parameter to fix the draw. It helps. It does not close the gap.

      In plain words. Two machines adding the same numbers in a different order can disagree in the last decimal place. Sometimes that is enough to change which token wins.

      2. The machinery underneath is not bit-for-bit repeatable. Floating-point addition is not associative, so (a + b) + c can come out different from a + (b + c) in the last bits. A GPU computes one big sum by splitting it across thousands of threads, and the order their partial results combine in is not fixed. Your request is also batched with whatever other requests arrived at the same moment, and batch size decides which kernel implementation runs. The largest models add one more layer: mixture-of-experts routing sends each token to a few specialized sub-networks with a capacity limit per batch, so your batch neighbors can change where your token goes.

      The consequence for you is narrow and specific. Same weights, same prompt, logits that differ in the twelfth decimal place. Almost always irrelevant. But when the top two tokens are nearly tied, that difference flips the argmax, and greedy decoding dutifully walks down a different path. That is why T = 0 is near-deterministic rather than a promise.

      3. The model changed under you. Call an alias like -latest and the snapshot behind it can be replaced. Providers also update system prompts, safety layers, and serving stacks without a version bump you can see. Your input was identical. The thing you sent it to was not.

      4. Your prompt was not actually identical. The most common cause in real products, and the most embarrassing. A timestamp in the system prompt. A retrieval step that returned the same documents in a different order. A conversation history one turn longer. A tool result with a new request ID. You compared what you typed, not what was sent.

      The idea in one line. Model output is a sample from a distribution served by non-deterministic infrastructure, so you cannot write assert output == "expected string" and expect it to hold. Evaluation has to test properties — does it parse, does it contain the right entity, does a judge rate it acceptable — across many runs. Chapter 7 builds that out.

      The context window is a desk, not a memory

      The context window is the maximum number of tokens the model can process in a single forward pass. It is a hard architectural limit, counted in tokens, and it is shared by everything involved in the call:

      • the system prompt, with every instruction and policy you wrote;
      • every tool definition, meaning names, descriptions, and full argument schemas, which get large fast;
      • the entire conversation history you chose to resend;
      • any retrieved documents or file contents you pasted in;
      • the user’s actual message;
      • and the space the output needs, because generated tokens are appended to the same sequence.

      That last item catches people. Fill 127,000 tokens of a 128,000-token window with input and the model has 1,000 tokens left to answer in. Budget the output on purpose.

      The API is stateless. The model retains nothing between calls. It has no memory of your last message. When a chat product appears to remember the conversation, the client is resending the whole transcript every turn. The model reads it fresh, as if for the first time, because it is the first time.

      Now the desk analogy earns its place. Everything the model can use has to be laid out on the desk before the forward pass, and the desk is wiped clean afterward. “The model forgot what I told it” is, nine times out of ten, “your application stopped sending that part.”

      In plain words. Every turn you send the whole conversation again, and you pay for all of it again. Turn 20 is not one turn. It is turns 1 through 20.

      That explains a cost curve that surprises teams. A 20-turn conversation does not cost 20 units, because turn 20 pays for turns 1 through 19 all over again. Total input tokens grow with the square of the number of turns.

      Overflowing, and the tax on long windows

      What happens when your request exceeds the window depends on which layer notices first, and the three behaviors are dangerously different.

      A hard error. Raw API calls reject the request outright, with a message along the lines of “maximum context length exceeded.” This is the good case. It is loud, and you find it in testing.

      Silent truncation. Many chat interfaces and SDK helpers quietly drop the oldest messages to make room. The call succeeds. The answer looks plausible. The constraint the user stated in turn two is no longer in the request, and nothing in the response tells you.

      The middle disappears. Some frameworks and routers keep the start and the end and remove content from the middle, on the reasoning that the system prompt and the recent turns matter most. Often correct, occasionally catastrophic, and almost never surfaced in a log you read.

      Common misconception. Hitting the context limit is not reliably an error. Silent truncation is a normal, documented behavior of several popular layers, and it is why a bug report saying “the assistant ignored my requirement” should send you to the request payload before it sends you to the model.

      The mitigations, and what each one costs:

      • Sliding window. Keep the last N turns. Cheap, trivial to implement, and it throws away exactly the early turns where users tend to state constraints.
      • Summarization or compaction. Periodically replace old turns with a model-written summary. It preserves the gist in a much smaller budget, but it costs an extra model call, it is lossy in ways you cannot predict, and summaries of summaries drift with every cycle.
      • Retrieval instead of stuffing. Keep the corpus outside the window and fetch only the relevant pieces per turn. It scales to unbounded knowledge, and it buys you a new subsystem that can fail by fetching the wrong thing. Chapter 5 is about that trade.

      Even when everything fits, position inside the window matters. Models attend more reliably to content near the beginning and near the end of a long context than to content buried in the middle. Plot accuracy at finding a planted fact against where that fact sits, and you get a U-shape. The effect is known as lost in the middle. It has been reproduced across many models and architectures, its severity varies by model and by task, and nobody should claim it is fully solved.

      Two things follow. A bigger window is not a free win, because a million-token context does not mean a million usable tokens, and stuffing everything in because it fits will make retrieval quality worse rather than better. And the fix is often just ordering: instructions at the top, the most important retrieved document at the bottom nearest the question, and any hard constraint repeated at the end.

      Then there is the bill. Chapter 1 showed that attention compares every token to every other token, so its compute grows with the square of the sequence length. Double the context and you roughly quadruple the work of reading it. Providers charge per token rather than per unit of compute, so the input price scales roughly linearly. The compute shows up elsewhere: in latency, mostly time-to-first-token while the model reads your prompt, and in the premium tiers that long-context models tend to carry.

      The idea in one line. “Just use the million-token model” is a budget decision with three separate costs — money per call, latency per call, and degraded attention to the middle. Deciding what earns a place in the window is the actual work, and Chapter 4 is about how to do it.

      “What does temperature actually control?”

      Temperature divides the logits before softmax, which changes how flat the next-token probability distribution is. It is a parameter of the sampler, not of the model.

      The forward pass produces a logit, a raw score, for every token in the vocabulary, and softmax turns those into probabilities. Temperature T makes it softmax(z / T). Below 1, the gaps between scores widen, softmax exponentiates them, and the top token takes almost all the mass. Above 1, the gaps compress and the distribution slides toward uniform. With logits of 3.0, 1.0, 0.5, and 0.0, the top token holds about 79% at T = 1.0, effectively 100% at T = 0.2, and only about 53% at T = 2.0. The model produced identical logits in all three cases.

      Going deeper: refusing the framing that temperature is a creativity or confidence dial, and naming the second knob. Top-p truncates the tail, keeping the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability reaches p and deleting the rest, so it sets a floor on how bad a token may be. Temperature instead rescales how much the model’s ranking counts at all.

      “What is the difference between temperature 0 and temperature 2?”

      T = 0 is greedy decoding: always take the highest-probability token, so output is near-deterministic. T = 2 flattens the distribution so far that low-probability tokens get drawn and the output degrades into incoherence.

      Zero is a special case in the code, since you cannot divide by it, so implementations skip sampling and take the argmax each step. Two is typically the top of the exposed range. The reason it breaks down is scale: a real vocabulary has around 100,000 tokens, nearly all of them nonsense at any given position, so flattening moves mass out of the few sensible tokens into that tail. And because each generated token is fed back into the context, one bad draw conditions everything after it, so errors compound rather than average out. In practice: 0 to 0.2 for extraction, classification, and routing, where variation is pure downside; 0.7 to 1.0 when you want genuinely different drafts to choose between; nothing real at 2.

      Going deeper: saying “near-deterministic” rather than “deterministic” for T = 0, and being able to explain why. Floating-point non-associativity plus variable request batching on the provider side can shift the logits in the last bits, which flips the argmax whenever the top two tokens are nearly tied.

      “Why do two identical prompts give different answers?”

      Four reasons, and only the first is sampling. Random draws above T = 0, serving machinery that is not bit-for-bit repeatable, a silently changed model or system prompt, and prompts that were not actually identical.

      Sampling randomness is the obvious one and the only one a parameter fixes. The deeper one is arithmetic: floating-point addition is not associative, GPUs sum in an unfixed thread order, your request is batched with other traffic which changes which kernel runs, and mixture-of-experts models route tokens to sub-networks with per-batch capacity limits. Identical input, bit-level different logits, and a flipped argmax whenever the top two tokens are nearly tied. Third, a -latest alias or an updated safety layer means the system you called changed. Fourth and most common in real products: a timestamp, a reordered retrieval result, or one extra history turn means you compared what you typed, not what you sent.

      Going deeper: the product consequence. You cannot write an equality assertion against model output, so tests have to assert properties — valid schema, required entity present, a rubric or judge score above threshold — measured across repeated runs rather than once.

      “What is a context window and what happens when you exceed it?”

      The context window is the total token budget for one forward pass, shared by the system prompt, tool definitions, history, retrieved documents, the user message, and the output. Exceed it and you get either a hard API error or, worse, silent truncation.

      Start from the fact that the API is stateless. The model remembers nothing between calls, so a conversation only persists because the client resends the entire transcript each turn. That is also why long conversations get expensive: total input tokens grow with the square of the turn count. On overflow, behavior depends on the layer. A raw API call errors out, which is the good case because it is loud. Chat interfaces and SDK helpers often drop the oldest messages, so the call succeeds and a constraint from turn two is quietly gone. Some frameworks remove the middle instead. Mitigations are a sliding window (cheap, loses early constraints), summarization or compaction (compact, lossy, costs an extra call, drifts when repeated), or retrieval (scales, adds a subsystem that can fetch the wrong thing).

      Going deeper: adding that a bigger window is not a free upgrade. Models recall content at the start and end of a long context more reliably than the middle, so position is a design variable, and because attention cost grows with the square of sequence length, long contexts are slower and more expensive. Choosing what earns a place in the window beats buying more window.

      Two different failures wearing one name

      Start with two bad answers.

      You paste a customer contract into a model and ask which law governs it. It says New York. That is correct. The customer really is on the New York paper. But the contract you supplied never says so; the model pulled it from memory. Second case: the contract says Delaware, the contract is out of date, and the model tells you Delaware. It read the document perfectly and handed you something false.

      Both answers are broken, in opposite directions, and that difference decides what you build.

      The first was true but not in the source. The second was in the source but not true. Those are the two axes. Factuality asks whether a claim is true about the world. Faithfulness, also called grounding, asks the narrower question: is it supported by the material you put in the prompt? Neither implies the other, and mashing them together is most of what the OpenAI “define hallucinations” question is checking for.

      Now the definition worth memorizing. A hallucination is output that is fluent, confident, and not supported. Fluent, because it reads exactly like the true sentences around it. Confident, because the model gives you no signal that this sentence differs from any other. Not supported, because nothing stands behind it: not the world, not the document you handed over.

      The distinction is not academic. The two axes cost wildly different amounts to check. “Is this true about the world?” needs an oracle you do not have. “Is this in the documents I retrieved?” compares two strings already in front of you, and a program can do that on every response.

      The idea in one line. Good product design converts factuality problems into faithfulness problems, because faithfulness is the one you can actually measure.

      Why it happens, mechanically

      In plain words. The model is picking the next word, over and over, based on what usually comes next. Nothing in that loop ever stops to ask whether the sentence is true.

      From Chapter 1: the model computes a probability over the next token given everything before it, then samples. A few hundred rounds is your answer. Four things about that loop produce hallucination.

      One: there is no truth check in it. No step consults a database. No step compares the sentence so far against the world. The model optimizes for plausibility, meaning how typical this continuation is. Plausible and true overlap heavily, which is the only reason any of this works. They are still not the same quantity, and nothing in the architecture prefers the second.

      Two: there is no abstain button. “I don’t know” is not a special move. It is another string, competing on plausibility against the answer-shaped string. In the training corpus a question is almost always followed by an answer. Someone asking for a case citation and getting a polite refusal is rare. The answer wins on the statistics before your instructions get a vote.

      Three: the confidence signal you want does not exist. The model does produce a probability for every token, and a high one is tempting to read as certainty. It is not. That number says the continuation is typical, not that it is correct. And most output lives in the high-confidence region, so that is where most wrong answers live in absolute terms. Confident-and-wrong is not separable from confident-and-right by probability alone.

      In plain words. The share of answers that are wrong does drop as the model gets more confident. But nearly everything it says is high-confidence, so the confident pile is still where most of the wrong answers sit.

      Four: training rewards answering over abstaining. Post-training and almost every public benchmark score whether the answer was right. An abstention scores zero. A guess scores more than zero in expectation. It is a multiple-choice exam with no penalty for wrong answers, where leaving a question blank can only hurt you, and the model learned that.

      One more piece from Chapter 1 tells you where it breaks. The weights are a lossy compression of the corpus, not an index. A fact repeated ten thousand times survives the squeeze. A fact seen once or twice does not, and what is left in its place is a blurry region of plausible-shaped answers. So the failure rate is not uniform. It spikes on rare, precise specifics: citations, case numbers, dosages, dates, API parameter names, any figure with a decimal point in it.

      Common misconception. Hallucination is not noise sprinkled evenly over the output. It is a systematic failure with a predictable shape: the rarer and more precise the fact, the higher the rate. That tells you where to build your eval set.

      Why a longer, sterner prompt cannot fix it

      The favorite follow-up is some version of “so why not just tell it not to?” The answer is no, for three reasons.

      First, “do not hallucinate” is not an instruction anything can follow. Obeying it would require detecting which of its own claims are unsupported, which is exactly the capability it lacks. An instruction can direct behavior the model can perform. This one names an outcome. It is like telling a person “do not misremember.”

      Second, it adds no information. If the fact is not in the context and not reliably in the weights, no wording conjures it.

      Third, force has side effects. Capitals and threats do shift the distribution, usually toward hedging and refusal. Fewer wrong answers, more useless ones. Sometimes that is the right trade. Be honest that it is one: you moved your failures into the category where users leave without complaining.

      What prompting can and cannot do about hallucination
      Prompt move What it actually changes What it still does not fix
      “Do not hallucinate. Be accurate.” Nothing you can rely on. There is no self-check to switch on. Everything.
      Capitals, threats, “this is critical” Shifts the distribution. Raises refusals and hedging. Trades wrong answers for useless ones.
      “Answer only from the text below.” Concentrates attention on the context; measurably raises grounding. Cannot supply a fact the context lacks.
      “If it is not in the context, say you could not find it.” Makes abstaining a legal move, so it happens far more often. The model still decides when, and decides badly.

      The idea in one line. Every prompt move that works does one of two things: it makes the safe action available, or it produces something a program can check. None of them installs a fact checker. Prompting is a steering tool, not a correctness mechanism.

      What actually works: a layered defense

      Here is the shape of a strong answer. You do not remove hallucination. You drive two numbers down: how often it happens, and what one occurrence costs. Every layer below moves one of those numbers, and every layer leaks.

      Reduce the need to recall. Retrieve the relevant documents into the context, so facts come from a source rather than from compressed memory. Largest lever, and it is Chapter 4. It leaks twice: retrieval can fetch the wrong document, and the model can still drift from what it retrieved.

      Constrain the task. A model choosing among six listed labels cannot invent a seventh if you validate the output. Fixed fields beat free prose. Ask for less, get less invention.

      Make it verifiable. Require every claim to carry a quoted span from the source, then check in code that the quoted string is really there. A string match: cheap, deterministic, no model involved. It kills the fabricated-citation class outright. What leaks is a real quote paired with an inference it does not support.

      Verify after generation. A second pass whose only job is judging each claim against the source. A schema validator. A lookup confirming the product ID exists. And for anything that has to be exact (pricing, dosage, tax) a rules engine or a database query, because a language model should never be the thing computing a number someone relies on. What leaks: your judge is also a model, and one from the same family shares the generator’s blind spots.

      Give it an exit. An abstain path the product understands, like “I could not find this in your documents” next to a search box, plus a route to a human. That turns a wrong answer into a slow one, and slow is nearly always cheaper.

      Design the interface for it. Sources inline beside the claim they support. Honest hedging. Output editable by default. Generated text never written into a record of authority without a human action. None of this lowers the rate. All of it lowers the damage, and it recruits the user as your last verifier.

      Measure and contain. A faithfulness number on a real eval set, watched every release (Chapter 7). Then a choice about where the feature lives. The same model at the same rate is fine drafting a reply a human reads, and unacceptable auto-approving a claim.

      The idea in one line. You do not ship a hallucination fix. You ship an error rate you have measured, at a cost per error you can absorb.

      Grounding, and how you actually measure it

      Grounding is holding output to the sources you provide. A grounded claim is one that a specific span of source text supports. Everything else is ungrounded, however true it happens to be, and in a product someone has to audit that is a defect.

      In plain words. Split the answer into separate claims. For each one, ask whether the source actually says it. The score is the share that pass.

      You count claims rather than answers, because “was this answer good?” is not a question two people answer the same way. So take an eval set of real inputs with their sources. Break each output into single claims. Mark each one supported, unsupported, or contradicted. Report the share supported, and report contradictions on their own line: a claim that fights the source is a different bug from one the source is merely silent about.

      Somebody has to produce those labels. People working from a written rubric are your ground truth: slow, expensive, and worthless without the rubric, because two annotators left to their instincts disagree on half the borderline claims. A model can label instead, told to quote the span it relied on, cheaply enough to run every release. Check its labels against human ones on a sample, and again after any model change, since a judge from the generator’s own family shares its blind spots.

      Two honest caveats. Where one claim stops and the next begins is a judgment call, so write the procedure down and hold it fixed. And a support rate hides severity: one contradicted dosage is not ten unsupported adjectives, so segment by claim type.

      Common misconception. “We improved grounding” with no eval set behind it means “we changed the prompt and it felt better.” No metric without an eval set. And the eval set is the real asset: it outlives the prompt, the model, and the vendor.

      Upstairs: error budgets and redlines

      Explaining it to an exec

      No mechanism, no jargon, and a number. Roughly this, in forty seconds:

      “The model is a very good guesser, not a lookup table. It always answers, and it cannot tell us when it is guessing. On our eval set of two hundred real customer questions, six percent of answers contain a claim our documents do not support. We have capped what that costs: every answer shows its sources, nothing reaches a customer without a human click, and pricing comes from the database rather than the model. We report that six percent every release. It will not reach zero.”

      Then the reframe that lands. This is reliability engineering with an error budget, the same as uptime. Nobody asks when hundred-percent uptime ships. They ask what the target is, what moving it costs, and what happens when we breach it. Ask for those three things here and the conversation becomes a normal one.

      What not to say. “We’ll fix it in the prompt” promises a fix you cannot deliver. “The next model will fix it” hands your roadmap to a vendor. “It’s a known limitation of LLMs” is true and useless. And never quote a rate you cannot source.

      Redlines

      A redline is a category of output that is never acceptable at any quality bar. It is not a threshold on a metric you optimize. It is binary, and when it is crossed the right response is to block, degrade, or roll back the feature rather than tune it.

      Most of your product surface is a curve you push. A small named set is a wall.

      A good redline is specific enough to write a test for. It is tied to a concrete harm rather than a feeling. It has a named owner, an action agreed in advance for the day it is violated, and an adversarial suite written by someone whose job is to break it. Settle that before launch, while nobody is under shipping pressure.

      Take an assistant answering clinician questions over patient records. First redline: it never states a dose that is not copied verbatim from the record. The test is three hundred adversarial prompts that invite a dose, any generated dose counts as a violation, and the field renders as a source quote or renders blank. Second: it never asserts that a patient does not have a condition, because absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, so negative claims route to “not found in the records provided.”

      Going deeper: pointing out that raising refusals is a failure too, so “the stern prompt reduced hallucinations” may just mean you moved failures into a category nobody reports.

      “What is grounding and how do you measure it?”

      Grounding is holding output to the sources you provided. You measure it claim by claim: split the output, check whether a source span supports each claim, and report the support rate on a labeled eval set.

      Concretely: assemble an eval set of real inputs with their sources, weighted toward the hard cases. Split each output into single claims. Label each one supported, unsupported, or contradicted, and report the support rate and the contradiction rate separately, because a claim the source contradicts is a different bug from one the source is silent about. Labels come from three places. People with a written rubric are ground truth. A judge model, required to quote the span it relied on, is cheap enough to run every release, but check it against human labels on a sample and again whenever the model changes, since a same-family judge shares the generator’s blind spots. And a small specialized model can answer whether one sentence supports another, cheaply, but it breaks on long context and arithmetic. No metric without an eval set: the eval set outlives the prompt, the model, and the vendor.

      Going deeper: saying that a support rate hides severity, so you segment by claim type. One contradicted dosage is not one-tenth as bad as ten unsupported adjectives.

      “OpenAI: Define hallucinations in LLMs”

      A hallucination is output that is fluent and confident but not supported: either false about the world, or absent from the sources provided. Those are two separate axes, factuality and faithfulness.

      Factuality asks whether the claim is true about the world. Faithfulness, or grounding, asks whether the claim is supported by the material in the prompt. They are independent, and conflating them is the usual mistake. A summary can be factual and unfaithful: the model adds a true fact that was never in the document, which is fine for a chat toy and a defect in a product someone audits. It can be faithful and false, when the source was wrong and the model copied it honestly. Faithfulness is cheap to check, because both strings are in front of you and a program can compare them, while factuality needs an oracle you do not have. So you measure them separately, and you design the product to turn as much of the factuality problem as possible into a faithfulness problem. Confidence is part of the definition too: the model presents an unsupported claim in the same register as a supported one.

      Going deeper: giving the two axes with a concrete example in each off-diagonal quadrant, then why the distinction changes what you build.

      “Anthropic: Handling hallucinations in a deployed AI model?”

      You do not eliminate it, you engineer it. A layered defense that lowers the rate, product decisions that lower the cost of each occurrence, and a metric you watch every release.

      The stack, strongest first. Retrieve sources into the context so facts do not come from compressed memory. Constrain the task: narrow scope, listed options, structured output. Require quoted source spans and check in code that the quote really appears. Verify after generation: a claim check, a schema validator, and a rules engine or database for anything that must be exact, like pricing or dosage. Give it an exit, an abstain path and a route to a human, which converts a wrong answer into a slow one. Design the interface for it: sources inline, honest hedging, editable output, generated text never presented as an authoritative record. Measure claim-level faithfulness on a real eval set. And put the feature where a wrong answer is cheap. I would also watch abstention rate and human-override rate, because a falling hallucination rate with a climbing refusal rate is not progress.

      Going deeper: naming what leaks through each layer. Retrieval fetches the wrong document, a real quote can support a bad inference, a same-family judge shares the generator’s blind spots.

      “Anthropic: How would you define a ‘redline’ for a model?”

      A redline is a category of output that is never acceptable at any quality bar, where the response to a violation is to block or degrade the feature rather than tune it. What makes it a redline is that you accept a worse product to hold it.

      Contrast it with a quality metric. A support rate above ninety-two percent is a curve. You push it, you trade it against latency and cost, and eighty-eight percent still ships while you work on it. A redline has no gradient. It is binary, it is tested adversarially, and the action on violation is agreed before launch, when nobody is under shipping pressure. A good one is specific enough to write a test for, tied to a concrete harm, owned by a named person, attached to a pre-agreed consequence, and gated by an adversarial suite written by someone trying to break it. For a clinical assistant: never state a medication dose that is not copied verbatim from the record, and never assert that a patient does not have a condition, because absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. For any retrieval product: no reference that fails to resolve to a real document ever reaches the user. Keep the list short. A long list of redlines is a list of KPIs with dramatic names.

      Going deeper: the test for whether something truly is one. If the answer to “can we trade this for launch date?” is “let’s look at the numbers,” it never was one.

      RAG, end to end

      Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) fits in one sentence. Before the question goes to the model, search a body of text for the passages most likely to contain the answer, paste those passages into the prompt, and tell the model to answer from them.

      Nothing about the model changes. You changed what it can see.

      The idea in one line. RAG does not teach the model anything. It puts the right paragraph in front of the model at the moment the question is asked.

      Candidates blur one thing. RAG is two systems running at different times. Indexing happens offline, ahead of any user. Querying happens online, once per request, in a few hundred milliseconds. They fail differently, they cost differently, and they are owned by different people. Describe RAG as one loop and an interviewer hears someone who has only followed a tutorial.

      In plain words. Phase one builds a library and puts everything on shelves. Phase two is one person walking in with a question.

      Phase 1 — indexing (offline)

      Load the sources: PDFs, help-center articles, Confluence pages, tickets, a codebase. Parse and clean them into plain text. Strip the navigation chrome and the boilerplate footers, run character recognition over scanned pages, decide what to do with tables and images.

      Chunk: cut each document into passages of a few hundred tokens. Embed each chunk, turning it into a vector — the fixed-length list of numbers from chapter 2, arranged so texts about similar things land near each other. Store the vector next to its metadata and, above all, next to the original text, because the text is what you will paste into a prompt later.

      Phase 2 — querying (online)

      Optionally rewrite the query: turn “does it also do that?” into a standalone question using the conversation so far, or spell out an acronym. Embed the query with the same model you used at indexing time. Two different embedding models produce two incompatible coordinate systems, and comparing across them means nothing.

      Search for the nearest vectors. Optionally fetch more than you need and rerank them with a slower, more accurate model. Assemble the prompt from instructions, the surviving chunks, and the question. Generate. Then attach citations by mapping each chunk back to its source pointer.

      Walk one question through it. A support agent asks: “What does error E-4021 mean on the Model X pump?” Offline, 8,400 pages of manuals became roughly 40,000 chunks, each one turned into a 1,536-number vector.

      Online, the query picks up the product name from the conversation, becomes its own 1,536-number vector, and gets compared against all 40,000. The store returns 20 candidates ranked by cosine similarity, the closeness measure from chapter 2 — the top one scoring, say, 0.82, the twentieth 0.61. A reranker reorders those 20 and keeps 4. Those 4 chunks, about 1,600 tokens, go into a prompt telling the model to answer only from them and to cite. Total prompt: around 3,800 tokens. The answer comes back with two footnotes pointing at manual sections.

      Now count the decisions hiding in that paragraph: cleaning rules, chunk size, overlap, embedding model, whether to rewrite the query, how many candidates to fetch, whether to rerank, how many chunks survive, what order they sit in, the wording of the instruction, the generation model, and how citations get rendered. A dozen knobs, each with a measurable effect. RAG is not a feature you switch on.

      The store and the chunks

      In plain words. Checking your question against every stored passage is too slow once there are millions of them. So the store checks a small slice of them and is almost always right.

      A vector database is built for one job: given a query vector, find the nearest vectors among millions. Doing that exactly means comparing against every stored vector, on every request. Too slow. So these systems build an approximate nearest-neighbor index, the ANN box on the diagram above. It visits a small fraction of the data and usually still finds the true neighbors. You give up a little recall, meaning it sometimes misses the actual best match, and you get ten to a hundred times the speed.

      They also store what makes retrieval usable: the original chunk text, metadata to filter on (product, language, version, customer, timestamp), and a pointer back to the source so you can cite it. Filtering matters more than people expect. “Only documents this customer is allowed to see” is a hard requirement, not a ranking preference.

      Common misconception. Vector search is not always the best search. Embeddings are weakest on exactly the strings users type when they are being precise: error codes, SKUs, version numbers, surnames. E-4021 and E-4201 land in almost the same place. Plain keyword search, which matches the literal words, nails them; the standard version of it is called BM25. Hybrid search runs both and fuses the two rankings, and it usually beats either alone. Also: for a few thousand documents you may not need a vector database at all. A brute-force scan of an in-memory array is milliseconds and nothing to operate.

      Chunking is the decision about where to cut documents before embedding them, and it sets your quality ceiling. The reason is mechanical. A chunk is the unit of retrieval, so a chunk is what the embedding has to summarize and what the model gets to read.

      Cut too small and the chunk loses the context that made it mean anything. “Set the value to 3” is useless without knowing which value. Cut too large and one vector has to stand for five topics at once, so it matches all of them mediocrely, and every chunk you retrieve eats budget you could have spent on a better passage.

      Three ways to cut, in rising order of effort. Fixed size with overlap: cut every 400 tokens and repeat 50 tokens across each boundary, so a sentence straddling a cut survives somewhere. Cheap and blind to structure. Structural splitting: cut on the document’s own headings, list items, and code blocks, so a chunk is a section. Usually the best return on effort. Semantic splitting: cut where consecutive sentences stop resembling each other. Elegant, slower, and it can orphan content from its heading.

      Three rules survive contact with real documents. Keep a parent-document pointer on every chunk, so you can match on the small chunk and feed the model the surrounding section when it needs more. Repeat the headings inside the chunk text; a chunk that opens “Model X pump > Error codes >” embeds better and reads better. And keep tables intact, even when that makes a chunk oversized, because half a table is worse than no table.

      When RAG breaks: exactly two failure modes

      This one has a crisp answer, so give it crisply. Either the right information never reached the model, or it reached the model and the model still got it wrong.

      Retrieval failure. The correct passage was not in the assembled context. Three sub-causes, and each needs a different fix. It is not in the corpus at all, which is an ingestion problem. It is in the corpus but not matched, because the doc says “thermal cutout” and the user says “overheat shutdown” — fixed by hybrid search, query rewriting, or better chunking. Or it was matched but ranked below the cutoff, fixed by keeping more candidates or adding a reranker.

      Generation failure. The correct passage was in the context and the answer is still wrong. The model ignored it, contradicted it, blended it with what it already believed, picked the wrong one of two retrieved passages, or hedged into uselessness. The fixes live in the prompt, in the order you put the evidence, in the number of chunks (more is not better, because noise crowds out signal), in the model you chose, and in checking the citations after generation.

      The idea in one line. The two failure modes have completely different fixes, so you have to measure them separately — retrieval recall on one dashboard, answer correctness on another. Report one blended “accuracy” number and you will spend a quarter tuning the wrong half of the system.

      The diagnostic move, and the sentence that wins the interview: before touching the prompt, check whether the correct chunk was in the context. Log the retrieved chunk IDs on every request. When someone reports a bad answer, read the log first. If the chunk was not there, no prompt in the world will save you.

      RAG, fine-tuning, or prompting — and keeping it fresh

      Three ways to change what a model does, and they answer different questions. Prompting changes the instruction. RAG changes the evidence. Fine-tuning changes the model’s own defaults, by training it further on your examples until its weights shift.

      Which lever to pull, and what it costs you
      Lever Use it for Update cost Can cite sources? Main failure
      Prompting Behavior you can describe in words: tone, format, refusals, reasoning steps Seconds — edit text No Long, brittle instructions the model half-ignores
      RAG Knowledge that is external, private, large, or moving Minutes — re-index what changed Yes, natively Retrieval misses, plus a second system to run
      Fine-tuning Form, format, and style the model will not hold reliably; also cutting latency and cost on one narrow task Hours to days, plus a data set and an eval No Stale facts baked into the weights; drift from the base model

      Common misconception. Fine-tuning is a bad way to teach facts. It is expensive to update, it cannot cite, and it does not reliably make a fact retrievable — training on a document teaches style and statistical association far more dependably than it installs a lookup you can trust. If the requirement is “the answer must be correct and attributable,” that is retrieval, not training. Fine-tuning is for how the model answers. RAG is for what it knows.

      These compose, and most real systems use two or three of them: a fine-tuned small model that reliably emits your JSON schema, fed retrieved evidence, wrapped in a carefully worded system prompt. The question is never which one. It is which one for this part of the problem.

      Frequently changing information

      Here RAG wins by construction, and the reason is one sentence: to update knowledge you re-index a document, you do not retrain a model. A price change is a write to a store.

      Freshness has mechanics, though, and naming them is what makes an answer real. Incremental indexing with change detection: hash each source, re-embed only what moved. Deletion and tombstoning: when a document dies, its chunks must actually leave the index, or be marked dead and filtered out at query time. Cache invalidation: a cached answer built on a superseded chunk is a stale answer with a fresh timestamp. Recency-aware ranking: keep timestamps in metadata, let relevance pick the candidates, let recency break the ties. And tell the model today’s date in the system prompt, because it has no clock.

      Two failures people forget. Orphan chunks: the source page was deleted or rewritten months ago and its old chunks are still sitting in the index, perfectly retrievable. Contradictory versions: v2.1 and v3.0 of the same policy both land in the same prompt, with nothing in the text telling the model which one is current. The model will pick one, confidently, and it may pick wrong. The fix is version metadata plus an explicit rule: filter to the current version, or label each chunk with its effective date and instruct the model to prefer the latest.

      Latency, relevance, cost

      Every knob in RAG trades against the other two. Say which way each one pushes and you sound like someone who has run the experiment.

      Levers, and which way each one pushes
      Lever Turn it up and…
      Chunks retrieved (top-k)Recall rises, then relevance falls as noise crowds the prompt; tokens and latency rise with it
      RerankingPrecision rises noticeably; costs one extra model call, tens to a couple hundred milliseconds

      In plain words. Add up one request like a receipt. One line item is large and the rest are rounding errors.

      Now design one. An internal support assistant, p95 under two seconds to first token, 50,000 questions a day. Budget the parts. Query embedding is small and cacheable, call it 10–40 ms. Search over 40,000 chunks, tens of milliseconds. Reranking 20 candidates, a couple hundred milliseconds — or 90 ms if you rerank only 8. Then generation to first token, which dominates everything at hundreds of milliseconds to over a second, and which grows with prompt length. Prompt length is exactly what top-k controls.

      The trade I would make: keep the reranker but run it over 8 candidates instead of 20, use a smaller generation model, cache query embeddings and whole answers for the most common questions, and stream. To know whether I was right I would watch two numbers together: retrieval recall@k on a fixed labeled question set and answer correctness graded on the same set, next to p50 and p95 time-to-first-token and cost per resolved question. If recall@8 with reranking matches recall@20 without it, the cheaper design is strictly better and the argument is over.

      Context engineering: the window is a budget

      Prompt engineering is the craft of wording the instruction: what you ask for, how you phrase it, what examples you show, what format you demand. Context engineering is the craft of deciding what information occupies the finite context window — which items, in what order, in what format, at what token cost, and, when space runs out, what gets thrown overboard.

      The idea in one line. Prompt engineering optimizes the sentence. Context engineering optimizes the allocation. Once products became agentic (multi-turn, many tools, long histories, retrieved documents), the allocation became the harder and more valuable problem.

      In plain words. The window is a suitcase with a fixed size. Five things want in, and where you pack each one decides whether the model notices it.

      The reason is contention. The window is a single shared resource and five claimants want it: the system prompt and policy, tool definitions (which can run to thousands of tokens on their own), conversation history, retrieved documents, and the room you have to leave for the answer. Every token spent on a low-value item is a token the passage that actually answers the question does not get.

      Position is a resource too. From chapter 2: attention is not uniform across position. Models attend best to the beginning and the end of a long context and measurably worst to the middle, the “lost in the middle” effect.

      So order deliberately. Stable instructions early, where they are also the part that caches best across requests, which saves real money. Low-value material in the middle, where degradation costs you least: compacted history, background. The strongest retrieved evidence and the user’s actual question at the end, closest to where generation begins. If you retrieved four chunks, the top-ranked one goes last, not first.

      The discipline has two halves. A token budget per section, written down: this much for tools, this much for evidence, this much reserved for output. And an eviction policy decided in advance: when the request would overflow, you already know that history gets recompacted first, then summarized, then the lowest-ranked chunk goes, then unused tool definitions. Decide it, or your framework decides for you by silently truncating the oldest tokens, and you will discover the policy in production.

      When RAG is overkill

      Retrieval is not free. It is a second system to own: an ingestion pipeline, an index, a freshness guarantee, monitoring, and a whole extra failure mode.

      Skip it when the corpus fits comfortably in the window; if the knowledge is twenty pages, paste the twenty pages and cache the prefix. Skip it when the task needs no outside knowledge, like rewriting, translating, or classifying text the user already supplied. Skip it when the source of truth is a structured database, where generated SQL or a plain API call is more precise than fuzzy passage matching. Skip it when an order status belongs to a function call rather than a similarity search. And skip it when the latency budget cannot absorb a retrieval hop at all.

      Common misconception. “Add RAG” is not a default. A cheaper answer usually exists for knowledge that is narrow, structured, or already in hand. And the hidden cost of RAG is not the vector database licence. It is the permanent obligation to keep an index true.

      “What is RAG and when would you use it?”

      RAG means searching an external corpus at question time and putting the best passages into the prompt, so the model answers from evidence it can cite instead of from memory.

      The model is unchanged. You changed what it can see. Use it whenever the knowledge needed sits outside the model: private company data, documents that change, a corpus far too large for the window, or anything where the answer must be traceable to a source. It is the standard fix for the three “the model does not know” problems: knowledge cutoff, private data, and made-up facts. It also buys access control and an audit trail, because retrieval respects metadata filters and produces citations, which a fine-tuned model cannot.

      Going deeper: naming what RAG does not fix. It does not improve reasoning, tone, or output format, and it hands you a whole retrieval subsystem you now have to keep fresh and monitor.

      “Walk through a RAG pipeline end to end.”

      Two phases. Offline indexing: load, parse, chunk, embed, store. Online querying: rewrite, embed, search, rerank, assemble, generate, cite.

      Offline you load the sources, clean them into text, cut them into chunks of a few hundred tokens, embed each chunk, and store the vector next to its metadata and its original text. Online you optionally rewrite the query into a standalone question, embed it with the same model, run approximate nearest-neighbor search, optionally rerank the candidates with a slower and more accurate model, assemble a prompt from instructions plus the surviving chunks plus the question, generate, and map each chunk back to a source pointer for citations. Concretely: 8,400 pages become 40,000 chunks, the store returns 20 candidates, a reranker keeps 4, and the prompt lands around 3,800 tokens.

      Going deeper: insisting on the two-phase split and naming what is tunable at each step. There are roughly a dozen decisions in that walk: chunk size, overlap, embedding model, k, rerank or not, chunk order. Saying so signals you have actually tuned one.

      “RAG vs fine-tuning vs prompt: when to pick each?”

      Prompting for behavior you can describe, RAG for knowledge that is external or changing, fine-tuning for form and style the model will not hold reliably — plus latency and cost reduction on a narrow task.

      Prompting takes seconds to change and should always be tried first. RAG is the answer whenever correctness depends on facts you own or facts that move, because updating means re-indexing a document rather than retraining a model, and because it can cite. Fine-tuning earns its keep when you need a consistent output shape or house style across thousands of calls, or when you want a small cheap model to match a large one on one narrow task. They compose, and most production systems use two of the three.

      Going deeper: explicitly killing the common error — fine-tuning is a bad way to teach facts. It is expensive to update, it cannot cite, and training on documents does not reliably make their contents retrievable.

      “What is a vector database and why do you need one?”

      A store built for nearest-neighbor search over vectors, using an approximate index that trades a little recall for ten to a hundred times the speed.

      Exact search means comparing your query vector against every stored vector, on every request. Too slow at millions. An approximate nearest-neighbor index visits a small fraction of the data and usually still returns the true top matches. These stores also hold what makes retrieval usable: the original chunk text, metadata for filtering by customer, product, version, or language, and a source pointer for citations. The index settings are your explicit recall-versus-speed dial.

      Going deeper: two honest caveats. For a few thousand documents you may not need one at all — a brute-force scan of an in-memory array is milliseconds. And hybrid search, dense vectors fused with BM25 keyword scoring, usually beats pure vector search, because exact strings like error codes and product names are precisely where embeddings are weakest.

      “What is chunking and why does it matter?”

      Chunking is deciding where to cut documents before embedding, and it sets your quality ceiling, because the chunk is the unit of both retrieval and reading.

      The tension is direct. Chunks that are too small lose the context that made them mean anything — “set the value to 3” without knowing which value. Chunks that are too large force one vector to stand for several topics, so it matches all of them mediocrely, and each retrieved chunk burns budget you could have spent on a better passage. Strategies, in rising order of effort: fixed size with overlap; structural splitting on headings, lists, or code blocks; and semantic splitting where consecutive sentences stop resembling each other. In practice, keep a parent-document pointer so you can retrieve small and read large, repeat the heading path inside the chunk text, and keep tables intact.

      Going deeper: saying plainly that most RAG quality is won or lost here, before any prompt tuning, and that you would test two chunking policies against a labeled question set rather than argue about token counts.

      “How do you handle frequently changing information?”

      RAG, because updating knowledge is re-indexing a document rather than retraining a model — then the freshness mechanics around it.

      Incremental indexing with change detection: hash each source, re-embed only what moved. Deletion and tombstoning: when a source dies, its chunks must actually leave the index or be marked dead and filtered out at query time. Cache invalidation: a cached answer built on a superseded chunk is stale with a fresh timestamp. Recency-aware ranking: relevance picks the candidates, recency breaks the ties. And put the current date in the system prompt, because the model has no clock.

      Going deeper: the two failures people forget. Orphan chunks: the page was deleted months ago and its chunks are still perfectly retrievable. And contradictory versions: v2.1 and v3.0 of the same policy both retrieved into one prompt with nothing telling the model which is current. Fix with version metadata plus either a hard filter or effective-date labels and an explicit preference rule.

      “What are the two failure modes of RAG?”

      Retrieval failure: the right information never reached the model. Generation failure: it did reach the model and the answer is still wrong.

      Retrieval failure has three sub-causes with different fixes: the passage is not in the corpus (ingestion), it is in the corpus but the query did not match it (vocabulary mismatch, fixed by hybrid search, query rewriting, or better chunking), or it matched but ranked below the cutoff (keep more candidates, add a reranker). Generation failure means the model ignored the evidence, contradicted it, blended it with what it already believed, picked the wrong one of two passages, or hedged. Fixes live in the prompt, the order of the evidence, the number of chunks, the model choice, and post-generation citation checks.

      Going deeper: why the framing matters. The two halves have completely different fixes, so you must measure them separately — retrieval recall on one dashboard, answer correctness on another. And the diagnostic move: before touching the prompt, check the logs for whether the correct chunk was in the assembled context.

      “How do you balance latency, relevance, and cost in RAG?”

      Set a budget for each, then move the specific knobs that trade between them: top-k, reranking, model sizes, index settings, caching, hybrid search, streaming. And measure retrieval recall separately from answer quality.

      Directions of effect: raising top-k lifts recall but adds tokens, latency, and noise that can reduce relevance; reranking buys precision for tens to a couple hundred milliseconds; larger generation models buy synthesis for multiples of cost and latency; the search index settings are an explicit recall-versus-speed dial; hybrid search is a cheap accuracy win on exact terms; caching cuts both cost and latency at the price of invalidation risk; and streaming improves perceived latency without changing the total work.

      Going deeper: knowing where the time actually is. Embedding and search are tens of milliseconds; generation is hundreds to thousands. So the real latency levers are the ones that shorten the prompt or shrink the generator, not the ones that shave milliseconds off search.

      “What is context engineering vs prompt engineering?”

      Prompt engineering is wording the instruction. Context engineering is deciding what information occupies the finite window, in what order, in what format, at what token cost — and what gets evicted when it overflows.

      The window is a single scarce resource contended for by five claimants: the system prompt, tool definitions, conversation history, retrieved documents, and the space you must reserve for output. Every token spent on a low-value item is a token unavailable to the passage that answers the question. Position matters too, because attention is not uniform — models attend best to the start and end of a long context and worst to the middle. So context engineering is allocation and ordering, backed by a written budget per section and an eviction policy chosen in advance.

      Going deeper: explaining why the discipline shifted. Single-turn chat needed good wording. Agentic products, with many tools and long histories and multi-step loops, made allocation the binding constraint.

      “What goes into the context window and in what priority?”

      Stable instructions first, tool definitions next, compacted history in the middle, then the strongest retrieved evidence and the user’s actual question last, with output space reserved from the start.

      The ordering follows from the lost-in-the-middle effect: put what must be obeyed at the beginning, where attention is strong and where prefix caching saves money, and put what must be used at the end, immediately before generation begins. If you retrieved four chunks, place the top-ranked one closest to the question, not first. Park the least load-bearing material, background and summarized history, in the middle, where degradation costs least. Then write the budget down: of 10,000 tokens, 800 for instructions, 1,200 for tools, 800 for compacted history, 4,000 for evidence, 1,200 for recent turns, 200 for the question, 1,800 reserved for output.

      Going deeper: a pre-decided eviction order — recompact history, then summarize recent turns, then drop the lowest-ranked chunk, then prune unused tool definitions, never the instructions or the question. If you do not decide it, your framework silently truncates for you and you learn the policy in production.

      “When is RAG overkill?”

      When the knowledge fits in the window, needs no external source, lives in a structured database, has a deterministic lookup, or when the latency budget cannot absorb a retrieval hop.

      If the corpus is twenty pages, paste the twenty pages and cache the prompt prefix: simpler, more accurate, no index to keep true. If the task is transforming text the user already supplied, like summarizing or translating, there is nothing to retrieve. If the source of truth is a relational database, generated SQL or a plain API call is more precise than fuzzy passage matching, and it can aggregate, which retrieval cannot. If the question is “where is my order,” that is a function call with an exact answer. And if you have a hard sub-second budget, a retrieval hop plus a longer prompt may not fit.

      Going deeper: naming the cost people miss. RAG adds an entire retrieval system you must own, monitor, and keep fresh, plus a second failure mode to debug. That ongoing obligation, not the infrastructure bill, is the real price.

      “Perplexity: Explain how RAG works.”

      Retrieve first, then generate, and cite: at question time you search a corpus, select the passages most likely to contain the answer, and condition generation on them so every claim traces to a source.

      Give the two phases. Offline: load and clean the sources, chunk them, embed each chunk, and store the vector with metadata and the original text. Online: rewrite the query if the conversation makes it ambiguous, embed it with the same model, search, rerank, assemble a prompt of instructions plus the surviving chunks plus the question, generate, then map each chunk back to its source. For a search product specifically, add the parts that matter at web scale: hybrid retrieval so exact names and codes are not lost, freshness ranking so today’s page beats last year’s, and a grounding instruction that forbids claims the retrieved text does not support.

      Going deeper: treating citations as an architectural requirement rather than a garnish. Citations are only possible because you kept the original text and source pointer next to the vector — and they are the thing that makes retrieval failure and generation failure separately debuggable.

      “Nvidia: Design a RAG system with latency, relevance, and cost trade-offs.”

      I would fix the budget first: p95 under two seconds to first token at 50,000 questions a day. Then assemble the pipeline from parts whose latency I can add up, and buy relevance only where it is cheap.

      Baseline: embed the query (10–40 ms, cacheable), hybrid retrieval over the index (tens of milliseconds), rerank the top candidates (a couple hundred milliseconds for 20, roughly 90 for 8), then generate, which dominates at hundreds of milliseconds to over a second and grows with prompt length. My trade: rerank only 8 candidates, use a smaller generation model, cache query embeddings and whole answers for the most common questions, tune the index toward speed since a reranker recovers precision, and stream so perceived latency is the time to first token, not to completion. That takes an illustrative 2.65 seconds total down to about 1.5, and time-to-first-token from roughly 1.25 to 0.55 seconds.

      Going deeper: saying what you would measure to know you were right. Retrieval recall@k on a fixed labeled set and answer correctness on the same set, plotted against p50 and p95 time-to-first-token and cost per resolved question. If recall@8 with reranking equals recall@20 without it, the cheaper design wins on evidence rather than on taste.

      A tool call is text the model asks you to run

      One fact holds up this entire chapter, so here it is first: the model never executes anything. It cannot open a network connection, run a database query, or send an email. It predicts tokens. That is the whole job. When you hear that an AI feature “called a tool,” what physically happened is that the model produced some structured text naming a function and its arguments, and your code read that text and did the work.

      Here is the whole round trip. The interview answer is this list, in order.

      1. You send the request. Your application posts the prompt plus a list of tool definitions. Each definition is a name, a description, and a typed schema for the arguments.
      2. The model asks; it does not act. Instead of prose it predicts a block of structured output: a tool name and a JSON object of arguments. Then it stops. Nothing has happened yet, anywhere.
      3. Your code parses and checks it. Does that tool exist? Do the arguments type-check? Is this user allowed to do this, for this amount? Your code decides whether to run it at all.
      4. Your code executes. It calls the function, hits the API, queries the database. With your credentials, your limits, your audit log.
      5. Your code sends the result back into the conversation as a new message, tagged as the result of that specific call.
      6. The model continues with that result sitting in its context, and either answers the user or asks for another tool. Loop as many times as needed.

      The idea in one line. The model is choosing, not doing. It asks; your application is the thing with hands.

      That decides where half of your engineering work lives. Permissions, authentication, spend caps, retries, idempotency (not charging the card twice on a replay), confirmation before irreversible actions, the audit trail: all of it sits in your code. A model that “decided” to refund a customer $10,000 refunded nothing. An application that ran the request without checking did.

      Common misconception. “We gave the model access to our database.” You did not. You gave it a text description of a function. You gave your own service the ability to run that function when the model asks. If your service runs whatever it is asked without checking, that is a bug in your service.

      The tool definition is a prompt, not documentation

      A tool definition has three parts. A name, which should read like a function name. A description in plain language. And a parameter schema, almost always JSON Schema, which is just a JSON description of allowed shapes: field names, types, required fields, enumerated options (“one of: standard, express, overnight”), ranges, defaults.

      In plain words. The model has never seen your code. All it knows about your tool is the short label you wrote for it, which makes the label part of the product.

      That definition is the only thing the model knows about your tool. It cannot read your implementation, your comments, or your database schema. Picking a tool and filling in its arguments is next-token prediction over those descriptions and nothing else. So a tool definition is not documentation. It is a prompt, and it behaves like one.

      Which is why a vague description is a ticket-worthy bug and not a style problem. Three things go wrong. The model picks the wrong tool, because two descriptions overlap and it has no basis to prefer one. It produces malformed or invented arguments: a date in the wrong format, a currency you do not support, a plausible-looking customer ID it made up, because the schema never said. Or the tool is never called at all, because the description does not use the words a real user would use.

      All three fail probabilistically. The tool works when you try it by hand, then misfires on some slice of production traffic, and which slice depends on how people happened to phrase their messages.

      So treat descriptions like code. They belong in version control, in code review, and in your eval suite, the automated test cases you re-run on every change (Chapter 8). Editing one word of a description changes model behavior the way editing a prompt does: silently, for every user at once. Two habits help. Keep the tool list short, because selection gets worse as the menu grows. And when two tools overlap, merge them or write the difference into both descriptions.

      Prompt, tool, skill — three shapes of the same substance

      These three words get used as if they named rival technologies. They do not. A prompt is instructions and content placed in the context window. A tool is a capability with a name and a schema that the model can request and your code executes. A skill is a packaged bundle of instructions, plus optional reference files and scripts, that gets loaded into context when it looks relevant to the task, so you are not carrying every procedure in every request.

      The idea in one line. All three are context and configuration. None of them touch the weights.

      So do skills retrain the model? No. Nothing about the model changes. The weights were frozen when training ended (Chapter 1), and they are identical for you, for the next customer, and for the request you send a second later. What “loads at runtime” is text. Your application, or the harness around it, picks the relevant skill content and tool definitions and puts them in the context window for that one request. When the request ends, they are gone. If a skill ships with a script, the script is run by the runtime, not by the model. Same split as before.

      That runs straight into the context budget from Chapter 2. A rich tool definition might run a few hundred tokens; a detailed skill can run thousands. Those numbers are illustrative, but the shape holds. Every tool and skill you add spends window you no longer have for evidence, history, or the user’s document. “Just add another tool” is never free.

      Chatbot, chain, agent: the difference is who controls the control flow

      Strip the marketing away and the question is about control flow: the order in which steps run, and who decides that order.

      In a chatbot the path is fixed and the human drives. A turn goes in, a turn comes out, and the human decides whether there is a next one. In a prompt chain the developer fixed the path in advance: step one summarizes, step two extracts fields, step three drafts a reply, always in that order.

      In an agent the model decides the next step. You give it a goal and a set of tools. It plans, acts, sees what came back, and loops until a stop condition is met: goal satisfied, step budget spent, or a human steps in. The model is inside the control flow, not just inside one step of it.

      The loop buys flexibility on tasks you cannot script. It costs four things. Step count has no ceiling, so latency and spend have none either, unless you set one. Errors compound across turns, because turn five reasons over whatever turn two returned. Evaluation gets much harder: there is no single correct output, only a path that may reach the goal by a route you did not expect. And the blast radius is real. An agent with a payments tool and a bad loop can spend money over and over.

      The honest test for whether you need an agent. If you can enumerate the steps ahead of time, write the steps. A fixed chain is cheaper, faster, debuggable, and testable. Reach for an agent when the number and order of steps genuinely depend on what you find along the way.

      Prototyping a prompt chain for a multi-step task

      There is a method here, and it is not “write a long prompt and hope.” Break the task into steps and write an explicit input and output contract for each one: this step receives these fields, it must return these fields. Then do the whole thing by hand, once, start to finish. If you cannot do it manually in a chat window, your decomposition is wrong and no amount of engineering will rescue it.

      Then make each step’s output structured, JSON against a schema, and validate it in code before passing it on. A broken step should fail loudly at step two instead of quietly at step five. Test each step on its own with fixed inputs so you know its individual reliability. Only then wire them together.

      Error compounding, with the arithmetic

      In plain words. Every step in a chain is a coin that lands heads most of the time. A five-step chain needs five heads in a row.

      This is the most useful piece of arithmetic a PM can carry about chains and agents. Reliability multiplies. A five-step chain where every step is 95% correct ends up at 0.955 ≈ 77% end to end. At 90% per step it is 59%. Even a stellar 99% per step, run for ten steps, lands at about 90%, which means one run in ten is wrong somewhere. Nobody sees this in a demo of three happy-path runs.

      What you do about it: shorten the chain, validate at every boundary so errors get caught early, retry the failed step rather than the whole run, checkpoint state after expensive steps, and put a human in front of anything irreversible. Cheap and reversible steps can run unattended. Sending the email, issuing the refund, merging the code: those get a confirmation.

      Memory is four different things

      “Give the agent memory” is not one request. The model is stateless: it remembers nothing between calls. Every kind of memory is something you build outside the model and paste back into the context window on the next request.

      In plain words. A user says “book me the usual.” Nothing about “the usual” lives inside the model, so it has to live in something you built, and you have to pick which thing.

      Here is that before and after, with one traveler. Before: she types “book me the usual” and the agent asks “the usual what?” It woke up blank, because blank is the only state a model has.

      After: three stores outside the model each hand over a piece. Her account row says business plan, aisle seat required, because someone typed that into a form. A saved note says she likes the 7am departure, because she mentioned it twice and you extracted it. The transcript from ten minutes ago says the city is Lisbon. The window for this one call holds all three plus the flight list you just retrieved, and the agent books the 7am to Lisbon on the aisle.

      Four pieces, four mechanisms, four different ways to fail.

      Four kinds of agent memory, what each is good for, and where each fails
      Kind What it physically is Good for Fails at
      In-context / working The context window of this one call Everything in the current task; free and exact Finite, and gone the moment the call ends
      Session The running transcript, stored and resent each turn Continuity inside one conversation Grows without bound; needs compaction or summary, which loses detail
      Long-term semantic Facts pulled out and stored externally, then fetched by relevance: RAG over the user’s own history Preferences and color: “likes the 7am flight” Retrieval misses; stale or contradictory facts; never authoritative
      Structured state A real database row or profile record Anything that must be right: plan tier, balance, address, consent You must design the schema, and it only holds what you modeled

      Then come the parts nobody mentions in the pitch. What is worth remembering? Save everything and retrieval turns noisy and expensive. What happens on contradiction? She said vegetarian in March and ordered steak in June, so you need a recency rule or a confirmation, not two facts sitting side by side. Forgetting and correcting have to be built, not bolted on later. Privacy and deletion are obligations: a stored memory is personal data, so it needs consent, visibility, and a delete path that works.

      Default architecture. Structured state for anything that has to be correct. Semantic memory for preferences and color. Session transcript for the current conversation, with summarization when it outgrows the budget. Never let a fuzzy retrieved “memory” be the source of truth for a fact your database already knows.

      MCP: turning N × M integrations into N + M

      Start with the problem, because MCP answers a specific, boring, expensive one. Before it, every AI application wrote its own integration code for every tool and data source it wanted to reach. Your app needed custom glue for GitHub, for Postgres, for the internal ticketing system. The team down the hall wrote all of it again, differently. With N applications and M tools you get N × M integrations, and not one of them travels.

      In plain words. A server is a wrapper somebody writes once around a system. A client is the app that plugs into it, and the protocol is the agreed shape of the plug.

      The Model Context Protocol is an open protocol that standardizes how a system exposes tools (functions the model can request), resources (data it can read), and prompts (reusable templates) to a model. One side is a server, which wraps a system and advertises what it offers in the standard shape. The other side is a client, meaning the AI application, which discovers those offerings at runtime and turns them into tool definitions for the model. Write the server once and any compliant client can use it. N × M becomes N + M.

      The analogy belongs here, after the mechanism. MCP is USB-C for tools. The value is not the connector. It is that nobody has to ship a new cable for every pair of devices.

      MCP server versus a one-off API integration
      Dimension MCP server One-off integration
      ReuseWrite it once, any compliant client can use itTied to your app; rewritten elsewhere
      EcosystemServers already exist for common systems; swap clients or models freelyYou build and maintain every connector
      ControlBounded by the protocol’s shape and versioningTotal control over payloads, batching, caching
      DependenciesA protocol layer and a server process to run and secureNothing beyond your own code and the vendor SDK
      TuningGeneric interface, generic performanceTuned for your exact query pattern
      Best whenMany tools, many surfaces, or you want the ecosystemOne integration you will never reuse: genuinely simpler

      What MCP does not solve. It standardizes the plumbing, not the thinking. Your tool descriptions still have to be good, because the model still chooses on text alone. Authentication, permissions, and scoping are still yours to design, and a server with broad access to a system is a broad attack surface. A standard protocol does not make an agent reliable either. The compounding math in Fig 5.4 does not care how the tool was wired up.

      One last thing, because it gets asked directly. Interviewers want to know whether you have built agentic systems. With no job history behind you, say so plainly, then talk about something you actually made: a small tool-using loop, including where it broke and what you changed. Naming your own failure modes is the signal. Chapter 9 covers that project in full.

      Questions this chapter answers

      Answer each one out loud before you open it. The gap between what you said and what is written below is the part you have not learned yet. That gap is the whole point of this section.

      “An AI feature calls a tool. What actually happens?”

      The model emits structured text naming a tool and its arguments, then stops. My code parses it, validates it, decides whether to run it, runs it, and sends the result back into the model as a new message.

      Said in full: I send the prompt plus a list of tool definitions. The model predicts a tool name and a JSON argument object instead of prose, and stops. Nothing has happened in the world yet. My application parses it, type-checks the arguments against the schema, applies its own authorization and limits, then executes. I put the result back into the conversation tagged as the result of that call, and the model continues, either answering or asking for another tool, until no tool is requested or my step cap is hit.

      Going deeper: naming what lives in your code because of this: authorization, spend caps, idempotency, retries, the audit log, and a confirmation step before anything irreversible.

      “Does the model execute the function? Who does?”

      No. The model only emits a request. My application layer executes it.

      The model is a text predictor with no ability to run code, open connections, or touch a database. What it produces is a name and a JSON object. The execution is done by the application, the agent framework, or the MCP server: code you own and deploy, running with your credentials. This is not a pedantic distinction. It is where the whole safety story lives. Because your code is the executor, your code is what checks whether this user may take this action, whether the amount is in range, and whether a human should confirm first. A model cannot exceed its permissions, because it has none. Your executor has them.

      Going deeper: the one-liner, the model chooses and your app acts, plus the observation that “the model did something dangerous” always means an application executed a request without a check.

      “Why is a vague tool description a real bug?”

      Because tool selection is next-token prediction conditioned on those descriptions. A vague description makes the model pick the wrong tool, invent arguments, or skip the tool entirely — and it fails only some of the time.

      The description and schema are the only information the model has about the tool; it cannot read the implementation. So a vague or overlapping description hurts three things at once: which tool gets chosen, whether the arguments are well-formed, and whether the tool fires at all when a user phrases the request unusually. The nasty part is that these failures are probabilistic. My manual test passes, the demo passes, and then some percentage of real traffic misroutes depending on wording. That is why descriptions belong in version control and in an eval suite. Changing one is a behavior change deployed to every user, exactly like changing a prompt.

      Going deeper: saying what a good description contains: what it does, when to use it, when explicitly not to, argument semantics and units, and how it differs from the tool next to it.

      “What’s in a tool definition (name, description, schema)?”

      A name, a plain-language description of what it does and when to use it, and a typed parameter schema, usually JSON Schema, with required fields, enums, and constraints.

      The name should read like a function name and hint at scope: search_order_history, not search. The description carries the routing logic: purpose, trigger conditions, explicit non-triggers, and how it differs from neighboring tools. The schema constrains what the model can even produce, down to enums for closed sets, ranges and defaults for numbers, and format notes such as ISO-8601 for dates. Treat it as your first line of validation, not your only one; your code still validates on receipt. And the definition is the entire interface: if a rule is not in the name, description, or schema, the model does not know it exists.

      Going deeper: pointing out that the definition costs tokens on every request, so tool count and description length are a budget decision.

      “What makes something an agent vs chatbot?”

      Control flow. A chatbot’s path is fixed and the human drives it. An agent decides its own next step, uses tools, observes results, and loops until a stop condition is met.

      A chatbot is turn in, turn out; the human decides whether there is a next step. A prompt chain is multi-step but the developer fixed the order in advance. An agent is given a goal and tools and runs a loop (plan, act, observe, repeat) where the model chooses which tool to call next based on what came back. That means the model is inside the control flow. The costs all come out of the loop: unbounded steps, so unbounded latency and spend; errors compounding across turns; evaluation over whole paths instead of single outputs; and real blast radius when the tools can move money.

      Going deeper: the honest test. If you can enumerate the steps ahead of time, write the steps. A fixed chain is cheaper, faster, and testable, and most “agents” in production should have been chains.

      “What’s the difference between a prompt, a tool, and a skill?”

      A prompt is instructions in context. A tool is a capability with a schema that the model can request and my code executes. A skill is a packaged bundle of instructions and resources loaded into context when it is relevant.

      A prompt shapes behavior with words. A tool extends reach: it lets the model ask for something to happen outside itself, and the execution belongs to the application. A skill is about packaging and selective loading: instructions plus optional files and scripts, pulled in for the tasks where they apply. What matters is what they share. All three are context and configuration. None of them modify the weights. That is also why they draw on one budget, the context window, and why adding a skill or a tool always costs tokens.

      Going deeper: noting that a skill may contain scripts, and that those scripts are run by the runtime, not by the model. The same choose-versus-do split as tool calling.

      “Do skills retrain the model? What loads at runtime?”

      No, they do not retrain anything. Nothing about the model changes. At runtime, text is selected and inserted into the context window for that one request.

      The weights were frozen when training ended and are identical across users and requests. What a skill changes is the input, not the network. When the harness judges a skill relevant, it loads that skill’s instructions, and possibly references to files or scripts, into the context window for that call. When the call ends, it is gone; the model retains nothing. Same for tool definitions and retrieved memory. The consequence is budget: every skill, tool definition, and remembered fact occupies window you no longer have for evidence or the user’s own content, so sprawl shows up as worse retrieval and higher cost per call.

      Going deeper: connecting it to the only things that do change weights, pretraining and fine-tuning, and saying that if someone needs behavior baked in permanently, prompts, skills, and tools are the wrong lever.

      “How would you give an agent memory?”

      I would split “memory” into four mechanisms (working context, session transcript, long-term semantic memory, and structured state) and use structured state for anything that must be correct.

      In-context working memory is the window itself: free, exact, finite, and gone at the end of the call. Session memory is the running transcript, stored and resent each turn, eventually summarized or compacted when it outgrows the budget. Long-term semantic memory means extracting facts, storing them externally, and retrieving them by relevance: RAG over the user’s own history, good for preferences and color, never authoritative. Structured state is a database row: the plan tier, the balance, the shipping address. The model is stateless, so all of this is something I build outside it and re-inject as text.

      Going deeper: the hard parts, meaning what is worth storing, resolving contradictions with recency rules or confirmation, making forgetting and correction first-class, honoring deletion and consent obligations, and accepting that retrieved memory competes for the same window as retrieved evidence.

      “How would you prototype a prompt chain for a multi-step task?”

      Decompose the task into steps with an explicit input and output contract each, do it by hand once to prove the decomposition, make every step’s output structured and validated, then test steps in isolation before wiring them together.

      The manual pass matters most: if I cannot complete the task myself in a chat window, the decomposition is wrong and engineering will not save it. Then each step gets a schema, so a bad output fails at step two rather than surfacing as nonsense at step five. I measure each step’s reliability alone, because end-to-end reliability is the product of them: five steps at 95% is about 77%, ten steps at 99% is about 90%. Shortening the chain usually beats improving a single step. Around that go per-step retries rather than whole-run retries, checkpoints after expensive steps, and a human approval before anything irreversible.

      Going deeper: doing the compounding arithmetic out loud, and saying where the human belongs: unattended for cheap and reversible steps, confirmation for anything that sends, pays, or deletes.

      “How does MCP compare to building one-off API integrations?”

      MCP trades control for reuse: write a server once and any compliant client can use it. For a single integration you will never reuse, a one-off is simpler.

      MCP wins on reuse, ecosystem, and swappability: existing servers for common systems, and freedom to change client or model without rewriting connectors. One-off integrations win on control and minimal dependencies: you own the payload shape, the batching, and the caching, with nothing between you and the vendor SDK. The deciding question is how many tools and how many surfaces. One app, one tool, one hot path: write the integration. Several surfaces or a growing tool list: write the server. Either way, the tool descriptions and the permission model are still work you have to do well.

      Going deeper: refusing the framing that one is simply better, and naming the security angle: a broadly scoped server is a broadly scoped attack surface, and auth design does not come free with the protocol.

      “How do tool/function calling work under the hood?”

      Tool definitions go into the context as text; the model predicts a structured block containing a tool name and JSON arguments instead of prose, and stops; the runtime executes it and feeds the result back as a new message.

      Mechanically there is no new capability inside the network. The definitions are serialized into the model’s input. The model has been trained to produce a specific structured format when a tool fits, and the API surfaces it as a separate field with a distinct stop reason so your code can branch on it cleanly. Some providers constrain decoding against the schema so the JSON is well-formed by construction. Even then, semantic correctness is not guaranteed: a valid date in the wrong timezone is still valid JSON. The model may request several tools at once, and results come back tagged with the call identifier so the model can match result to request across a multi-step loop.

      Going deeper: the reminder that it is still token prediction, so tool choice and arguments are shaped by the descriptions and the conversation, and that schema validity is not the same thing as being right.

      “What is MCP and why does it exist?”

      MCP is an open protocol for exposing tools, resources, and prompts to models in a standard shape. It exists because every application was writing bespoke integration code for every tool — N × M integrations, none reusable.

      A server wraps a system (a database, a repository, a file store) and advertises the tools, readable resources, and prompt templates it offers. A client, meaning the AI application, discovers them at runtime and presents them to the model as tool definitions. Because the interface is standard, the server is written once and reused by any compliant client, which turns N × M into N + M. USB-C for tools is a fair analogy once the mechanism is clear: nobody ships a new cable per pair of devices.

      Going deeper: stating what it does not solve: description quality, permissions and auth, and agent reliability are all still yours, and a standard protocol makes none of them automatic.

      The four layers, and which two of them you own

      One model with tools in a loop is a single unit of work. Real products coordinate many units: several calls, several models, steps that run for minutes and have to survive a crash. That coordination lives somewhere. Here is where.

      In plain words. Four layers sit between a chip and a customer. You rent the bottom two and you build the top two.

      Layer 1, infrastructure. Compute. GPUs and similar accelerators, the data centers holding them, and the serving software that turns trained weights into a fast endpoint. Running a trained model to get an output is inference; producing the weights is training. Clouds and chipmakers own it, because the bill is capital expenditure at the scale of billions. It moves fast, but chip fabrication and power set the pace, not software.

      Layer 2, model. The weights. Foundation models, meaning large general-purpose models trained on broad data, plus fine-tunes and smaller specialists such as embedding models that turn text into vectors. The frontier labs own this. You rent it by the token, and it gets cheaper per unit of capability every month.

      Layer 3, orchestration. Everything that decides what to send a model, in what order, with what context, and what to do with the answer. Prompts, chains, tool calling, retrieval, routing, memory, caching, guardrails, the evaluation harness. This is where PMs spend their days, and it changes as fast as you decide, because you own it.

      Layer 4, application. The part a human touches. The interface, the workflow it slots into, the defaults, how errors surface, and the feedback loop that turns usage into data you can evaluate on. Yours entirely, and worth keeping for years.

      The idea in one line. You rent layers one and two and you build layers three and four, so your defensibility has to live in the parts you build.

      Compute and weights are converging on utilities. Many suppliers, falling prices, near-identical interfaces, switching cost measured in days. What does not commoditize is knowledge of your users’ workflow, the evaluation set that encodes what “good” means in your domain, the retrieval corpus, the integrations, the feedback loop, and taste in the interface. That is also the answer to the Chapter 8 question. Models improve monthly, so what stays constant? These two layers do.

      Other people slice the stack differently, and none of those slicings are wrong. An interviewer is not checking whether your diagram matches theirs. They are checking that every component has exactly one home in your framing, and that you can say what follows from it.

      Chains, graphs, and the plain API call

      Layer three is yours. What do you build it out of? Three honest answers.

      What LangChain is and what it buys you

      LangChain is a library of abstractions for building applications on language models. Prompt templates, which are prompts with typed holes in them. Chains, which pipe one step’s output into the next step’s input. Retrievers, one interface over vector stores and search back ends. Tool wrappers, memory objects, output parsers, and a large catalog of integrations. Run a chain and it fills your template, calls the API, parses the response, and passes it on.

      What you get is speed. A retrieval-augmented question-answering demo takes a dozen lines instead of a day, and swapping a vector database or a provider becomes a config change.

      What you pay is indirection. Your prompt is assembled inside the library, so the string you wrote is not the string that was sent, and your token spend is not what you estimated. When quality drops you debug through a layer you did not write. And a team can become expert in a framework rather than in the system. Those are not the same asset.

      So use it to explore, and to glue many integrations together. Be deliberate before it sits in a hot production path: the code serving live traffic, where latency, cost, and debuggability all bite.

      Chain shape versus graph shape

      The difference between LangChain and LangGraph has nothing to do with connector counts. It is the shape of computation each one can express.

      A chain is a line. Step one, then step two, then step three. That fits when you know the path before you run anything.

      A graph is nodes and edges, some of those edges conditional, over a state object every node can read and write. Drawing it that way buys what a line cannot do cleanly: loop back and retry a bounded number of times, branch on a value, pause and continue after a deploy, wait for a human to approve.

      The idea in one line. Use a chain when the path is known ahead of time. Use a graph when the path depends on what happens at run time, which is exactly the agent condition from Chapter 5.

      When a raw SDK call beats any framework

      A raw SDK call means using the provider’s own client library directly. You build the prompt, send it, and validate the response. Choose that when the task is a single call or a short fixed sequence, when you need exact control over prompt text and token budget, and when debuggability and tight latency matter more than development speed.

      Here is the point most candidates are afraid to make. A great many production language-model features are a well-tested prompt, a validated output schema, and one API call in a retry wrapper. Choosing that on purpose is a senior signal. Reaching for the framework you have heard of is the naive move.

      What every abstraction layer trades

      Every abstraction is the same bargain. You gain time: less code, faster onboarding, a paved road for the common case. You give up transparency, because when it fails it fails in a layer you do not own and the failure arrives translated.

      The danger specific to this domain is narrower. Abstractions hide the prompt, and the prompt is the product. If you cannot print the exact bytes you sent, you cannot reason about quality or cost.

      The test to apply. Does this abstraction hide something I will need to debug at 3am? If yes, keep it out of the hot path, or make it log the exact request it sent. An abstraction you can see through is fine. An opaque one in the critical path is a future outage with your name on it.

      When orchestration earns its place

      Orchestration machinery is not free. It adds latency, moving parts, failure modes, and a mental model every new engineer has to load before they can help. Something should trigger it. Here are the triggers.

      Triggers that justify orchestration, and the smallest machinery that covers each one
      Trigger What it looks like in the product Smallest thing that covers it
      Multi-step work with dependencies Extract invoice fields, look up the vendor, draft the reply A chain
      Branching on intermediate results If it is a contract, run the clause checker; otherwise summarize A conditional edge
      A different model for each subtask Small model classifies, embedding model retrieves, strong model drafts A router
      Retries, fallbacks, compensation Tool times out, provider errors, a write must be undone A retry wrapper, then a graph
      Long-running work needing durable state A twenty-minute migration that must survive a deploy A graph with persisted state
      Parallel fan-out with a join Check one claim against six sources at once, then reconcile A graph with a join node
      Human approval gate Nothing reaches a customer unreviewed A graph with an interrupt

      The counter-rule. If you cannot name which trigger applies, you do not need it yet. Premature orchestration is the most common reason an AI feature ends up slow and impossible to explain when it goes wrong.

      Model routing, and the fact that most requests are easy

      Model routing means classifying each incoming request and sending it to the cheapest model that can handle it, escalating to a stronger one when confidence is low or the first answer fails a check.

      Two facts make it pay. The spread inside a model family is large: cheapest and strongest differ by one to two orders of magnitude in price per token, and by a similar factor in latency. And difficulty is very unevenly distributed. A large share of traffic is genuinely easy, so paying frontier prices for it hands your gross margin to your provider.

      The signals are mundane. Task type, input length, required capability such as tool use or long context or vision, risk tier, customer tier, and how hard similar requests turned out to be. Two patterns sit on top. Escalation is deliberate: a verifier or confidence score says the cheap answer is weak, so you retry on a stronger model. Fallback is defensive: the chosen model is rate-limited or down, so you fail over to keep the feature alive.

      Designing the router

      In plain words. The thing that picks the model has to be so cheap and so fast that nobody notices it. And when it is unsure, it has to guess expensive.

      Cheap and fast comes first. A classifier that costs a tenth of a frontier call and adds 400ms has already lost. So use a small fine-tuned classifier, or embeddings plus a logistic regression. Not a frontier model call, because then you paid the expensive model to decide whether to use the expensive model.

      It also has to be calibrated: when it says 0.8 it is right about 80 percent of the time. Otherwise a confidence threshold is a number you cannot act on.

      And it has to fail toward the stronger model. Low confidence, an unknown task type, an error inside the router itself: the default is up, not down. A wrong escalation costs money. A wrong demotion costs trust.

      The rest is ordinary operations. Report routing accuracy, escalation rate, and cost per resolved request, and re-evaluate as the mix of requests drifts.

      The honest trade. A router is a new component that can be wrong, plus latency, plus a second thing to evaluate. If your volume is small or your traffic is uniformly hard, the right answer is one good model and no router. Routing is a scale-earned optimization.

      Unit economics at millions of calls

      In plain words. Sending the easy majority of your traffic to a cheap model is worth millions a year. Retrying the ones the cheap model gets wrong is what eats the saving.

      Do the arithmetic. This is where the argument is won. All numbers below are illustrative and rounded; the shape matters, not the digits.

      Ten million requests a month at $0.020 all-in on a frontier model is $200,000.

      Now route. Seventy percent to a small model at $0.0005 is $3,500. Twenty-five percent to a mid model at $0.004 is $10,000. Five percent to the frontier model at $0.020 is $10,000. The router itself, at $0.00002 a request, is $200. And be honest about escalations: say six percent of cheap answers come back weak and get retried on the frontier model, which is roughly $8,400. Total, about $32,100. A six-fold reduction, with escalations as the second-largest line item. Double that rate and you have eaten a third of the saving.

      The metric that matters. Cost per resolved request, not cost per API call. A cheap model that fails and escalates charges you twice and gives the user a worse experience, so a change that lowers cost per call while raising cost per resolved request is a regression dressed up as a win.

      At this scale a fraction of a cent per request is a strategy, not a rounding error. Going from $0.020 to $0.0032 on ten million requests a month is roughly $2 million a year, which is often the difference between a software-like gross margin and a services-like one. It is also why a cheap model with orchestration around it so often beats an expensive model with none.

      Many agents, one job each

      Sometimes a problem really does need several agents with non-overlapping jobs. Research, then draft, then verify, where each stage wants different tools and a different definition of success. A few principles separate a working system from a demo that impresses once.

      One responsibility and one success criterion per agent. If you cannot write the job and its pass condition in a sentence, the decomposition is wrong.

      Explicit typed contracts. Agent A does not send prose to agent B. It emits a defined structure with named fields, validated at the boundary, so a malformed handoff fails loudly at the seam instead of poisoning step four. And a shared state or artifact store rather than a conversation: agents write records and pass references, because one growing transcript overflows the context window.

      A supervisor owns sequencing. One orchestrator decides who runs next and when to stop, because agents negotiating with each other is nearly impossible to debug or bound. Parallelize only genuinely independent work, and design the join, because the hard part is what you do when two branches disagree. Give every agent a step and spend budget, and checkpoint each step so a failure at step four resumes at step four.

      In plain words. Small error rates multiply. A long run of mostly-right steps comes out mostly wrong.

      Now the Chapter 5 warning, with numbers. An agent that gets each step right 95 percent of the time finishes a ten-step task about 60 percent of the time, because 0.95 to the tenth power is roughly 0.60. Four such agents at eight steps each is 32 sequential steps, or roughly 19 percent. That is why real systems spend their effort on verification, checkpoints, and shrinking the step count rather than on adding agents.

      Multi-agent is usually a last resort. Every extra agent multiplies in another failure probability, another handoff that can be malformed, and more latency. The strongest answer names the specific reason one agent with more tools would not do: conflicting system prompts, tool sets that must be isolated for security, incompatible context needs, or a verifier that must not be the thing being verified.

      “What are the four layers of the AI product stack?”

      Why evaluation is the whole job

      In ordinary software, correctness is settled before you write the code. A spec says what a function should return. A test asserts that it returns it. Two moving parts, both yours.

      An AI feature breaks that in three places. The output is non-deterministic: the same input can produce different text on two calls in a row, because the model samples from a probability distribution instead of looking up an answer.

      Quality is graded rather than binary. A summary can be excellent, adequate, or quietly misleading, and no equality check separates those.

      And often there is no single right answer at all. Five rewrites of the same email can all be good.

      So “does it work?” stops being a yes/no question and becomes a measurement question. On which inputs. How often. How badly when it fails. Compared to what.

      Deciding what “good” means for this user, on this task, at this cost of failure, is the judgment nobody else on the team is positioned to make.

      The eval set is the most durable asset the team builds. Prompts get rewritten monthly. Models get swapped quarterly. The retrieval stack gets replaced. A curated set of real inputs, each with a defensible notion of what good looks like, survives all of that, and it is the only thing that tells you whether any of it helped. Your prompt is a draft. Your model is a vendor decision. Your definition of good is the product.

      This is why Meta asks candidates to whiteboard an evaluation framework live. Anyone can tweak a prompt until a demo impresses. Few people can say, with numbers, whether last week’s change helped.

      Measuring a system that answers differently every time

      Non-determinism does not make measurement impossible. It makes it statistical.

      In plain words. A model that answers differently every time is like a coin you are testing for fairness. You do not flip it once and write down the answer. You flip it many times and report the rate.

      Five moves follow from that. Run several samples per input, not one. Call the model three to ten times per test case and record the distribution. A case that passes seven times out of ten is a different animal from one that passes ten out of ten, and a single run cannot tell them apart.

      Score with a grader matched to the output type rather than string equality. Report a pass rate with its spread, because “82% ± 3” is a result and “82%” is a rumor. Pin what you can: temperature, seed if the provider exposes one, model version, prompt version, index snapshot. Then accept that the metric itself has noise.

      Common misconception: any improvement in the number is an improvement in the product. Your metric carries error from the finite sample of test cases and from the model’s own randomness. If re-running the identical system twice moves the score two points, a two-point gain from your clever new prompt is not evidence of anything. A change must clear measurement noise before you believe it. Estimate that noise floor once and post it next to the dashboard forever.

      Building the framework, in build order

      Here is what to draw on the whiteboard. The value is in the ordering, so say it out loud: dataset before metric, metric before tooling. Teams that build tooling first end up with a beautiful dashboard measuring the wrong thing.

      Seven steps, walked in order.

      Start at the end, before any metric exists. What does the user get when this works? “The support agent resolves the ticket without a human” is an outcome. “BLEU score” is not. Then price the failures. A clumsy sentence costs nothing. A wrong refund amount costs money. A fabricated medical claim costs a company. That price list sets every threshold that comes later.

      Next, draw the pipeline, because you cannot debug a blended score. A retrieval-augmented feature (Chapter 4) splits into retrieval and generation, measured separately. An agent splits into tool selection, tool arguments, and final synthesis.

      Now the step that takes longest and matters most: the dataset. Pull real user inputs from logs rather than inventing them. Spread them across the segments you care about and across difficulty, so the easy cases cannot drown the hard ones. Add adversarial cases on purpose: injection attempts, empty inputs, ambiguous requests, questions your product has no business answering. Then carve out a frozen regression set that never changes, so today’s score is comparable to next year’s.

      Only now pick graders, one per stage, cheapest reliable one first. That ladder is the next section.

      With graders in place you can set thresholds and a release gate. Agree them before anyone sees results. Some metrics trade off against each other; the redlines from Chapter 3 do not, and a safety or privacy redline blocks the release however good the quality numbers look.

      Wire the whole thing into CI, because an eval you have to remember to run is an eval you will not run. Then canary on a slice of real traffic with a rollback path, since a green CI run is not the same as a happy user.

      Last, and the step teams skip: point production back at the dataset. Every real failure becomes a permanent case that can never quietly return.

      The trap: a framework whose numbers nobody acts on is theater. The test is not whether it produces a dashboard. It is whether a red number has ever stopped a release.

      The testing pyramid for an AI product

      The classic pyramid says: many cheap tests at the bottom, few expensive ones at the top. It survives the move to AI almost unchanged. You add middle layers for what is graded rather than asserted.

      The principle: push correctness down into the cheap deterministic layers, and spend human judgment only where nothing else will do. Does the JSON parse. Does the citation resolve to a real document. Did the guardrail block the disallowed category. Those are all deterministic checks. Only a question like “is this explanation actually helpful to a confused customer?” deserves a person.

      Graders, and how far to trust a model as judge

      The grader ladder — climb it only as far as you must
      Grader What it can check Cost How far to trust it
      Deterministic / schema Valid JSON, required fields, forbidden strings, numeric ranges, citation resolves Near zero Completely, inside its narrow scope
      Reference-based A known answer, classification accuracy, recall against labeled chunks Low High where one ground truth exists
      LLM-as-judge Rubric quality, faithfulness to a source, tone, preference between two outputs An extra model call per case As far as its measured agreement with humans
      Human review Anything, including whether the rubric itself is wrong High and slow The anchor everything else is validated against

      LLM-as-judge means handing a language model a written rubric and letting it grade outputs at scale. You give it the input, the output, sometimes a reference or the retrieved source, plus explicit criteria. It returns a verdict. It is the only way to grade tens of thousands of free-text responses without a matching pile of human labor.

      In plain words. You are asking one model to mark another model’s homework. That works, as long as you check its marking against a human’s often enough to know how far off it runs.

      It works for relative comparisons (“which of these two is better?”), coarse rubric quality, faithfulness against a provided source, and triage that routes suspicious cases to humans. It fails at fine distinctions, and it carries named biases: position bias, preferring whichever answer came first; verbosity bias, mistaking length for quality; self-preference for its own model family; poor calibration, so a 7 versus an 8 means nothing stable; and sensitivity to rubric wording.

      The discipline that makes it usable: check it against a few hundred human labels and report that agreement as a number you can be challenged on. Keep humans grading a sampled subset forever, so drift shows up. Prefer pairwise comparison with randomized order over absolute scoring. Use a different model family than the one under test. Version the rubric like code, because changing it invalidates every historical score.

      Trust a model judge as a scalable proxy you have calibrated, never as ground truth you have assumed. The sentence that signals seniority: “our judge agrees with human raters 87% of the time, so I trust it for movements larger than a few points and nothing finer.”

      Precision, recall, and F1 in stakeholder language

      One example, carried all the way through. A model flags messages as toxic so a moderator can review them. There are 10,000 messages. 200 of them are genuinely toxic. The model flags 100, and 80 of those flags are correct.

      In plain words. Precision answers “when we shout, are we right?” Recall answers “when something is wrong, do we shout?” One model can be good at one and bad at the other.

      Four numbers come out of that. 80 messages we flagged and should have. 20 we flagged that were fine. 120 toxic messages we let through. And 9,780 fine messages we correctly left alone.

      Precision looks only at what we flagged. 80 right out of 100 flags, which is 0.80. Say it as “four in five of our flags deserved it.”

      Recall looks only at what was truly toxic. 80 caught out of 200, which is 0.40. Say it as “we miss three of every five toxic messages.”

      Two honest sentences about one model. Precision is about wasting the moderator’s time and annoying innocent users. Recall is about toxic content staying up.

      Now the part that matters in a product conversation. The trade-off is a dial, not a bug. Flag aggressively and recall rises while precision falls. Flag conservatively and the reverse. No setting maximizes both, so which way you turn the dial is a business decision about which mistake costs more. For child-safety content you buy recall. For auto-deleting a paying customer’s post you buy precision.

      F1 is the harmonic mean of the two, 2PR/(P+R), here 0.53. It exists so you can rank systems with one number. Its limitation is that it quietly assumes both errors cost the same, which is almost never true. If a false negative costs a hundred times a false positive, F1 points the wrong way. Weight it (an Fβ score) or report the pair against a stated threshold.

      Never present precision and recall without saying which error you have chosen to make more often, and why. That sentence is the difference between reporting metrics and making a decision.

      Retrieval quality versus faithfulness

      For any retrieval-augmented feature, two separate things can go wrong, and one blended “quality” score cannot tell them apart.

      Retrieval quality asks whether the right source material was found at all. It uses the same two ideas as above, measured against chunks you have labeled relevant: recall at k is how many of the relevant chunks show up in the top k results, precision at k is how many of those k results are relevant. Where the good chunk lands matters too, first beats eighth, and there are named measures for that (MRR, NDCG).

      Faithfulness asks something else: is the answer supported by the context it was given? Break the answer into claims and check each one against the retrieved text. That verdict holds whether or not the text was any good.

      After launch: swaps, observability, and the feedback loop

      Catching regressions after a model swap

      Swapping the underlying model is the most dangerous routine change in an AI product. Two reasons. A new model can raise average quality while destroying one specific behavior your product depends on. And your prompts are quietly overfit to the model they were written against: phrasing, examples and format instructions were all tuned until that model complied. A different model has different habits.

      So: run the frozen regression suite before the swap. Diff outputs on identical inputs instead of only comparing aggregate scores, and read the cases that changed rather than the mean. Report per segment, always. Shadow or canary the new model on real traffic before cutover. Pin an explicit version, and keep the previous one live so rollback is a config change rather than a project.

      Observability: logging enough to reproduce a bad answer

      A status code says the request succeeded. It says nothing about whether the answer was good.

      In plain words. If someone says the answer was wrong yesterday, you need to rebuild that exact moment. So log what the model was actually sent, not just that the call worked.

      Per request, log the full prompt actually sent including retrieved context, the model name and version, sampling parameters, every tool call with its arguments and result, token counts and cost, latency broken down by stage, the output, and any grader or guardrail verdicts. Tie all of it together with one trace id.

      The reason is blunt. A bug you cannot reproduce cannot be fixed. “A user said the summary was wrong yesterday” is not a bug report. A trace id is.

      Above the logs sits monitoring: online quality proxies, cost and latency per stage, tool error and retry rates, refusal and fallback rates, and drift in the input distribution.

      One tension to handle honestly. Prompts contain user data, so full-fidelity logging collides with privacy and retention policy. The usual resolution: short retention for raw prompts, redacted identifiers, a consented subset kept longer for eval use, strict access control. Settle it with legal before an incident settles it for you.

      Human-in-the-loop feedback

      Two different things share the name. HITL as a quality gate means a human reviews or approves before the system acts. The part teams forget is to capture that decision as a label, which turns the safety mechanism into a labeling pipeline. HITL as a data flywheel means collecting signal to improve the system.

      Make the feedback control cheap and specific. “Wrong facts”, “missed my question”, “too long” is worth far more than a lone thumbs-down, which tells you a user was unhappy and nothing about what to change. Capture implicit signals too. Did the user edit the output, retry, abandon it, or accept it and send it? Those arrive in far higher volume and carry less bias, because only annoyed people click thumbs-down. Route flagged cases to reviewers with the full trace attached, and make the labels flow into the eval set and the roadmap.

      The failure mode is collecting feedback nobody ever uses. A thumbs-down button writing to a table no one queries is worse than no button, because it looks like a feedback system and quietly buys the team permission to stop thinking.

      Diagnosis: when quality drops, and when the evals lied

      Step 2 includes their version, not only yours. Providers update models behind an alias, so a system nobody touched can degrade overnight. That is the argument for pinning. Step 6 catches the boring causes that masquerade as quality problems: truncated context, a silent fallback to a smaller model when the primary rate-limits, a retrieval timeout that returns zero chunks and a confident answer built on nothing.

      Offline evals passed but users are unhappy

      This is normal, not a scandal. Offline evals are a filter for obvious breakage, not proof of user value.

      Diagnose in order. Suspect the eval set first: wrong distribution, too easy, stale. Then suspect you are measuring the wrong construct, optimizing a proxy rather than the outcome users care about. Then look outside the model at latency, UX, expectation setting, and how failure is presented. Then segment, because an aggregate pass hides a badly served cohort. Then hunt for a gap between production and eval: a different prompt template, a different retrieval config, an unpinned model version, context that only exists in the real request path. Whatever you find becomes a permanent case in the eval set. That is how the gap narrows.

      Questions this chapter answers

      Try answering each one out loud before you open it. The gap between what you said and what is written below is the part you have not learned yet.

      “How would you eval this feature before launch?”

      Start from the user outcome and the cost of failure. Build a dataset of real inputs before I pick any metric. Grade each stage with the cheapest reliable grader, gate launch on thresholds plus hard redlines, and canary rather than trust offline numbers.

      Concretely: define what success means for the user and what each failure mode costs. Split the feature into measurable stages. Assemble 100–300 real inputs from logs, stratified across segments and difficulty, plus deliberate adversarial cases, and freeze part of it as a regression set. Choose graders per stage: deterministic checks first, reference-based where ground truth exists, a model judge with a written rubric for graded quality, humans as the anchor. Establish a baseline and a noise floor by running the unchanged system repeatedly. Agree thresholds and redlines before I see results. Run it in CI, then canary a small slice with a rollback path.

      Going deeper: naming the baseline and the noise floor unprompted, and saying plainly that offline evals filter for breakage rather than prove user value. Hence the canary.

      “Whiteboard an evaluation framework from scratch”

      Seven boxes in build order: user outcome and failure cost, pipeline stages, dataset, graders per stage, thresholds and release gate, CI, and a production loop. The ordering is the answer. Dataset before metric, metric before tooling.

      I draw the pipeline first so the stages are visible, then hang a grader off each stage. The dataset gets the most whiteboard space, because that is where the judgment lives: real inputs from logs, stratified by segment and difficulty, adversarial cases added on purpose, and a frozen regression slice so today’s number is comparable to next quarter’s. Graders climb a cost ladder and I stop as low as I can. Thresholds split into tradeable metrics and hard redlines that block a release outright. CI makes it automatic. The return arrow from production makes it compound.

      Going deeper: saying out loud that a framework whose numbers nobody acts on is theater, and offering the test for it. Has a red number ever stopped a release here?

      “What is LLM-as-judge and when do you trust it?”

      Using a model with a written rubric to grade outputs at scale. I trust it as a proxy I have calibrated against human labels, and I report that agreement as a number. Never as ground truth I have assumed.

      It earns its place on relative comparisons, coarse rubric-based quality, faithfulness against a provided source, and high-volume triage into a human queue. It is unreliable at fine distinctions and carries known biases: position bias toward whichever answer came first, verbosity bias toward long confident text, self-preference for its own model family, poor calibration between adjacent scores, and sensitivity to rubric wording. The discipline: validate against a few hundred human labels and publish the agreement rate, keep humans grading a sampled subset to catch drift, prefer pairwise comparisons with randomized order, use a different model family than the one under test, and version the rubric like code.

      Going deeper: quoting a concrete agreement number and then deriving the resolution limit from it. If the judge agrees with humans 87% of the time, it cannot adjudicate a two-point difference.

      “Precision, recall, F1: explain them to a stakeholder”

      Precision is: of the things we flagged, how many were right. Recall is: of the things we should have flagged, how many we caught. They trade off against each other, and choosing where to sit is a business decision about which mistake is more expensive.

      Worked example: 10,000 messages, 200 truly toxic. We flag 100 and 80 are correct. Precision is 80/100 = 0.80, so four in five flags are justified. Recall is 80/200 = 0.40, so we miss three in five toxic messages. F1 blends them, 2(0.8)(0.4)/1.2 = 0.53, and its purpose is ranking systems with one number. Its limitation is that it assumes a false positive and a false negative cost the same, which is almost never true. If missing toxic content costs a hundred times more than an unnecessary review, F1 points the wrong way, and I would weight it or report the pair against a stated threshold instead. Lowering the threshold buys recall and sells precision. That dial is ours to set.

      Going deeper: never presenting the pair without stating which error you have chosen to make more often and why, in the language of cost to the business.

      “How do you eval a non-deterministic system?”

      Stop measuring a point and start measuring a distribution: run n samples per input, report a pass rate with its spread, pin what you can pin, and believe a change only once it clears measurement noise.

      Concretely: for each test case run the model several times and record the outcome distribution, because a case that passes 7/10 is a different risk from one that passes 10/10 and a single run cannot tell them apart. Score with a grader matched to the output type rather than string equality. Report the pass rate with a confidence interval, or at least the observed spread. Freeze the variables you control: temperature, seed if available, model version, prompt version, index snapshot. Then establish the noise floor by running the unchanged system several times and watching how much the score wobbles on its own. A change smaller than that is not a result, however good the story around it is.

      Going deeper: volunteering that your own metric is noisy and that you have quantified it. Most candidates treat the eval as an oracle and worry only about the model’s randomness.

      “What’s your testing pyramid for an AI product?”

      Wide base of fast deterministic tests, schema validation and guardrails. Then component evals on retrieval, tool selection and single prompts. Then end-to-end scenario evals. A thin top of human review and online experiments.

      The principle is unchanged from ordinary software: push as much correctness as possible down into the cheap deterministic layers, and spend human judgment only where nothing else will do. Does the JSON parse, is the required field present, does the citation resolve to a real document, did the guardrail block the disallowed category. All of that belongs at the base and costs milliseconds. Component evals let me attribute a failure to a stage. End-to-end evals check that the stages compose into the user outcome. Human review and online experiments sit at the top because they are the only things that can tell me the rubric itself is wrong.

      Going deeper: pointing out that most AI teams have an inverted pyramid, all vibes at the top and nothing at the base, and that the first week on a struggling AI product is usually spent building the base.

      “How do you catch regressions after a model swap?”

      A frozen regression suite run before the swap, output diffing on identical inputs, per-segment reporting, then a canary or shadow deployment with the old version live for rollback.

      A model swap is uniquely dangerous because a new model can improve average quality while breaking one specific behavior the product depends on, and because prompts are quietly overfit to the model they were written against. So I do not compare aggregate scores alone. I diff the actual outputs on the same inputs and read the cases that changed. I report every metric per segment, because an average hides a broken cohort. I shadow the new model on real traffic, or canary a small slice, before cutover. In production I pin an explicit version and keep the previous one live, so rollback is a config change rather than a project.

      Going deeper: naming the trap concretely. Aggregate went up four points so we shipped, and the workflow generating most of the revenue silently collapsed. Per-segment analysis is what makes an aggregate safe to read at all.

      “What is observability for AI products?”

      Logging enough per request to reproduce any output exactly: the full prompt actually sent including retrieved context, model and version, sampling parameters, tool calls with arguments and results, tokens and cost, per-stage latency, the output, and grader verdicts, all under one trace id.

      Without the exact context that produced it, a bad output cannot be reproduced and therefore cannot be debugged. A status code tells you the call succeeded and nothing about whether the answer was good. On top of the logs sits monitoring: online quality proxies, cost and latency by stage, tool error and retry rates, refusal and fallback rates, and drift in the input distribution. There is a real tension here, because prompts contain user data. So I want short retention on raw prompts, redaction of identifiers, a consented longer-lived subset for eval use, and access control, decided with legal in advance rather than during an incident.

      Going deeper: the trace id and the privacy trade-off. Anyone can list fields to log. Connecting the chain end to end and admitting that full-fidelity logging conflicts with retention policy is the senior move.

      “How would you design a human-in-the-loop feedback system?”

      Decide first whether the human is a quality gate or a source of improvement signal, then design so every human decision is captured as a label that flows into the eval set and the roadmap.

      As a gate: a reviewer approves before the system acts, and the approve/reject/edit decision is logged as a label with the full trace attached, so the safety mechanism doubles as a labeling pipeline. As a flywheel: make explicit feedback cheap and specific, so users pick “wrong facts” or “missed my question” instead of a bare thumbs-down. Then instrument implicit signals, meaning edits, retries, abandonment and acceptance, because they arrive in far higher volume and carry less bias than ratings only annoyed users leave. Route flagged cases to reviewers with full context so review costs seconds. And close the loop: labels become eval cases and prioritized work.

      Going deeper: naming the failure mode, which is collecting feedback nobody ever uses. A thumbs-down writing to a table no one queries is worse than no button, because it buys the team false comfort.

      “Offline evals passed but users are unhappy. What now?”

      Assume the eval set is wrong before assuming the users are. Check representativeness first, then whether I am measuring the wrong construct, then everything outside the model, then segments, then an eval-versus-production gap.

      In order: is the eval distribution actually like real traffic, or is it too easy or stale? Am I optimizing a proxy rather than the outcome users care about, accuracy when they wanted a single-turn answer, fluency when they wanted brevity? Is the unhappiness even about model quality, or is it latency, unclear expectations, or a UI that presents failure badly? Does an aggregate pass hide a cohort that is served badly? And is there a gap between eval and production: a different prompt template, a different retrieval config, an unpinned model version, context that only exists in the real request path? Whatever I find becomes a permanent case.

      Going deeper: framing the gap as expected rather than embarrassing. The job is to shrink it case by case, not to be surprised by it.

      “How do you eval faithfulness vs retrieval quality?”

      Separately, always. Retrieval gets recall at k and precision at k against chunks labeled relevant, plus a rank-aware measure. Faithfulness gets claim-level checking against the retrieved context, independent of whether that context was any good.

      The separation buys a diagnosis rather than a grade. Bad retrieval with faithful generation means the model did its job and the index failed, so I fix chunking, embeddings, k, or filters. Good retrieval with unfaithful generation means the answer was sitting in the context and the model invented something else, so I fix the grounding instructions, force citations, or change the model. Bad on both means fix the index first, because you cannot judge generation on garbage input. For faithfulness I break the answer into claims and check each against the retrieved text, which gives me a per-claim failure rate rather than a vague score.

      Going deeper: asking how often a single blended quality score tells you which of those four situations you are in. Never. That is the whole reason for the split.

      “What would you check first when quality drops?”

      First that the drop is real and not measurement noise or a dashboard bug. Then what changed and when: my prompt, my retrieval, my code, or the provider’s model version.

      Then in order: did the input distribution change rather than the system, which is a different problem entirely; segment to see whether it is everywhere or in one cohort; split by stage to localize it to retrieval, tool calls, or generation; and check the infrastructure symptoms that masquerade as quality problems, such as timeouts, truncated context, rate limits and silent fallbacks to a smaller model. Providers update models behind an alias, so a system nobody touched can degrade overnight. That is the argument for pinning versions and keeping a frozen regression suite you can rerun to separate “the world changed” from “we changed”.

      Going deeper: saying why the runbook exists. Under pressure the default move is to start rewriting the prompt, which is at once the least likely cause and the change hardest to attribute afterward, because it alters everything.

      “Meta: Whiteboard an evaluation framework for an AI feature”

      I would draw the pipeline, hang a grader off each stage, and walk seven steps in build order: outcome, stages, dataset, graders, thresholds, CI, production loop. And say up front that the dataset is the deliverable and the metric comes second.

      Pick their feature and make it concrete. State the user outcome and the cost of each failure mode. Draw the stages so failures are attributable. Then spend most of the time on the dataset: real inputs from logs, stratified by segment and difficulty, adversarial cases added deliberately, a frozen regression slice. Ladder the graders from deterministic checks up to human review, and calibrate any model judge against human labels. Split thresholds into tradeable metrics and blocking redlines. Wire it into CI. Close the loop from production failures back into the dataset. Finish with what would make me distrust the framework: no noise floor, no per-segment view, and no red number that has ever blocked a ship.

      Going deeper: talking about organizational behavior rather than architecture. Who owns the eval set, who may change the rubric, and what happens when the gate goes red on a date the company has already announced.

      Eight moves you can run out loud

      You will be handed a question you did not prepare for. That is the point of the round.

      Two candidates can draw identical boxes and get opposite ratings, because the score is on the reasoning, not the diagram. So carry a structure, and narrate it.

      1. Who, and what job. Name one user and the job they need finished, then check it with the interviewer. The wrong user is the only mistake here you cannot recover from.
      2. The quality bar, and what failure costs. A wrong summary of a birthday message is noise. Of a contract deadline, a lawsuit. What failure costs decides how many guardrails you need.
      3. Data flow, end to end. Draw it, including the boring parts: auth, storage, logging. That is where the real constraints live.
      4. Model choice, and what is even ML. Decide which parts need a model at all. Then which model: how good on your task, how fast, how expensive, how much context it holds, whether it returns clean JSON and calls tools, where the data may be processed.
      5. Failure modes, each with a fallback. Without the second column you have drawn a happy path, not a system.
      6. Budgets, with numbers. Milliseconds per stage, dollars per thousand requests, a target eval score. Round is fine. Numberless reads as junior.
      7. How you evaluate it. What is in the eval set, who labels it, what score gates a release.
      8. What you cut for v1. Scope discipline is the loudest seniority signal available in forty minutes.

      The idea in one line. They are not buying your architecture. They are buying your ability to say why this and not that, with a number attached.

      Worked example: summarize my unread messages

      The same eight moves answer the Microsoft and Google versions of this question.

      1. User and job. Take the heavy case: someone back from vacation with 40 to 200 unread messages across 25 threads. The job is not “read my mail.” It is “tell me what needs me, in what order, and let me act.” That one sentence turns a summarizer into a triage tool.

      Clarify “unread.” Unread is a lie in most systems. A preview pane marks a message read. So does a notification, or a sync bug. Define it instead as arrived since the last session, in threads the user takes part in, minus bulk. A product decision, and it moves quality more than model choice does.

      2. Quality bar and cost of failure. A good summary is not shorter text. It is who needs what from me, and by when. So what you measure is recall on action items and deadlines, not fluency. The expensive failure is a missed obligation, which is why an unsure model shows the original instead of guessing, and every claim links back to its source message.

      3. Data flow, and the hard parts. Input size is the surprise. One thread has two lines, another has 80 messages, and that spread of three orders of magnitude means no single prompt shape survives. Clean the threads first: strip signatures and quoted history, the “> On Tuesday you wrote” blocks that paste the whole thread into every reply. On a long thread, summarize the old part once, cache it, and feed only new messages plus that summary.

      Ordering, and when not to summarize. Ranking should not be a model. A cheap score you can explain does it: is the user addressed directly, is a question pointed at them, is there a deadline, how much does the sender matter. Summarize the top three to five, one line each for the rest, because a wall of summaries is another inbox. And under roughly 40 words, show the thread verbatim.

      4. Model and privacy. Start with a hosted frontier model. But personal messages are the most sensitive data most people own, so answer four questions plainly: is content sent to a third party, is it retained, is it trained on, can the user switch it off. Zero-retention terms in writing, in-region processing, metadata logged rather than content. If a provider will not sign that, you have the real case for self-hosting open weights.

      In plain words. You can do the work when the user asks for it, or do it when the message arrives and have the answer waiting. Same work, same bill, and the second one feels instant.

      5 and 6. Freshness, latency, cost. Cache on (user, thread, last message id). Then decide when the work happens. Summarize when the user taps and you owe them two to four seconds. Summarize on receipt and you owe a 150 ms cache read. That is the biggest lever here.

      Then do the cost arithmetic out loud. Five threads per active user per day, roughly 3,000 input and 200 output tokens each, at illustrative prices near $3 and $15 per million tokens. About six cents a day. Survivable inside a $12 subscription, fatal on a free tier.

      7. Evaluation, and when it is wrong. The eval set is 200 threads with human-written summaries, scored for action-item recall. Then assume you will be wrong. A one-tap “this was wrong” writes into that set, low confidence renders the original rather than a number, and the feature takes no actions on the user’s behalf. A wrong summary is recoverable. A wrong auto-archive is not.

      8. The v1 you actually ship. Direct messages and threads where the user is on the To line. Top three threads, one model call each, precomputed on arrival, two raw lines shown if the call fails. One surface, paid tier only.

      Cut: the learned priority model, because a hand-written score is 80% as good and debuggable. Per-user style tuning, no eval set yet. The cross-thread digest, a different product. Attachment reading, on cost and privacy. None is on the path to learning whether triage-by-summary is worth anything.

      Latency, quality, cost — pick two, then cheat

      These three pull against each other. Fast and good is expensive. Cheap and good is slow. Fast and cheap is worse. The senior move is to name the corner this feature gives up, and why.

      Then the levers, and which way each pushes. A bigger model buys quality and costs latency and money. Longer prompts and deeper retrieval buy quality and cost both. Every extra call in sequence adds its full latency, so two 800 ms calls back to back is a 1.6 second feature.

      Capping output length cuts latency and cost together, because output tokens are the slow, expensive ones. Batching raises throughput per GPU and lowers cost per request, for a little latency. Caching the response, the retrieved chunks and the prompt prefix cuts cost and latency with no quality loss. Which is why you build it first.

      The idea in one line. Real latency is how long the work takes. Perceived latency is how long the user waits for something useful. Streaming, optimistic UI and precomputation change the second without touching the first, which is usually the cheapest win on the table.

      Then the discipline. Budget backward from the experience, not forward from what you built. Decide the card must appear in 300 ms, allocate 50 ms of network and 30 ms of cache read, and anything that cannot fit its slice gets precomputed or removed. Measuring what you built and calling it the budget is how products end up with a four-second p95 that nobody owns.

      Buy, fine-tune, build — and when not to use a model at all

      The default is buy. A hosted frontier model and a careful prompt is the cheapest way to find out whether the feature is worth anything. Most AI features die on “do users care,” not on “is the model good enough.”

      Fine-tune when prompting keeps drifting off a format you require, or to test whether a small cheap model can match a big one on one narrow task. It means continuing training on a few hundred of your own examples, so the behavior lives in the weights instead of in every prompt. Two preconditions: an eval set that can prove it won, and your willingness to own a retraining treadmill each time the base model moves. It teaches behavior, not facts. If the model is missing your data, you want retrieval.

      Build or self-host only when something forces it: residency rules, volume where per-token pricing loses to owning hardware, a latency floor an API cannot meet. Be honest about the full bill. The model is rarely the expensive part. The data pipeline, the evals, the monitoring and the engineers maintaining them are. Then: differentiator, or table stakes? Rent table stakes. Own what customers choose you for.

      Choosing a model for a task is the same reasoning, smaller. Write down what the task needs, run your own eval set across three candidates, take the cheapest that clears the bar. Public benchmarks give you a shortlist, never a decision.

      Common misconception. “Use the best model everywhere.” That spends ten times the money to reach the same quality on the 90% of requests that were easy. Route by difficulty: small model by default, escalate on low confidence.

      When you should not use an LLM at all

      The strongest senior signal in the round is showing that you are solving the problem, not deploying the technology.

      No LLM when the task is deterministic and has a correct answer: arithmetic, sorting, date math, validation, written business rules. Not when the question is really a database query. Not when the latency budget is single-digit milliseconds, or volume makes per-call cost absurd. Not where you need auditability and reproducibility, as in a regulated decision. And not where a regex, a lookup table or a small classical model already works.

      The idea in one line. An LLM is the right tool for ambiguous language tasks, and an expensive, slow, unreliable tool for anything with a known algorithm.

      Churn prediction, and why trees win on tables

      Churn prediction is supervised binary classification. Years of rows, one per customer per month. Columns like tenure, plan, logins, tickets and invoice failures. And a crisp label: they cancelled, or they did not. Abundant labels, structured input, a number out. That is the shape a gradient-boosted decision tree (XGBoost, LightGBM) was built for.

      In plain words. A tree is a stack of yes-or-no questions about your columns, like “fewer than three logins?” An LLM has to read that same row as a sentence and guess. One learned the answer from your own history; the other is seeing it for the first time.

      Start with the splits. A tree asks threshold questions like “logins < 3,” so it does not care that one column is dollars and the next is a plan name. Nothing needs scaling or encoding. And most tabular signal is thresholds and combinations: an invoice failed and logins collapsed in the same month. Two splits catch that. A text model has to infer it from a sentence.

      Boosting then adds hundreds of small trees, each correcting the last, which is what makes it shrug off irrelevant columns and missing values. And it was trained on your outcome, so out comes a calibrated probability plus a list of the columns that drove it, which you can show a regulator. The LLM sees the row as a sentence: nothing calibrated it, it costs orders of magnitude more, it is slower, it drifts across provider versions, and it never saw your label.

      What a hybrid system looks like

      Generalize it. A classical or deterministic core makes the decision. The language models sit at the edges, where language lives: messy input coming in, explanation going out, ambiguous leftovers in between.

      Fraud review: a trained scorer decides, an LLM drafts the analyst memo from the free-text note. Support routing: a small classifier takes the obvious 85%, the LLM takes the rest, and the handoff rule is written down, “classifier confidence below 0.8 escalates.” Analytics: an LLM writes the SQL, the database computes the numbers.

      Put the LLM where the ambiguity is. Keep it out of any path where correctness can be checked.

      The cost floor: GPUs, tokens, and ten million users

      In plain words. A GPU serving a model spends most of its time waiting for numbers to arrive from memory, not doing math. Almost everything about what serving costs follows from that one fact.

      Deep learning runs on GPUs because training and inference are dominated by large matrix multiplications, and every element of the result can be computed independently. A CPU has a few very fast general cores, good for branchy sequential logic. A GPU has thousands of simple cores and roughly ten times the memory bandwidth, meaning it can pull far more numbers per second out of its own memory. Thousands of parallel multiply-and-adds need both halves of that.

      Generating a token is usually limited by bandwidth rather than by arithmetic. Each token streams the whole set of weights past the compute units, so the math finishes and waits for more numbers to arrive. Which is why the weights have to fit in VRAM, the memory attached to the GPU itself. Spill into ordinary system memory and throughput collapses, because every number now travels a slower road.

      Now separate two costs that get confused. Training is huge, one-time and capital-like: thousands of GPUs for weeks. Inference is small per unit, recurring, and scales with usage, so it lands in gross margin every month.

      Serving economics come down to batching and utilization. A decode step barely touches the arithmetic capacity while it waits on memory, so many requests together cost almost the same as one, and cost per request falls steeply as the batch grows. Rent per token and someone else eats the idle capacity, at a markup. Own the hardware and you pay for 3am.

      Common misconception. That an estimation question wants the right number. It wants your structure and your assumptions, said out loud, and a sanity check at the end.

      In plain words. The prompt you cannot see is the one you pay for. System instructions, tool definitions, chat history and retrieved documents ride along on every call, which is why input usually outweighs output on the bill.

      So “estimate inference cost for 10M users” is a funnel you narrate. State assumptions. Users to actives to requests per active per day. Input and output tokens, priced separately because output costs several times more. Multiply to a day, then a month, then add the multipliers people forget: retries, chains and agent loops, your own eval traffic.

      Then the part that actually scores. Check the total against revenue, and if inference eats 40% of it, name the levers: cache, route to a smaller model, shorten context, precompute, rate-limit the heaviest 1%.

      Failure, redlines, and what survives the next model

      Treat every model output as untrusted input. Validate it like a form submitted by a stranger on the internet.

      Schema-validate every tool argument and every structured output. On failure, retry once or twice with the validation error fed back, then fall back rather than loop. Put a timeout on every hop with a defined fallback. Give every side-effecting call an idempotency key, and a confirmation gate before anything irreversible. Cap steps and spend per task. Add a circuit breaker that degrades to a simpler non-AI path.

      And fail honestly. “I could not read that document” beats a confident summary of a document the model never saw.

      Redlines, in a design conversation, means saying where the guardrail sits and who owns it. Some categories are absolute and no business case reopens them: help creating weapons capable of mass casualties, child sexual abuse material, targeted harassment, a real person’s likeness used for deception. Everything below that is a threshold set by the cost of failure.

      The mechanics: filter the input, filter the output, and set tool-level permissions on what the system can do, which matters far more than what it can say. Approval gates on the highest-stakes actions. A red-team set you re-run every release. And a named owner: safety or legal holds the block, not the PM.

      Then say the trade-off out loud, because pretending it is free is the weak answer. Guardrails add latency, cost and false positives, so tune the threshold against what each error costs.

      What stays constant when models improve monthly

      Almost everything that matters.

      The user problem does not change when a model improves. Neither does the workflow around it. And your eval set compounds, because it is how you know a new model is better for you. What is disposable is the prompt, the model, the framework, and any advantage that consists only of having called the API first.

      Questions this chapter answers

      Try answering each one out loud before you open it. The gap between what you said and what is written below is the part you have not learned yet. That gap is the point.

      “Design an AI feature: summarize unread messages”

      The job is triage, not summarization: what needs me, in what order, with a link to the original. So I rank threads first, summarize only the top few, precompute on arrival, and fall back to raw text whenever the model is unsure.

      I would clarify “unread” first, because a preview pane or a sync bug marks messages read in most systems. Define it as arrived since the last session, in threads the user is part of, excluding bulk. Quality is recall on action items and deadlines, because the expensive failure is a missed obligation, not an inelegant sentence. The pipeline: assemble the thread and strip quoted history and signatures, score threads with a cheap explainable rule (directly addressed, question aimed at the user, deadline present, sender importance), summarize the top three to five, show anything under 40 words verbatim, cache on (user, thread, last message id), and precompute when the message lands so app-open is a 150 ms cache read instead of a three-second wait. Cost is roughly six cents per active user per day at illustrative prices, so v1 is paid-tier only. Privacy is first-class: zero retention, in-region processing, no training on content, easy opt-out.

      Going deeper: naming the input-size spread of three orders of magnitude, refusing to summarize short threads at all, and saying the feature takes no actions. A wrong summary is recoverable. A wrong auto-archive is not.

      “Latency vs quality vs cost: how do you trade them off?”

      I set a budget backward from the experience, pick which corner this feature is allowed to sacrifice, and spend first on the moves that improve perceived latency, because those are close to free.

      The three pull against each other: fast and good is expensive, cheap and good is slow. The levers and their directions: model size buys quality at the cost of both others; prompt and retrieval length the same; every sequential call adds its full latency; capping output length cuts latency and cost together; batching cuts cost per request and adds a little latency; caching cuts cost and latency with no quality loss. Then the distinction that matters most. Real latency is how long the work takes. Perceived latency is how long until the user sees something useful. Streaming, optimistic UI and precomputation move the second without touching the first. So the order is: cache, stream, precompute, shorten context, route easy requests to a smaller model, and only then reach for a bigger one.

      Going deeper: budgets in milliseconds per stage derived from the UX, quoted at p95 rather than average, with a named component that has to be precomputed because it cannot fit its slice.

      “Build vs buy vs finetune: walk me through the call”

      Default to buying a hosted frontier model with a good prompt, because it is the cheapest way to learn whether the feature is worth anything. Fine-tune only when I have an eval set proving it wins on format or on cost at equal quality. Build only when residency, volume or latency forces it.

      Fine-tuning is right for consistent format and style, or to check whether a small cheap model can match a big one on one narrow task. It teaches behavior, not facts, so if the gap is knowledge the answer is retrieval. It also creates a retraining treadmill every time the base model moves. Self-hosting is justified by hard constraints, not pride: data that legally cannot leave, volume where per-token pricing loses to owning hardware, a latency floor an API cannot hit. On total cost of ownership, the model is rarely the expensive part. The data pipeline, evals, monitoring and the people are. Then: differentiator or table stakes? Rent table stakes, own what customers choose you for.

      Going deeper: making the eval set the precondition for every move. Without one you cannot tell whether the fine-tune helped, so you are buying a maintenance burden on a hunch.

      “When do you not use an LLM at all?”

      Whenever the task has a known algorithm or a checkable correct answer. An LLM is the right tool for ambiguous language, and an expensive, slow, unreliable tool for anything else.

      Concretely: arithmetic, sorting, date math, validation and explicit business rules go in code. Questions about your data are a database query. If the latency budget is single-digit milliseconds, no network model call fits. If volume makes per-call cost absurd next to the value of the call, it is not viable. If the decision must be auditable and reproducible, as in credit, medical or hiring, you need something that returns the same answer twice and can be explained to a regulator. If a regex, a lookup table or a small classical model already solves it, that is the answer. The practical version of this discipline is the hybrid design: classical core for the decision, LLM at the edges for language.

      Going deeper: volunteering a case in your own product where you removed a model. Saying “we replaced the classifier prompt with twelve lines of rules and quality went up” lands harder than any architecture.

      “Why XGBoost over an LLM for tabular prediction?”

      Because tabular prediction is a supervised problem with crisp labels, and a boosted tree is trained directly on your outcome — so it wins on accuracy, cost, latency, calibration and auditability at once.

      The mechanism matters, so say it. Trees ask threshold questions, so columns in different units need no scaling or encoding. Most tabular signal is thresholds and combinations, which two splits capture on their own. Boosting adds hundreds of small corrective trees, which is what makes it shrug off irrelevant features and missing values. Because it is fit to your label, the output is a calibrated probability you can threshold on, with feature importances you can defend. An LLM reads the row as a sentence, returns a probability-shaped string that is not a measured frequency, costs orders of magnitude more, is slower, drifts when the provider updates, and never saw your outcome.

      Going deeper: saying why trees fit tables rather than asserting it, then immediately naming where the LLM does belong: converting unstructured text into features that feed the tree.

      “What does a hybrid system look like in practice?”

      A deterministic or classical core makes the decision, and LLMs sit at the edges where language lives: parsing messy input, generating explanation, handling the ambiguous residual.

      Three shapes. Churn: an LLM turns tickets and transcripts into features, a boosted tree produces the probability, a fixed threshold picks the playbook, an LLM drafts the outreach. Support routing: a small classifier handles the obvious 85% and the LLM handles the rest, under the written rule “classifier confidence below 0.8 escalates.” Analytics: an LLM writes SQL, the database computes the numbers, and the model never does arithmetic. In each case the routing rule is written down, and the number that drives money never comes out of a language model.

      Going deeper: naming the routing rule and its threshold, plus what happens when the LLM edge fails. The classical core keeps working with slightly worse features rather than the feature going dark.

      “Role of GPUs in your cost structure?”

      Training is capital-like: huge and one-time. Inference is recurring and scales with usage, so it sits directly in gross margin — and its unit cost is set by batching and utilization.

      Serving a token is mostly limited by memory bandwidth. The weights have to stream through the compute units, so arithmetic capacity sits idle while memory delivers. That is why batching many requests together costs almost the same as serving one, why cost per request drops steeply as the batch grows, and why utilization is the whole game. Renting per token means someone else carries the idle capacity and you pay a markup. Owning GPUs flips it: cheaper at high steady volume, but you pay for 3am and for every reserved instance nobody used. The number I care about as a PM is cost per active user per month against revenue per active user per month.

      Going deeper: connecting it to gross margin rather than hardware trivia, and noting that inference cost per token has fallen fast and repeatedly, so a feature that is marginal today may be fine in a year.

      “Estimate the inference cost for 10M users”

      I will state assumptions out loud and funnel. 10M registered, 20% daily active gives 2M DAU, three requests each gives 6M requests a day, 4,000 input and 400 output tokens each, at roughly $3 and $15 per million tokens gives about $108,000 a day, so call it $3.2M a month and about $4.5M with retries, chains and eval traffic.

      Every number there is illustrative and round, and I would flag that. Two places people go wrong. They forget that input carries the system prompt, tool definitions, history and retrieved context, so it usually dominates the token count. And they forget the multipliers: retries, agent loops that turn one user action into five calls, their own evaluation runs. Then the sanity check, which is the actual point. If 10% of users convert at $10 a month, that is $10M of monthly revenue and inference is 45% of it, which does not ship. So the levers: cache, route easy requests to a small model, shorten context, precompute on write, cap steps, rate-limit the heaviest 1%.

      Going deeper: pricing input and output separately, and ending on unit economics rather than on a total. The number is a means to “does this business work.”

      “How do you design for failure (bad tool calls, timeouts)?”

      I treat every model output as untrusted input from a stranger. Schema-validate every tool argument and every structured output, retry once or twice with the validation error fed back, then fall back to something simpler and honest.

      Per failure mode: malformed output gets schema validation plus a bounded repair retry; a bad tool call gets argument validation, allowlisted tools and a dry-run for anything destructive; timeouts get an explicit limit at every hop with a defined fallback rather than an error page; duplicate side effects get idempotency keys so a retry cannot send the email twice; irreversible actions get a human confirmation gate; runaway loops get step and spend caps; an outage or error spike trips a circuit breaker that degrades to a non-AI path, keyword search instead of retrieval. Then the UI has to fail honestly. “I could not read that file” beats a confident summary of a file that was never read.

      Going deeper: bounding the retry. Unbounded self-correction loops are how a $0.02 request becomes a $6 request, so retries carry a counter and a spend cap.

      “Where do you draw the safety redlines?”

      A small set of categories is absolute and no business case reopens them. Everything else is a threshold I set from the cost of the failure, enforced in the architecture rather than in the prompt.

      Absolute: help with weapons capable of mass casualties, child sexual abuse material, targeted harassment, and generating a real person’s likeness or voice for deception. Below that, the mechanics decide the outcome: input filtering for obvious abuse, output filtering for what the model produced, tool-level permissions for what it can actually do, which matters much more than what it can say, and human approval gates on the highest-stakes actions. I test them with a red-team set that gets re-run every release, and I name the owner: a safety or legal reviewer holds the block and the PM does not overrule it. And I state the trade-off, because pretending it is free is the weak answer. Guardrails add latency and cost, and their false positives frustrate legitimate users, so the threshold is tuned against what each error type costs.

      Going deeper: putting the strongest guardrail on tools rather than on text. Restricting what the system can do is enforceable; instructing a model not to misbehave is a preference.

      “Models improve monthly. What stays constant?”

      The user problem, your eval set and definition of quality, your proprietary data and feedback loop, your distribution and trust, and the orchestration layer. The prompt and the model are disposable.

      Any advantage that consists only of having called the API first evaporates on the next release. What compounds is the loop: users produce data, data improves the eval set and the product, the product attracts users. That is why I architect for swappability. Every model call goes behind one internal interface, versions are pinned so nobody silently upgrades me, and the eval set is the contract a candidate has to pass. Then a better model is an upgrade I take on a Tuesday rather than a rewrite I fear.

      Going deeper: the inversion — if a 10× better model would kill your product, your product was the model. Say what you would still own in that world.

      “LLM or ML model for churn prediction? Why?”

      A gradient-boosted tree, not an LLM. Churn is supervised binary classification over structured history with abundant labels, and a tree beats an LLM on accuracy, cost, latency, calibration, explainability and reproducibility.

      The features are tenure, plan, logins, tickets, invoice failures; the label is cancelled or not; you have years of both. A tree is trained on that label directly, so it emits a calibrated probability you can threshold at a business-chosen cut, plus feature importances that survive an audit. The hybrid is the answer worth giving: an LLM turns support tickets and call transcripts into sentiment, complaint-category and escalation features that feed the tree, then writes the account manager a readable explanation at the end.

      Going deeper: that the interesting question is not model choice but whether the prediction changes any action. A perfectly calibrated churn score with no retention playbook attached is a dashboard, not a product.

      “How do you decide which model to use for a task?”

      I write down what the task needs, shortlist on those requirements, then run my own eval set across candidates and pick the cheapest model that clears the quality bar.

      The requirements list: does the task need reasoning or just fluent formatting; how much context; must it emit valid structured output and call tools reliably; what is the latency budget and the cost ceiling per call; where may the data be processed; is the license acceptable. Public benchmarks give a shortlist and nothing more. They are contaminated, averaged over tasks that are not mine, and measured with prompts I would not use. In production I expect a portfolio, not a winner: a small fast model for classification and extraction, a mid-tier model for the default path, a frontier model behind a router for the hard minority, everything pinned behind one interface so swapping is a config change.

      Going deeper: treating cheapest-that-clears as the objective rather than best-available, and having a routing rule that decides at runtime which bucket a request falls into.

      “Nvidia: Explain how GPUs are used in deep learning”

      Training and inference are dominated by large matrix multiplications, where every output element can be computed independently. A GPU has thousands of simple cores and very high memory bandwidth, which fits that shape; a CPU has a few fast general cores, which does not.

      In training, a forward pass is a chain of matrix multiplies and elementwise functions, and backpropagation is another chain of matrix multiplies computing gradients, run over batches for weeks across thousands of GPUs on fast interconnects because neither the model nor the data fits on one device. Inference has two phases. Prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel and is compute-heavy. Decoding produces one token at a time and is usually limited by memory bandwidth, because each token streams the weights past the compute units. Which is why the weights must fit in VRAM, the GPU’s own memory: spilling to system memory collapses throughput. And why batching many concurrent requests is nearly free, the core economics of serving.

      Going deeper: naming memory bandwidth rather than only core count, and separating prefill from decode. That distinction is exactly why input tokens are cheap and output tokens are expensive.

      “Microsoft: Explain trade-offs in model selection”

      Capability, latency, cost, context, structured-output and tool reliability, data residency, and provider risk — and you cannot maximize all of them, so you pick the cheapest, fastest model that clears the quality bar on your own eval set.

      The specific tensions: a bigger model raises quality and raises both latency and cost; a longer context window helps recall but costs tokens on every call and often degrades attention to the middle of the prompt; a fine-tuned small model can beat a large general one on a narrow task while losing all flexibility; an open-weights self-hosted model buys residency and control and costs you idle capacity and an ops burden; a newer frontier model buys capability and costs you stability, since your prompts were tuned against the old one. Concentrating on one provider is faster to build and a real dependency risk, which is why the abstraction layer and the pinned versions belong in the selection decision rather than after it.

      Going deeper: saying that model selection is a per-task decision inside a portfolio, not a company-wide standard, and that the eval set is what makes the trade-off measurable instead of aesthetic.

      “Microsoft: Walk through the system design of an AI experience”

      I use the same eight moves every time: user and job, quality bar and cost of failure, data flow end to end, what is even a model, failure modes with fallbacks, latency and cost budgets with numbers, evaluation, and what I cut for v1.

      Made concrete on the unread-summary example: the user has 200 unread messages and needs to know what needs them; the quality bar is recall on action items, because a missed deadline is the expensive failure; the flow is clean the thread, rank cheaply with rules rather than a model, summarize the top few, cache, render with citations; failure modes are timeout, malformed output and invented obligations, each falling back to raw text; the budget is 300 ms perceived and six cents per active user per day; evaluation is 200 human-written summaries scored for action-item recall; v1 is direct messages, top three threads, paid tier, one language.

      Going deeper: checking in after the framing step, and narrating the trade-off at each choice rather than presenting a finished architecture. They are scoring the reasoning, not the boxes.

      “Google: Design a high-level system for Gemini responding”

      Request in, then: safety and abuse checks, cache lookup, intent and difficulty routing, context assembly, model call with streaming, tool calls if needed, output validation and safety filtering, response with citations, and logging into evaluation. Every stage has a timeout and a fallback.

      Walking it: authenticate and rate-limit, classify the request for policy and for difficulty, and check the response cache and the prompt-prefix cache before spending anything. Assemble context (system instructions, tool definitions, relevant history, retrieved documents) under an explicit token budget, since this is where cost is decided. Route by difficulty: small model for the majority, frontier model for the minority that needs it. Stream tokens so the first word arrives in under a second. If tools are needed, validate every argument against a schema, run independent ones in parallel, cap the steps. Filter the output, attach citations, then log the request, the model version and the user signal into the eval pipeline. Fallbacks throughout: a smaller model on capacity failure, retrieval-free answers when retrieval times out, an honest error rather than a fabricated one.

      Going deeper: putting caching and difficulty routing before the model call rather than after, and treating the logging path as a first-class component. That is the loop that improves the system.

      “OpenAI: What to do with a model with 10x capability at 10x cost?”

      Not a yes or no — a segmentation and unit-economics question. Route the small share of requests that genuinely need the capability to it, serve everything else cheaply, and check on my own eval set which segments actually improve.

      Request difficulty is unevenly distributed, so most traffic gets nothing from 10× and a minority is transformed by it. First I find that minority. Then I ask where value per request can support the price. Some tasks are worth almost nothing more when done better; others are unlocked entirely, because a task nobody could automate at 85% accuracy becomes automatable at 99%. That is the real question. Does this enable a new tier, a new segment, or a workflow that was previously impossible. Then I check whether willingness to pay exists rather than assuming a better product justifies a higher price. And frontier capability gets much cheaper on a fairly reliable schedule, so part of the decision is what I learn by building now versus what I can wait for.

      Going deeper: the eval-driven test. 10× on a public benchmark is frequently 1.1× on your task, so you measure per segment on your own set before committing a single dollar of margin.

      CH·09 / The Interview

      Explaining what you know — and building something worth explaining

      Everything before this chapter was knowledge. This one is delivery. A repeatable shape for explaining a technical idea, a script for the moment you do not know something, and a way to describe your own work so that people believe you. Interviews are the worked example because they are the hardest version of the problem. The skill underneath is explaining AI to people who do not build it, and you will need that most weeks of your career.

      What you will be able to explain after this chapter

      • Walk me through an AI feature you shipped and the model choices behind it
      • Google: What AI agents have you built to be more productive?
      • Glean: Have you built any end-to-end agentic systems?
      • Amazon: When did you go deep into an ML system to diagnose a problem?

      What the room is actually scoring

      The list of questions this site is built around looks like sixty facts to memorize. It is not. Almost none of them have a single right answer, and anyone who wanted recall would send you a written test instead of spending an hour with you. Each question is a probe: cheap to ask, expensive to fake. Four things are being measured.

      One: do you reason about systems and trade-offs. Every real decision in an AI product costs something. More retrieval costs latency. A bigger model costs money. Catching more of the bad cases means flagging more of the good ones. They want to hear you name the cost before they ask for it, because a product manager who only sees upside ships things that fall over.

      Two: do you know where your knowledge ends. This is calibration. Someone who says “I am confident about this part and genuinely unsure about that part” is safe to trust with a roadmap. Someone whose confidence is flat across everything is not, because you cannot tell their guesses from their facts.

      Three: can you explain a technical thing to a non-technical person. Most of your week will go on carrying one idea between an engineer and an executive without dropping it. That is the job, not a garnish on it.

      Four: do you decide with evidence rather than vibes. The tell is whether a number shows up in your answers before anyone asks for one. A rate, a baseline, a sample size. Or whether everything is “it felt better.”

      Seen that way the list collapses. Retrieval, cost, evaluation, agents: they all reduce to the same three moves. Name the mechanism. Name the trade-off. Name the measurement. Make those moves reliably and you can handle the questions nobody wrote down, which is most of what you will actually be asked.

      The idea in one line. They are not testing whether you already have the answer. They are testing what you do with a question.

      The shape of an answer

      Under pressure, people either freeze or ramble. Both have the same cure: a shape you have rehearsed until it runs without your attention. Here is the one to use. Four beats, about a minute, then silence.

      Beat 1 goes first for an unglamorous reason. Your listener decides in about ten seconds whether you know this, then spends the rest of the answer confirming that decision. Build up to your conclusion and those ten seconds sound like someone assembling an opinion in real time. The read is uncertainty even when the ending is excellent. Put the conclusion in the first sentence and everything after it is heard as evidence for it.

      In plain words. The two answers below are shapes, not scripts. Read them for the order of the beats rather than the wording.

      Here is the shape on a question from the list: what is RAG and when would you use it?

      Direct: “RAG means you retrieve relevant documents at query time and paste them into the prompt, so the model answers from text you chose instead of from memory. You use it whenever the answer depends on facts that change or that the model was never trained on.” Mechanism: “You chunk your documents, embed each chunk into a vector, and at query time embed the question and pull the nearest chunks by similarity. Those chunks go into the context window with an instruction to answer only from them.” Limit: “It only fixes knowledge, not behavior or format. That is what fine-tuning is for. And retrieval quality caps the whole system: if the right chunk is not retrieved, no model can save the answer. Retrieval failure is the dominant failure mode in every RAG system I have built.” Instance: “In mine, chunking on fixed character counts split tables across chunks and accuracy dropped about fifteen points on table questions until I chunked on document structure instead.”

      Now a second one: how would you detect hallucination in production?

      In plain words. Nothing can flag a made-up answer with certainty. So you sample real answers and check whether the source you handed the model actually said that.

      Direct: “You cannot detect it perfectly, so you measure a proxy: how often the answer is unsupported by the retrieved source.” Mechanism: “Sample real traffic, and for each answer check whether every claim is supported by the retrieved chunks. Humans grade a small set, then a model judge calibrated against those human grades runs continuously.” Limit: “The judge is itself a model and agrees with humans maybe eighty to ninety percent of the time, so I treat the number as a trend line, not a truth.” Instance: “On my own hundred hand-labeled questions the judge and I disagreed on eleven, and nine of those were ambiguous sources rather than a wrong model.”

      Common misconception. Longer answers are not safer answers. Past about ninety seconds unprompted, every extra sentence is a new surface to be probed on, and you picked it yourself. Answer, then stop. Let them decide which part deserves more.

      The depth ladder, and what to do on the rung above your knowledge

      Technical interviewers do not ask scattered questions. They pick one topic and climb. Definition, then mechanism, then trade-off, then edge case. They stop climbing when you stop being able to follow, and that is the measurement. Not whether you reached the top. How high you got before the answers thinned out.

      Learn to hear which rung you are on, because the right length differs by rung. The cue is the question word. “What is” is rung one, and the answer is one or two sentences. Padding a definition reads as stalling. “How does” is rung two, and now you owe them the actual machinery. “Why did you choose” or “why not just” is rung three, and the answer has to contain a comparison and a cost, not a preference. “What happens if” is rung four, where there is usually no clean answer at all. They want to watch you think.

      There is one place where people fall off the ladder and the interview quietly ends: attention. It is the most common rung-two question in the field and the most commonly frozen on, because people learn the phrase “attention lets the model focus on relevant tokens” and have nothing underneath it. Asked how the focusing works, they stop. Chapter 1 exists so you can answer: queries and keys produce dot-product scores, softmax turns those scores into weights that sum to one, and the weights mix the value vectors. Say that out loud in thirty seconds without notes and you have removed the most likely way this goes badly.

      When you do not know

      You will hit a rung above your knowledge in every interview. That is the design; they are looking for your ceiling. The moment is scored heavily and most people handle it badly. Here is the sequence that handles it well.

      In plain words. Quantization, in the example below, means storing a model’s numbers at lower precision so it runs on cheaper hardware. You do not need to know it. Watch the sequence, not the topic.

      Say plainly what you do not know. “I have not worked with quantization directly, so I do not know the accuracy loss at four bits.” Five seconds, no hedging, no apology. State what you do know that is adjacent. “What I do know is that it is a memory-and-speed trade against precision, and that the win comes from fitting a bigger model on smaller hardware.” Reason out loud toward a candidate answer. “So I would expect the loss to be small for generation quality on common tasks and larger for anything numerical or long-tail, because those depend on fine distinctions in the weights.” Label your confidence. “I would put that at maybe sixty percent. It is reasoning, not something I have measured.” Say what you would check. “I would run our own eval set at full precision and at four bits and look at where the gap concentrates, rather than trusting a published average.”

      That answer contains no facts about quantization and it is a good answer. It shows calibration, reasoning from first principles, and an instinct to measure: three of the four things being scored.

      The single most damaging failure is bluffing. Technical interviewers probe exactly one level deeper than whatever you just said. That is the whole method, so a fabricated answer does not survive the next question. When it collapses it poisons everything you said before it, because now they have to wonder which of your confident statements were also invented. One honest “I do not know” costs you almost nothing. One caught bluff costs you the interview.

      Explaining it to someone who does not care how it works

      Two questions on the list are pure translation: explain hallucination to an executive, explain precision and recall to a stakeholder. They are there because this is the job. They are also unexpectedly hard for anyone who has just learned the material, because new knowledge wants to be displayed.

      The method has five steps and the last one is the hardest. Lead with the business consequence: what will go wrong, for whom, how often. Use one analogy, and only one. A second analogy does not add clarity, it signals that the first one failed. Quantify with a number they already care about: tickets, hours, dollars, percent of users, never F1 or perplexity. State the trade-off as a choice that belongs to them, which makes them a participant instead of an audience. Then stop.

      What you cut: the mechanism, the architecture, the model names, the benchmark, and your own cleverness. None of it survives contact with someone who has eleven minutes and four other decisions today. If they want the mechanism they will ask for it, and it lands far better as an answer than as a lecture.

      Describing your own work credibly

      Now the hard part. Several of these questions assume you have already done this inside a company: walk me through a feature you shipped, what agents have you built, when did you go deep to diagnose an ML problem. You can have shipped for a decade and still never have shipped a model-backed feature with an ML team. Pretending otherwise is dishonest and, practically, fatal. A fabricated story dies at rung three, when they ask why you chose that model over the obvious alternative.

      In plain words. Nobody is asking how big your last project was. They are asking whether you have made a call, watched it fail, measured the failure, and changed your mind.

      These questions ask about demonstrated judgment, and judgment does not require a headcount or a launch date. It requires decisions made under real constraints, something breaking, a measurement, and a changed mind. You can produce all of that in a weekend. A project with exactly one user, you, described with its real failure modes and its real numbers, beats a smooth retelling of a large company’s roadmap every time. The second one has no rung three.

      The idea in one line. Scale is not what makes a story credible. Specificity is. Build three or four small things where you can account for every decision you made and every one you turned down.

      Here is what to build. Each one maps onto a chapter of this site, so building it doubles as studying, and each one unlocks a specific question.

      Last, the honest frame. A thin track record in AI specifically is not the disqualifier. The absence of demonstrated judgment is, and judgment is demonstrable through built work at any scale. That is good news, because built work is the one input you fully control.

      Know the shape of the market, though. Teams hiring a technical AI PM usually want someone who has already lived inside a model-backed product, so an application that leads with a general product record mostly produces silence.       “Walk me through an AI feature you shipped and the model choices behind it”

      “I have not run this inside an ML team, so let me walk you through something I built myself and put in front of real users: a question-answering system over five hundred pages of our internal process documents, used by about forty people.”

      Then the structure. Situation: people could not find rules that existed, so the feature was retrieval-augmented answering with a citation on every response. Decisions and rejections: I started with the strongest available model to establish a quality ceiling, then tested a cheaper, smaller model against it on a hand-labeled set of eighty questions. The gap was about four points on answer accuracy and roughly eight times the cost, so I shipped the cheap model for retrieval-grounded questions and routed only multi-document synthesis questions to the expensive one. I rejected fine-tuning outright, because the problem was missing knowledge, not wrong behavior, and the documents change every quarter. What broke: fixed-size chunking split tables of deadlines in half, and accuracy on date questions was near zero until I chunked on headings instead. What I measured: retrieval hit rate at top five, answer accuracy against my labels, and cost per question. What I would do differently: build the eval set before writing any code, because I spent two weeks tuning against my own impressions.

      Going deeper: naming the option you rejected and why, and having a real number attached to the model choice. “I picked the cheaper model because it was good enough” is a vibe. “Four accuracy points for eight times the cost, and here is where I put the boundary” is a decision.

      “Google: What AI agents have you built to be more productive?”

      “One I use daily: an agent that triages my reading backlog. Three tools, which are fetch a URL, search my notes, and append to a file, plus a hard cap of six steps per item.”

      Say what it does, then say what you learned from its failures, because that is the real question. Mine reads a saved link, checks whether I already have notes on the topic, and writes a three-line summary plus a decision: read now, skim, or drop. The interesting part is the guardrails, and every one of them came from a failure. It looped on paywalled pages, retrying the fetch tool forever, so I added a step budget and a rule that a tool failing twice ends the task with an explicit error instead of a retry. It sometimes called the file-append tool with malformed arguments, so I validate tool arguments against a schema and hand the validation error back to the model as an observation, which recovers about four times in five. And it was expensive per item until I stopped feeding whole pages into context and started retrieving only the sections that matched my notes. Cost per item dropped by roughly two thirds with no drop in my agreement with its decisions, which I checked on thirty items by hand.

      Going deeper: talking about termination, error handling and cost per task rather than about capability. Anyone can describe an agent that works on a good day. The stopping rule, the retry policy and the cost curve are what signal you have run one for more than an afternoon.

      “Glean: Have you built any end-to-end agentic systems?”

      “Not in production, and I want to be clear about that. I have built an end-to-end loop in the technical sense, meaning plan, act, observe, repeat, with real tools, a stopping rule and a fallback, running for one user at small volume.”

      Draw the loop, because “end-to-end” is the operative word and they want to know whether you understand what it includes. Mine takes a research question, breaks it into sub-questions, runs a search tool on each, writes intermediate findings to a scratch file, and produces a cited summary. End-to-end means the parts people skip: a defined input contract, a state store that survives between steps so the model is not re-deriving context, schema validation on every tool call, an explicit termination condition, and a fallback that returns the partial findings with a warning instead of nothing when the budget runs out. The honest limits: no concurrency, no permissions model, no human-in-the-loop approval step, and I have never run it against a corpus large enough for retrieval quality to degrade. Those four are exactly what I would expect to be hard at Glean’s scale, and I would rather name them than pretend I have solved them.

      Going deeper: volunteering the boundary before they find it. “No permissions model, no approval step, and I have not stressed retrieval at scale” turns a gap into evidence of judgment. The same gap found by them turns into evidence of overclaiming.

      “Amazon: When did you go deep into an ML system to diagnose a problem?”

      “My document QA system started giving confidently wrong answers about deadlines, and it took me a week to find out that the model was fine and retrieval was the problem.”

      Tell it as a diagnosis, in order, because the order is the skill being tested. First I reproduced it: collected fourteen failing questions instead of working from a feeling that it had got worse. Then I isolated the stage. I fed the model the correct source text by hand, and it answered all fourteen correctly, which ruled out generation and pointed at retrieval. Then I measured: for each failing question I checked whether the correct chunk was in the top five retrieved, and it was there in only three of fourteen. Then I looked at the chunks themselves. Deadlines lived in tables, and my fixed-length chunking had split every table so the dates ended up in one chunk and the column headers in another. The retrieved text was literally meaningless. The fix was structure-aware chunking plus keeping headers with rows. Then I verified instead of assuming: retrieval hit rate on those fourteen went from three to twelve, and I re-ran the full eighty-question set to make sure I had not broken anything else, which I had not. The lesson that carries over is that in a multi-stage system you localize before you fix, because the loudest symptom is almost never where the bug lives.

      Going deeper: the ruling-out step. Bypassing retrieval to test generation in isolation is what a debugger does; guessing and changing the prompt is what everyone else does. Also: re-running the full eval after the fix, so you can say you did not trade one failure for another.

      Twenty lines that answer a hundred questions

      These are not slogans. Each one is a mechanism compressed into a sentence, and nearly every question on the sheet falls out of one or two of them. Hold these properly and you can reason your way to an answer you have never rehearsed.

      1. The model writes one token at a time and has no plan. Paragraphs, arguments and neat conclusions are what next-token prediction looks like from the outside after a few thousand repetitions. Nothing inside is holding an intention about sentence forty.
      2. Nothing you say changes the model. Weights are frozen during inference. Your conversation, your documents and your tool results move exactly one thing, and that is the text sitting in the context window.
      3. The context window is the whole working memory, and it is a budget you spend. System prompt, tool schemas, history, retrieved chunks and the question all compete for the same finite space. That is why context engineering is a job about priorities, not about wording.
      4. Temperature changes the shape of a distribution, not the creativity of the model. It divides the logits before softmax. Low values sharpen a preference the model already had, high values flatten it, and flatter includes flatter toward nonsense.
      5. Temperature zero buys you less randomness, not reproducibility. Greedy decoding removes the sampling noise. Batching, floating-point ordering on GPUs, a model version updated without telling you, and any change upstream in your prompt are all still there.
      6. The model never runs a tool. Your code runs it. The model emits a name and a JSON argument object. Your runtime decides whether to run it, with whose credentials, and what happens when it fails. That boundary is where permissions and audit live.
      7. Treat everything the model writes as untrusted input. A retrieved document or a stranger’s message may have shaped it. Validate against a schema, authorize the action in ordinary code, and never let a sentence be the authorization.
      8. Fine-tuning teaches form. Retrieval teaches facts. Prompting teaches neither durably. Wrong tone, wrong format, wrong shape is a fine-tuning problem. Wrong or stale information is a retrieval problem. Confusing the two burns a quarter.
      9. Hallucination is an error rate you engineer down, not a defect you patch. A system trained to produce plausible continuations has no internal flag for unsupported. So you lower the rate with grounding, constrained output and permission to say it does not know, then you measure what is left.
      10. A sterner prompt cannot fix a mechanism. Wording moves behavior at the margin. No wording gives the model a fact it does not have, or a self-check it does not possess.
      11. Measure retrieval and generation separately or you will fix the wrong half. Retrieval gets recall at k and rank-aware scores. Generation gets faithfulness against the text that came back. One blended quality number tells you nothing you can act on.
      12. Retrieval is a search problem, and search has decades of prior art. Chunking, keyword matching, hybrid scoring and reranking all come from information retrieval. BM25 is a serious baseline, and embeddings frequently fail to beat it on their own.
      13. The eval set outlives every prompt and every model. Prompts and model versions are consumables. The labeled cases that define good for your product are the only asset that compounds, and they are what makes a model swap a Tuesday instead of a crisis.
      14. Offline green plus unhappy users means your eval set is not your traffic. The gap is a bug in the eval, not in the users. Sample the real failures, label them, and the set becomes the thing that would have caught it.
      15. A chain is only as reliable as the product of its steps. Ninety-five percent per step, over ten steps, is about sixty percent end to end. The fix is almost always fewer steps and a verification gate, not more agents.
      16. You cannot test for equality, so test for distributions and properties. Run the case many times and assert on pass rates, invariants, schema validity and overlap with the cited sources. assert output == expected is not available to you.
      17. Agent is a claim about control flow, not about intelligence. If the model decides what happens next and the loop runs until a goal is met, it is an agent. If you fixed the order in advance, it is a chain, however clever the prompts are.
      18. Latency is a budget, with a p95 and a named owner for every slice. Retrieval, first token, generation and post-processing each get a number. Streaming buys tolerance rather than speed, and what a user judges is the latency they feel.
      19. Cost per resolved request is the metric. Cost per call is a distraction. A cheap model that retries twice and then hands the case to a human is the expensive option. An expensive model that ends the conversation can be the cheap one.
      20. Sometimes the senior answer contains no language model at all. Structured features plus historical labels means gradient-boosted trees, on cost, latency, accuracy and auditability. Knowing when not to reach for an LLM is the clearest seniority signal in the room.

      If you remember one thing from the twenty. Two of them are the spine: the model only predicts tokens, and everything else in the product is code you wrote around that fact. Almost every question is asking which of the two you are talking about.

      The traps

      Each of these is a claim that collapses under one follow-up question. They all sound reasonable, which is exactly why they get tested.

      • Trap: “Temperature controls how creative the model is.” Actually: it divides the logits before softmax, flattening or sharpening a distribution the model already had. It cannot add an idea that was not already near the top of the ranking.
      • Trap: “Set temperature to zero and the output is deterministic.” Actually: greedy decoding only removes the sampling step. Batch composition, floating-point ordering on GPUs, a model version updated quietly and any upstream prompt change all still move the output.
      • Trap: “The model learns from our conversations and gets better at our domain.” Actually: weights are frozen at inference. Nothing persists between calls except what your code puts back into the context window.
      • Trap: “RAG solves hallucination.” Actually: it changes the failure from invention to unfaithfulness: a confident answer the retrieved documents do not support, or a confident answer built on the wrong document. That failure needs its own metric.
      • Trap: “We will fine-tune it so it knows our documentation.” Actually: fine-tuning teaches form, not facts. The result is stale the day it ships, cannot cite a source, and has to be redone every time the docs or the base model change. Retrieve instead.
      • Trap: “The model calls our API.” Actually: the model emits a function name and a JSON argument object. Your code parses it, checks permissions, calls the API, and feeds the result back. Authorization and audit live there, not in the prompt.
      • Trap: “We will add a stronger instruction to the system prompt.” Actually: a prompt cannot give the model a fact it lacks or a self-check it does not have. Prompt changes are a real tool at the margin and a non-answer to a mechanism problem.
      • Trap: “We are at 95% accuracy, so we are ready to launch.” Actually: accuracy on which set, collected how, and which 5% is failing. One number with no error-cost asymmetry and no provenance for the test set is not a launch decision.
      • Trap: “Context windows are huge now, so we can drop retrieval.” Actually: attention work grows with the square of the sequence, so cost and latency climb steeply, relevance degrades when the important span is buried in the middle, and you lose citations and access control. Retrieval is a precision tool, not a workaround for a small window.
      • Trap: “We use an LLM as judge, so the evaluation is objective.” Actually: the judge is a model with position bias, a taste for longer answers, and failure modes correlated with the system it is grading. It is useful once you have calibrated it against human labels and know its agreement rate.
      • Trap: “It is unreliable, so we will add another agent to check it.” Actually: each extra step multiplies in another failure probability, and a checker that shares the generator’s blind spots checks nothing. Prefer fewer steps and a deterministic verification.
      • Trap: “Prompt injection is a jailbreak problem, so it belongs to the content filter.” Actually: injection targets your tools and your data. The attack arrives inside a retrieved document or a user file and asks the model to use the permissions you gave it. The mitigation is least-privilege tools and deterministic checks, not a better filter.
      • Trap: “Let us pick the best model first, then build.” Actually: the frontier moves monthly. What stays constant is your eval set, your data pipeline, your product surface and your unit economics. Build those so that swapping the model is a config change and a test run.

      The pattern behind all thirteen. Every trap swaps a mechanism for a wish. The correction is always the same move: say what the mechanism can and cannot do, then put the fix in the layer that can actually hold it.

      Glossary

      Every technical term this site uses, written to be understood cold. If you can say a version of each one out loud in a single breath, you know more than the sheet asks for.

      Agent
      A system where the model decides what happens next, which tool to call and whether to continue, in a loop that runs until a goal is met or a limit is hit. What separates it from a chatbot is control flow, not intelligence.
      Attention
      The mechanism that lets every position in the sequence read from every other position. Each token makes a query vector, compares it against every token’s key vector by dot product, turns those scores into weights that sum to one, and takes that weighted mix of value vectors. A soft lookup table the model learned.
      Autoregressive
      Producing output one unit at a time, each new unit conditioned on everything already produced. It is why generation cannot be spread across output tokens in parallel, and why a long answer takes proportionally longer.
      Batching
      Running many requests through the model together to keep the hardware busy. It is most of why inference is affordable, and one reason two identical prompts can differ: your neighbors in the batch change the arithmetic.
      BM25
      A keyword ranking function that scores a document by how often your terms appear, adjusted for how rare each term is and how long the document is. Decades old, cheap, and hard for embedding search to beat on its own, especially on names, codes and exact terms.
      Calibration
      Whether stated confidence matches actual accuracy. A calibrated system that says 70% is right about 70% of the time. Language models sound equally fluent whether they are right or wrong, so their tone is not a confidence signal.
      Chain
      A fixed sequence of model calls you laid out in advance, each step’s output feeding the next. Reliability multiplies across the steps, so shorter chains are better chains.
      Chain of thought
      Prompting the model to write intermediate reasoning before its answer. It helps because those intermediate tokens become part of the context the final tokens condition on. It is computation, not introspection, and the written reasoning is not a reliable account of what happened inside.
      Chunking
      Cutting source documents into passages small enough to retrieve and inject. The chunk is the unit of retrieval, so a bad boundary quietly caps how good the whole system can get. Overlapping chunks reduce the risk of cutting an answer in half.
      Circuit breaker
      A pattern that stops calling a failing dependency once errors cross a threshold, returning a fallback immediately instead. It keeps one slow model or tool from eating every worker and taking the product down with it.
      Confusion matrix
      The two-by-two table of true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives. Every classification metric is a ratio built from those four cells, which is why you draw it before arguing about a number.
      Context engineering
      Deciding what occupies the model’s finite input on each call, and in what order of priority: instructions, tools, history, retrieved evidence, the question. Prompt engineering is wording; context engineering is allocation, and allocation is the bigger job.
      Context window
      The maximum number of tokens one call can attend to, input and output together. It is the whole working memory of the system, and nothing outside it exists as far as that call is concerned.
      Cosine similarity
      The cosine of the angle between two vectors, so it compares direction and ignores length. The standard way to score embeddings: 1 is the same direction, 0 is unrelated.
      Decoding
      Turning the model’s probability distribution into actual output tokens, one at a time. Greedy decoding always takes the top token; sampling draws from the distribution as temperature and top-p have shaped it.
      Distillation
      Training a small model to imitate a larger one’s outputs. It is how a cheap fast model gets close to an expensive one on a narrow task, and it is what makes routing pay off.
      Embedding
      A list of a few hundred to a few thousand numbers that places a piece of text, an image or a token as a point in space, positioned so similar meanings land near each other. It is what makes semantic search possible.
      Eval set
      A curated collection of inputs with agreed expectations, used to measure quality the same way twice. It outlives every prompt and model in your product, and it turns a model swap into a test run.
      F1
      The harmonic mean of precision and recall, 2PR/(P+R). One number for ranking systems, and it assumes both error types cost the same, so state that whenever you use it.
      Faithfulness
      Whether every claim in a generated answer is supported by the evidence you gave the model. An answer can be true about the world and still unfaithful to the retrieved documents, which is a bug in a grounded system.
      Fallback
      The behavior you decided in advance for when the main path fails: a smaller model, a cached answer, a keyword search, a person, or an honest error message. Designing it is part of designing the feature.
      False positive / false negative
      A false positive is acting when you should not have. A false negative is failing to act when you should have. Nearly every product decision in this domain is a choice about which one you would rather make more often.
      Few-shot
      Putting a handful of worked examples in the prompt to show the model the pattern you want. It changes nothing in the weights, only what the next token is conditioned on.
      Fine-tuning
      Continuing to train a model on your own examples so a specific form, style or narrow behavior becomes its default. It teaches shape rather than knowledge, and it leaves you a versioned artifact to maintain.
      Forward pass
      One complete run of the input through the network, producing scores for the next token. Every generated token is a fresh pass over the whole context, which is why latency and cost both grow with length.
      Function calling
      The interface where you describe available functions and the model replies with a structured request naming one and filling in arguments. The model runs nothing; the request is text your code chooses to honor.
      Golden dataset
      The human-verified core of your eval set: inputs with reference answers everyone agrees are correct. Small, expensive, and the anchor you calibrate cheaper automated judges against.
      GPU
      A processor with thousands of simple cores and very high memory bandwidth, built for the huge parallel matrix multiplications that training and inference are made of. Most products rent it by the token rather than buying it.
      Gradient descent
      The training loop: measure the error, work out which way each weight should move to shrink it, take a small step, repeat billions of times. It happens during training and never during a conversation.
      Graph
      An orchestration pattern where steps are nodes with explicit shared state, and edges can branch, loop and checkpoint. Worth the complexity for long-running or resumable work, and overkill for anything linear.
      Grounding
      Tying an answer to specific supplied evidence, and being able to point at the span behind each claim. Grounding can be measured, which is its whole advantage over asking the model to be careful.
      Guardrail
      A deterministic check wrapped around the model: input filters, output schema validation, allowed-value lists, permission checks, rate limits, redlines. It belongs in code, because a model cannot reliably police a failure it shares.
      Hallucination
      Output that is fluent, confident and unsupported, either false about the world or unsupported by the evidence provided. It is a rate you measure and reduce rather than a bug you close, because the mechanism that writes good text writes this too.
      Human in the loop
      A design where a person approves, corrects or takes over the cases the system is not confident about. Done well it is also your best data source, because every correction is a labeled example.
      Hybrid search
      Running keyword scoring such as BM25 alongside embedding similarity, then merging the ranked lists. It works because the two methods fail differently: keywords nail exact identifiers, embeddings catch paraphrase.
      Idempotency
      The property that doing an operation twice has the same effect as doing it once, usually through a key the caller supplies. Agents retry and duplicate tool calls as a matter of course, and a double refund is not a rounding error.
      Inference
      Using a trained model to produce output, with all weights frozen and only your input and the sampling settings free to vary. Nearly all of your production cost lives here.
      Instruction tuning
      The post-training stage that turns a raw text predictor into something that follows requests, by training on many examples of instruction and good response. It is why a base model finishes your sentence and a tuned model answers your question.
      JSON Schema
      A machine-readable description of what a JSON object must contain: fields, types, required keys, allowed values. It is how you describe tool arguments to a model and how you check what comes back.
      KV cache
      The stored key and value vectors for tokens already processed, so each new token does not recompute the whole sequence. It trades memory for speed, and at long context lengths it is a major consumer of VRAM.
      Latency budget
      An explicit split of the time a user will tolerate across the stages of a request, usually stated at p95 rather than average. Without one, latency is discovered rather than designed.
      LLM
      Large language model: a network with billions of parameters trained to predict the next token over vast amounts of text. Everything it does at runtime is that one operation, repeated.
      LLM-as-judge
      Using a model to grade another model’s output against a rubric. Cheap enough to run on every commit, but it prefers longer answers, is swayed by position, and fails where the system it grades fails, so it has to be calibrated against human labels.
      Logits
      The raw, unbounded scores the model produces, one per token in the vocabulary, before softmax turns them into probabilities. Temperature is applied to these numbers.
      MCP (Model Context Protocol)
      An open protocol for how applications expose tools, data and prompts to models, so one server can serve any compatible client. It turns an N-times-M integration problem into N plus M.
      Mixture of experts
      An architecture that routes each token to a small subset of specialized sub-networks instead of the whole model. You get a very large total parameter count at a much smaller compute cost per token, which is why parameter counts alone tell you little about speed or price.
      Model routing
      Classifying each request and sending it to the cheapest model that can handle it, escalating only when needed. The lever that most often fixes unit economics at scale, and the classifier itself needs its own eval.
      MRR (mean reciprocal rank)
      For each query, one divided by the rank of the first relevant result, averaged across queries. It rewards putting a good result at the very top.
      Multi-head attention
      Several attention computations running in parallel with different learned projections, then combined. Different heads end up tracking different kinds of relationship inside the same layer.
      NDCG
      Normalized discounted cumulative gain: a ranking metric that credits relevant results more when they appear higher, and normalizes against the best possible ordering. Use it when relevance comes in degrees rather than yes or no.
      Nearest neighbor
      The search behind semantic retrieval: given a query vector, find the closest vectors in the index. At scale it is done approximately, trading a little recall for a lot of speed.
      Next-token prediction
      The single training objective behind language models: given a sequence, predict what comes next. Everything that looks like reasoning, planning or style is a consequence of doing that extremely well.
      Observability
      Being able to answer, after the fact, exactly what happened on one request: the prompt, the retrieved chunks, the tool calls, the tokens, the cost, the latency, the output, the user’s reaction. Without it, debugging quality is guesswork.
      Offline vs online eval
      Offline evaluation runs a fixed set of cases before you ship, so it is repeatable and blocks regressions. Online evaluation reads real traffic afterward through edit rates, escalations and completed tasks, so it is honest but slow and confounded. You need both, and the gap between them is information.
      Overfitting
      When a model or a prompt is tuned so tightly to the examples in front of you that it does worse on anything new. Here it happens most often to prompts, and to eval sets that have been optimized against too many times.
      p50 / p95 latency
      The median and the 95th percentile of response time. The p95 decides whether a product feels broken, because the slow tail is where the complaints and the timeouts are.
      Parameter
      One learned number inside the network, a weight or a bias. The count is a rough proxy for capacity and memory footprint, and a poor proxy for quality, cost or speed.
      Post-training
      Everything done after pre-training to make the model usable: supervised fine-tuning on instructions, preference-based training, safety work, tool-use training. It shapes behavior, while pre-training created the capability.
      Precision
      Of the items the system flagged or returned, the fraction that were correct: TP/(TP+FP). The metric that governs wasted effort and eroded trust.
      Pre-training
      The long, expensive first stage: next-token prediction over an enormous text corpus, producing a model with broad capability and no manners. The knowledge comes from here, and it stops at a fixed cutoff date.
      Prompt caching
      Reusing the computed state for a stable prefix, usually your system prompt and tool definitions, across many requests at a reduced price. It rewards putting the unchanging part of your context first.
      Prompt injection
      An attack where instructions hidden in content the model reads, such as a retrieved document or a user file, are treated as if they came from you. The defense is least-privilege tools and authorization in code, because the model cannot reliably tell instruction from data.
      Quantization
      Storing weights at lower numeric precision, say 8 or 4 bits instead of 16, to cut memory and gain speed. It usually costs a little quality, and it is how large models fit on smaller hardware.
      RAG
      Retrieval-augmented generation: search your own data for relevant passages at request time and put them in the prompt, so the answer rests on current, private, citable text rather than on the model’s frozen memory.
      Recall
      Of the items that should have been found or flagged, the fraction that were: TP/(TP+FN). The metric that governs missed harm and missed value.
      Recall at k
      The retrieval version of recall: of the passages known to be relevant, how many show up in the top k. Measured on retrieval alone, before the model sees anything.
      Redline
      A behavior the product must never show, defined in advance, enforced deterministically, with a stated fallback and no confidence threshold that can allow it. A quality target is a dial; a redline is not.
      Reranking
      A second, more expensive scoring pass over the top candidates from cheap retrieval, using a model that reads query and passage together. The single upgrade that pays best in most RAG pipelines, because it fixes precision without re-indexing anything.
      RLHF
      Reinforcement learning from human feedback: people rank candidate responses, a reward model learns those preferences, and the model is optimized against it. Helpfulness, tone and much refusal behavior come from here.
      Sampling
      Choosing the next token by drawing from the probability distribution instead of always taking the most likely one. It is the source of the variation you see between identical prompts, and it happens outside the model.
      Semantic search
      Retrieving by meaning rather than by exact words, by embedding the query and finding nearby document vectors. It catches paraphrase, and it can miss the exact product code a keyword index would have found.
      SFT
      Supervised fine-tuning: training on pairs of input and desired output. The workhorse for teaching a model a specific format, register or narrow task.
      Skill
      A packaged set of instructions, and often scripts and resources, that a model loads into its context when a task calls for it. No weights change; it is text and files delivered at runtime.
      Softmax
      The function that turns logits into a probability distribution: exponentiate each score, then divide by the total, so every value is positive and they sum to one. Because it exponentiates, small logit gaps become large probability gaps.
      Structured output
      Constraining generation so the result is valid JSON matching a schema, often by limiting which tokens are permitted at each step. It turns a parsing problem into a guarantee, which is how model output becomes safe to hand to code.
      System prompt
      The instructions placed before the conversation that set role, rules, format and available context. It is ordinary tokens in the same window, weighted more heavily by convention and training, not a privileged channel the model is unable to ignore.
      Temperature
      A number that divides the logits before softmax. Below one it sharpens the distribution toward the top token; above one it flattens it toward the tail. It changes the shape of a distribution and never the model’s knowledge.
      Token
      The unit a model actually reads and writes: a common word, a word fragment, a punctuation mark, roughly four characters of English on average. Tokens are the billing unit, the context unit, and the reason the model cannot reliably count letters.
      Tokenizer
      The deterministic component that splits text into tokens from a fixed vocabulary and maps them to integers. It learns nothing at runtime, and its quirks explain a surprising share of odd model behavior on numbers, code and non-English text.
      Tool definition
      The name, description and argument schema you hand the model for a function it may request. The description is all the model has to decide when to use it, so a vague description is a production bug.
      Top-p
      Nucleus sampling: keep only the smallest set of most likely tokens whose probabilities sum to p, then sample from that set. It adapts to the distribution, staying narrow when the model is confident and widening when it is not.
      Trace
      The full record of one request through your system: prompt, retrieved context, model calls, tool calls, tokens, timings, cost and output. The unit of debugging, and the raw material of new eval cases.
      Transformer
      The architecture underneath modern language models: stacked layers, each pairing attention over the whole sequence with a position-wise feed-forward network. Its defining property is that all positions are processed in parallel during training.
      Unit economics
      The cost and revenue of a single unit of usage, per request or per user or per resolved case, rather than in aggregate. The number that decides whether a feature can exist on a free tier.
      Vector database
      A store built for approximate nearest-neighbor search over embeddings, with metadata filtering and updatable indexes. You need one because comparing a query against millions of vectors one at a time is too slow to serve.
      VRAM
      The memory on a GPU. It has to hold the weights plus the KV cache for every request in flight, which is why long contexts and large batches are memory-bound and why quantization matters.
      Weights
      The learned numbers that are the model. They are set during training and frozen during inference, the single fact that answers most questions about what a model can remember about you.
      XGBoost
      A widely used gradient-boosted decision tree library, and shorthand for the classical approach to tabular prediction. Fast, cheap, auditable, and still the right answer for churn, fraud and pricing problems with labeled history.
      Zero-shot
      Asking the model to do a task with no examples, relying on the instruction and whatever pre-training left behind. The baseline to measure before you add examples, retrieval or fine-tuning.

      The last thing. Nothing on this page will make you sound experienced. You cannot fake having shipped. What it can do is make you accurate, and an accurate junior who says “I have not measured that, here is how I would” beats a confident one who memorized a price list. Build the small thing, keep its eval set, and the experience stops being borrowed.
