      1. Tokens: the model never sees your letters

      Before a model can do anything with your text, the text has to become numbers. The component that does this is the tokenizer, and it is the least intelligent part of the whole system. It holds a fixed vocabulary — usually between 30,000 and 200,000 entries, depending on the model — and chops your string into the longest pieces it can find in that list. Each piece is a token with an integer id. The model never sees your characters. It sees a list of ids.

      Tokens are neither words nor letters. They are subword fragments discovered statistically before training even began: whichever byte sequences appeared often in the corpus earned their own vocabulary entry. Common English words got one token each, usually with the leading space attached — so ·the and the are two different tokens with two different ids. Rare words get shredded into four, six, eight pieces.

      Four consequences you will use constantly.

      Pricing and context limits are counted in tokens, not words. A useful order of magnitude for English prose is four characters per token, so 1,000 tokens is roughly 750 words — a planning rule of thumb, never a number to quote. What is stable is the shape: input and output are billed separately, and output costs several times more per token, because generating is serial work while reading is parallel.

      Non-English text costs more for the same meaning. Vocabularies are fit to data that skews heavily English, so a Portuguese, Turkish, or Thai sentence fragments into more tokens than its English translation — sometimes two or three times more. Higher price, and less of it fits in the window. If your product is multilingual, your unit economics are not uniform across markets.

      Character-level and digit-level work fight the representation. Ask the model to count the letter r in a word and it is reasoning over a few opaque fragments, not over letters — same for reversing strings and rhyme. Long numbers split into chunks that ignore place value, so digit arithmetic is unreliable by construction. Neither is a reasoning failure, so neither is fixed by prompting harder. Give the model a calculator instead, which is a tool call, covered in Chapter 5.

      Common misconception. “The model reads text like we do.” It does not. It reads a sequence of vocabulary ids. Every character-level weakness you have ever seen a chatbot exhibit traces back to this one design choice.

      2. Embeddings: meaning becomes geometry

      An integer id carries no meaning — token 2415 is not “more” than token 254. So the first thing the model does is look each id up in a big table, the embedding matrix. Row 2415 is a list of numbers, typically a few hundred to a few thousand of them. That list is the token’s embedding: a point in a high-dimensional space. A 100,000-token vocabulary with 4,096 dimensions means a lookup table holding roughly 400 million learned numbers.

      Those numbers start out random. Training nudges them so that tokens which behave similarly end up pointing in similar directions. That is the whole trick: meaning is encoded as position. Nothing in the model knows what a dog is; it knows that the vector for dog sits near puppy, cat, and bark, and far from mortgage.

      To measure “near,” the standard tool is cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between two vectors. Same direction scores 1, right angles score 0, opposite directions score −1. Cosine ignores length and looks only at direction — which is what you want, since magnitude mostly reflects frequency rather than meaning.

      Token embeddings are not the embeddings your search index uses

      This distinction trips people up, and Chapter 4 depends on it. The embeddings above live inside the model, one per token, and they are only a starting point: the layers above rewrite each token’s vector using context, which is how one bank embedding can mean riverbank in one sentence and lender in another. A retrieval system uses something else — a sentence or document embedding, one vector for a whole chunk of text, produced by a separate model whose only job is to place similar passages near each other.

      3. Next-token prediction is the whole engine

      Here is the complete job description. The model takes a sequence of tokens. It pushes those vectors through many layers of arithmetic. At the end it takes the vector sitting at the last position, multiplies it by one final matrix, and gets one raw score — a logit — for every single entry in the vocabulary. All 100,000 of them. A function called softmax exponentiates those scores and divides by their sum, turning them into a probability distribution: every value between 0 and 1, all of them summing to 1.

      That distribution is the model’s entire output. Not a sentence. Not an answer. One probability per possible next token. A sampler then picks one token from that distribution, the chosen token is appended to the sequence, and the whole thing runs again on the longer sequence. This loop is called autoregression, and it is why responses stream out one piece at a time.

      The idea in one line. There is no plan, no goal, and no lookahead. The model does not decide what the paragraph will say and then write it. Fluency, structure, and apparent intent are all side effects of a very good conditional probability distribution applied one token at a time.

      Hold onto that, because it explains most of the sheet. Hallucination is what happens when the most probable continuation is not the true one. Prompt engineering works because the prompt is the condition in the conditional distribution. Chain-of-thought helps because intermediate tokens become context for the next prediction — the model is thinking out loud into its own input.

      4. Transformers and attention

      This is the section that ends interviews when a candidate freezes. Go slowly; it is not actually hard.

      Why the architecture exists

      Before 2017, sequence models were mostly recurrent neural networks. An RNN reads token one, updates a hidden state, reads token two, updates the state, and so on. Two problems. It is inherently serial — you cannot compute step 500 before step 499, so a GPU’s advantage of doing thousands of things at once is wasted. And information from token 3 reaches token 400 only by surviving 397 rounds of being squeezed through one fixed-size state, which it usually does not.

      The transformer removes the recurrence. Every token computes its relationship to every other token in one parallel step. Long-range connections become direct instead of relayed, and the layer becomes one big matrix multiplication, which is what GPUs do best. That is the real reason transformers won: not smarter per parameter, but they made training on internet-scale data economically possible.

      What attention actually computes

      Take the sentence “The cat drank the milk because it was thirsty.” To process it correctly, the model needs to pull in information from cat. Attention is the mechanism that does that, and here is the arithmetic.

      Every token’s vector gets multiplied by three separate learned matrices, producing three new vectors:

      • a query (Q) — what this token is looking for;
      • a key (K) — what this token offers to anyone looking;
      • a value (V) — the content this token will hand over if selected.

      Now, for the token it: take its query vector and compute the dot product with the key vector of every token in the sequence, including itself. A dot product is one number, large when two vectors point in similar directions, so each score answers “how relevant is that token to what I am looking for?” The raw scores are divided by the square root of the vector dimension (which keeps them from growing large enough to saturate the next step) and passed through softmax, producing attention weights that are all positive and sum to exactly 1. Finally, multiply each token’s value vector by its weight and add them up. That weighted sum is the output for it — a new vector now mostly made of cat.

      Now the analogy, and only now. A hard lookup table takes a key and returns exactly one value. Attention takes a query, scores every key, and returns a mixture of all the values weighted by relevance. It is a soft lookup table whose matching rules were learned rather than written. Mechanism first, then that line — saying only the line is what gets a candidate marked down.

      Many heads, many layers

      One attention operation can express only one notion of relevance, so transformers run several in parallel — typically 8 to 128 — each with its own Q, K, and V matrices. This is multi-head attention. Because each head starts from different random values and gets its own gradients, heads specialize: researchers have found heads that track syntactic dependencies, heads that resolve pronouns, heads that just look at the previous token, and heads nobody has cleanly characterized. Their outputs are concatenated and mixed by another learned matrix.

      That whole assembly — multi-head attention, plus a small feed-forward network applied to each position independently, plus normalization and residual connections that let the original vector pass through alongside the update — is one transformer block. A large model stacks dozens to well over a hundred of them. Depth produces sophistication: layer 2 can only relate raw tokens, but layer 40 relates representations that already encode what layers 1 through 39 found. Composition, not magic.

      Causal masking, and why generation only goes forward

      In a generative model, position 5 is forbidden from attending to positions 6 and beyond, enforced with a causal mask: before softmax, scores for future positions are set to negative infinity, so their weights come out exactly zero. Without it, training would be useless — the model would predict token 6 by looking at token 6. The mask is what makes “predict the next token” a real problem, and why autoregression runs left to right.

      The quadratic cost, and why it shapes your roadmap

      With n tokens, attention computes an n × n grid of scores per head per layer. Ten times the context is roughly a hundred times the attention arithmetic. Engineers have softened this a great deal — smarter memory layouts, sparse and sliding-window patterns, sharing keys across heads — and windows have grown from a couple of thousand tokens to a million in a few years. But the scaling has not been repealed. It is why long context costs more and answers slower, why “put the whole database in the prompt” is not a strategy, and why retrieval exists.

      The idea in one line. A transformer is a tall stack of blocks; each block lets every token gather a weighted blend of information from every earlier token, using relevance scores it learned. Attention is that gathering step, expressed as query · key → softmax → weighted sum of values.

      5. Weights, pre-training, and post-training

      Every matrix mentioned so far — the embedding table, the Q, K, and V projections in every head of every layer, the feed-forward networks, the final output matrix — is made of weights. Weights are just numbers. A model described as “70 billion parameters” has 70 billion of them. Training adjusts those numbers; everything the model can do is stored in their values and nowhere else.

      Common misconception, and the important one. “The model learned from our conversation.” It did not. During a conversation the weights are read-only — literally the same file, serving thousands of users at once. The illusion of learning comes from your earlier messages still sitting in the context window and being re-read on every turn. Start a new chat and it is gone. If a product genuinely remembers you, that is a database plus retrieval bolted on the outside, not a changed model.

      Weights change only in training, which comes in two phases. Knowing which phase owns a problem is one of the genuinely useful things a PM can hold in their head.

      Pre-training is next-token prediction over an enormous corpus of text and code. No labels, no instructions, no human feedback — just “predict what comes next” trillions of times. This is where capability comes from: grammar, world knowledge, code, translation, latent reasoning. It is also where nearly all of the cost sits — months on very large GPU clusters, budgets in the tens or hundreds of millions of dollars at the frontier. A raw pre-trained model is a spectacular text continuer and a terrible assistant: ask it a question and it may produce five more questions, because that is a plausible continuation.

      Post-training turns that into something usable. First, instruction tuning (also called supervised fine-tuning, or SFT): train on curated instructions paired with good responses, so the model learns that a question should be followed by an answer. Then preference-based methods: collect judgments about which of two responses is better and use them to push the model toward the preferred behavior. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is the classic implementation, and there is now a family of relatives — direct preference optimization, AI feedback against a written constitution, and others. The specific method is a detail. The category is what matters: post-training installs helpfulness, tone, format-following, refusals, and safety behavior, and it is a rounding error on the pre-training bill.

      6. Why an LLM is not a database you query

      A database stores records. Each row exists somewhere, byte for byte. You can point at it, count it, prove it is there, update it, and delete it. Retrieval is exact by construction.

      A model stores weights. During pre-training, far more text than the model itself is pushed through a fixed number of parameters. That is lossy compression, and it is statistical: the weights end up encoding regularities and associations, not copies. When you ask a question, nothing is looked up. The forward pass reconstructs a plausible answer, one token at a time. When the patterns are strong and consistent — the capital of France appeared identically in a million documents — the reconstruction is reliable. When they are thin or conflicting — a citation, a niche date, your company’s internal policy — the reconstruction is still fluent, because fluency comes from the same machinery. It is just wrong.

      Common misconception. “It knows the answer, it just needs a better prompt.” Sometimes true, often not. If a fact was rare in training, changed after the training cutoff, or lives only in your private systems, there is nothing in the weights to surface and no wording will conjure it. That is an architectural gap, and the fix is to put the fact in the context window via retrieval or a tool call.

      Two later chapters exist because of this. Chapter 3 covers hallucination, now unsurprising: a reconstruction engine with no notion of “no record found” produces something plausible rather than nothing. Chapter 4 covers retrieval-augmented generation — keep facts in a real database, fetch the relevant ones at query time, and put them in the prompt so the model is reading rather than remembering.

      The idea in one line. A database retrieves. A model reconstructs. Every difference that matters — provenance, deletability, guaranteed recall, reproducibility — follows from that one distinction.

      “How does an LLM actually work?”

      Text is split into tokens, each token becomes a vector, a stack of transformer layers lets every token gather information from every earlier token, and the model outputs a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary for the next token. It samples one, appends it, and repeats.

      If they want more, walk the pipeline in order: tokenizer to ids; ids to embeddings via a learned lookup table; dozens of transformer blocks, each containing multi-head attention plus a per-position feed-forward network, with residual connections and normalization; then a final projection from the last position’s vector to one logit per vocabulary entry; softmax to probabilities; sample; loop. Then name the one thing that makes it coherent: the model is not executing a plan, it is repeatedly answering “what token is most likely next, given everything so far.” All the weights are fixed during this — they were set in pre-training and post-training and do not move while you talk to it.

      Going deeper: saying what the model outputs (a distribution over the entire vocabulary, not a sentence) and naming where the cost lives (attention is quadratic in sequence length, generation is serial). That signals you understand the mechanism, not just the vocabulary.

      “What is next-token prediction?”

      The model reads a sequence and produces a probability for every possible next token. One is sampled, appended to the sequence, and the process repeats — that loop is autoregression, and it is the model’s only real operation.

      Two details worth adding. First, the output is a full distribution over a vocabulary of tens or hundreds of thousands of entries, produced by softmax over raw logits, not a single guess — which is what makes sampling controls like temperature meaningful. Second, there is no lookahead: the model does not choose the ending of a sentence before writing its beginning. Coherence emerges because each token is conditioned on all previous tokens, including the ones the model just wrote. That single fact explains a surprising amount: why prompt wording matters so much (the prompt is the condition), why chain-of-thought helps (intermediate tokens become context for later predictions), and why long generations drift.

      Going deeper: explicitly killing the intentionality framing. “It has no plan, no goal, and no representation of the answer it is heading toward — fluency is a side effect of a very good conditional distribution.” Then note the cost consequence: output tokens are serial, so they are slower and priced higher than input tokens.

      “Explain how transformers work”

      A transformer is a deep stack of identical blocks. Each block runs multi-head attention, which lets every token gather a weighted blend of information from every other token in one parallel step, followed by a small feed-forward network applied to each position, wrapped in residual connections and normalization.

      Give the motivation, because it shows you know why the design exists. RNNs processed tokens strictly in order, so they could not be parallelized on GPUs, and long-range information had to survive hundreds of sequential updates through one fixed-size state. Attention replaces that with direct, all-to-all connections computed as one large matrix multiplication — which is what made training on internet-scale data affordable. Then add depth: early layers relate raw tokens, later layers relate representations that already encode earlier relationships, so sophistication comes from composition across layers. In a generative model a causal mask zeroes out attention to future positions, which is what makes next-token prediction a non-trivial task and forces generation to run left to right.

      Going deeper: naming the cost. The attention grid is n × n per head per layer, so context is quadratic; there is also a key-value cache that grows linearly with sequence length. This is why context windows are finite and expensive, and it connects the architecture directly to your product’s unit economics.

      “What is attention?”

      Every token produces a query, a key, and a value vector. A token’s query is dot-producted with every key to get relevance scores; softmax turns those into weights that sum to 1; the output is the weighted sum of the value vectors. It is a soft, learned lookup table.

      Make it concrete. In “The cat drank the milk because it was thirsty,” the token it issues a query. Its dot product with the key of cat is large, with the is small. After scaling by the square root of the head dimension and applying softmax, most of the weight lands on cat, so the new vector for it is mostly built out of cat’s value vector. That is coreference resolved by arithmetic. Multi-head attention runs many of these in parallel with separate learned projections, so different heads can specialize in different relationships; stacking dozens of layers lets later heads operate on relationships that earlier ones already found.

      Going deeper: giving the mechanism before the metaphor, and knowing why it is soft. A hard lookup returns one value and is not differentiable; a weighted blend of all values is, which is the only reason the matching rules can be learned by gradient descent at all.

      “What are tokens and why do they matter?”

      Tokens are the subword chunks a tokenizer splits text into — the only units the model ever sees. They matter because they are the unit of billing, the unit of the context limit, and the reason the model is weak at character-level and digit-level work.

      The vocabulary is fixed before training and typically holds tens of thousands to a couple hundred thousand entries, chosen so that frequent byte sequences get their own token. As a planning rule of thumb, English prose runs about four characters per token, so 1,000 tokens is roughly 750 words — treat that as an order of magnitude, not a fact. Four product consequences: pricing and context are token-denominated, with output priced well above input; non-English text fragments into more tokens for the same meaning, so your margins differ by market; letter-level tasks such as counting characters or reversing strings are fighting the representation; and long numbers split in ways that ignore place value, so arithmetic should be delegated to a tool rather than prompted harder.

      Going deeper: connecting tokenization to a real decision. For example: a multilingual launch changes both cost per request and effective context length, so the same feature can be profitable in English and unprofitable in Thai — and you should measure that before pricing, not after.

      “What are embeddings?”

      An embedding is a vector of numbers representing a piece of text, learned so that similar things sit close together in the space. Meaning becomes geometry, and similarity becomes a measurable angle — usually cosine similarity.

      Then draw the distinction that matters in practice. Token embeddings live inside the model: each token id indexes a row of a learned matrix, and that vector is the input to the first transformer layer. It starts out context-free, and the layers above rewrite it using attention, which is how one bank token can end up meaning riverbank in one sentence and lender in another. Sentence or document embeddings are a different thing entirely: one vector for a whole passage, produced by a separate and usually much smaller model, stored in a vector database, and compared by cosine similarity to answer “which chunks are most like this query.” That is the retrieval half of a RAG system, and it does not require the generating model at all.

      Going deeper: being unambiguous about which kind you mean, and noting that cosine similarity measures direction and deliberately ignores magnitude. Bonus: semantic similarity is not the same as relevance or correctness, which is exactly why naive vector search retrieves passages that look related and answer nothing.

      “What are model weights and when do they change?”

      Weights are the billions of learned numbers that make up the model — the embedding table, the query, key and value projections in every head, the feed-forward layers, the output matrix. They change only during training or fine-tuning. They never change during a conversation.

      Everything the model can do is stored in those values and nowhere else. At inference the weights are read-only, and the same file is serving many users simultaneously. What people mistake for learning is context: your earlier turns are still in the context window and are re-read on every request, so the model appears to remember. Open a new chat and it is gone. Products that genuinely remember a user implement that with external storage plus retrieval — a database write and a later read — not by modifying the model. Changing weights means a deliberate offline job: full pre-training, continued pre-training, or fine-tuning on a curated dataset, all of which cost real money and need evaluation before and after.

      Going deeper: stating the security and compliance implication out loud. “Sending data in a prompt does not train the model by default — whether your data is retained or used for training is a contractual and configuration question about the provider, not a property of the architecture.” That is the version of this answer a legal or enterprise stakeholder needs.

      “What is the difference between pre-training and post-training?”

      Pre-training is next-token prediction over a vast corpus and produces raw capability at enormous cost. Post-training is instruction tuning plus preference-based methods, and produces helpfulness, format-following, tone, and refusals. Pre-training builds the engine; post-training builds the car around it.

      Pre-training uses no labels and no human feedback, just prediction repeated trillions of times, and it is where grammar, world knowledge, code, translation, and latent reasoning come from. It also carries nearly all of the compute bill — months on large clusters. The output is a model that continues documents beautifully and takes instructions badly. Post-training fixes that in two stages: supervised fine-tuning on curated instruction-and-response pairs, then preference optimization, where human or AI judgments about which response is better are used to shift behavior. RLHF is the classic recipe; direct preference optimization and constitution-style AI feedback are relatives. The exact algorithm is a detail. The category is the point.

      Going deeper: using it as a triage rule. Most behavior a PM complains about — verbosity, over-refusal, ignoring the output schema, wrong tone — is post-training, and is often addressable with prompting or fine-tuning. Most capability a PM wants — can it reason about this domain at all — is pre-training, and fine-tuning will not install a capability the base model never had.

      “Why isn’t an LLM a database you query?”

      Because a database retrieves and a model reconstructs. Weights are a lossy, statistical compression of the training data, not a set of records, so there is no row to look up, no pointer to a source, and no guarantee of recall.

      Far more text goes into pre-training than the model has parameters to hold, so what survives is regularities and associations rather than copies. A forward pass regenerates a plausible answer from those patterns token by token. When a fact was frequent and consistent, the reconstruction is reliable; when it was rare, contested, or absent, the output is equally fluent and possibly false, because fluency and accuracy come from the same machinery. Practical consequences: no provenance, so you cannot cite; no “record not found” error, so failure looks like confidence; no clean way to update or delete a single fact, which is a real problem under data-deletion obligations; and no guarantee of identical output for identical input once sampling is involved.

      Going deeper: going straight to the architectural fix. If you need provenance, freshness, access control, or auditable deletion, keep the facts in a system that has those properties and put the relevant ones in the context window at query time. That is retrieval-augmented generation, and framing it as “a database for facts, the model for language” is what a senior candidate says.

      The model does not choose a word

      Chapter 1 ended in a specific place. The forward pass finished, and what came out was not a word. It was a logit vector — one raw, unbounded score for every token in the vocabulary, typically 50,000 to 200,000 of them. Softmax turned those scores into a probability distribution: every number between 0 and 1, all of them summing to 1.

      That is the entire output of the model. It is a ranked opinion about what could come next. It is not a decision.

      Something has to convert that distribution into one concrete token to append to the text. That something is the sampler, and the rules it follows are called the decoding strategy. The sampler is a few lines of ordinary code that sit outside the neural network. It has no weights. It learned nothing. It reads the probabilities and returns an index.

      Keeping that boundary clear is the single thing that makes the temperature question answerable properly. Temperature is not a setting inside the model’s reasoning. It is a parameter of the code that picks.

      The idea in one line. The model produces a distribution; the decoder produces a token. Everything you tune with temperature, top_p, or top_k lives in the decoder, not in the model.

      Temperature, mechanically

      Temperature is a single number that divides the logits before softmax runs. That is the whole mechanism. If the raw scores are z and the temperature is T, the model computes softmax(z / T) instead of softmax(z).

      Dividing by a number smaller than 1 makes every score bigger, and it makes the gaps between scores bigger too. Softmax exponentiates, so bigger gaps become dramatically bigger probability ratios. The leader runs away with everything. Dividing by a number larger than 1 squeezes the scores toward each other, the gaps shrink, and the distribution slides toward uniform — toward every token being equally likely.

      Take a concrete case. The prompt is “The capital of France is” and suppose only four tokens have meaningful scores, with logits 3.0, 1.0, 0.5, and 0.0.

      And this four-token example understates the effect badly. A real vocabulary has on the order of 100,000 tokens, and almost all of them are nonsense in any given position. Flattening the distribution moves probability mass out of the handful of sensible tokens and into that enormous tail of garbage. That is why high temperature does not produce interesting writing; it produces text that falls apart.

      Common misconception. Temperature is not a creativity dial, a confidence dial, a correctness dial, or an effort dial. It controls exactly one thing: how flat the probability distribution is before a token is drawn from it. Everything people attribute to it — more varied phrasing, more hallucination, more incoherence — is a downstream consequence of that flatness. A model given a low temperature does not “think harder”; it just stops taking chances on its own second-choice tokens.

      Zero, two, and the other knob

      At T = 0 the formula would divide by zero, so implementations special-case it: they skip sampling entirely and take the argmax, the single highest-probability token, every step. This is called greedy decoding. Set T = 0 and you have asked for the model’s most likely continuation and nothing else. Run it twice on the same input and you should get the same text. You often will. You are not guaranteed to, for reasons in the next section that have nothing to do with sampling.

      At T = 2 you are at the top of the range most APIs expose, and the distribution is flat enough that the model’s ranking barely constrains the output. In practice the text starts well, drifts, and then degrades — invented words, broken syntax, abandoned sentences. Once one low-probability token lands, it is in the context, and every subsequent token is now conditioned on nonsense. Errors compound because the loop feeds its own output back in.

      Temperature is not the only knob, and knowing the second one is a cheap way to sound like you have actually shipped something. Top-p, also called nucleus sampling, truncates the tail instead of reshaping the whole distribution. Sort the tokens by probability, walk down the list accumulating probability, stop as soon as the running total reaches p, throw away everything below that cut, renormalize what is left, and sample from it. In our T = 1.0 example, top-p = 0.9 keeps Paris, Lyon, and Nice (cumulative 96%) and deletes “Tokyo” outright — probability zero, not low probability. Top-k is the blunter cousin: keep the k highest-scoring tokens regardless of what their probabilities are.

      The practical difference: temperature changes how much the model’s preferences matter, while top-p sets a floor on how bad a token is allowed to be. They compose, which is why tuning both at once is a good way to confuse yourself. Change one, hold the other at its default.

      Where on the range each kind of task belongs. Values are starting points to be measured against your own evaluation set, not settings to memorize.
      Task Range Why
      Extraction, classification, routing, structured output 0 to 0.2 There is one right answer. Variation is pure downside, and reproducibility makes debugging possible.
      Summarization, rewriting, code generation 0.2 to 0.6 Slight variation is harmless and sometimes escapes a bad phrasing, but the content must stay faithful.
      Ideation, name generation, drafting several options 0.7 to 1.0 Variety is the product. Generating five samples and picking one requires that the five actually differ.
      Anything at all 1.5 to 2.0 Effectively nothing. Reach for it only to demonstrate degradation, or to sample a deliberately weird distribution for research.

      Why the same prompt gives you a different answer

      Most candidates answer this with “sampling is random” and stop. That is one cause of five, and it is the only one you can fix from your side by changing a parameter. Naming the others is what signals that you have debugged a production system.

      1. Sampling randomness. Above T = 0 the sampler draws from a distribution. Different draw, different token, and one different token early on sends the whole continuation somewhere else. Some providers accept a seed parameter to fix the random draw, which helps and still does not fully close the gap.

      2. Floating-point arithmetic and batching — the real reason T = 0 is not a promise. Computers add floating-point numbers non-associatively: (a + b) + c can differ from a + (b + c) in the last bits, because each intermediate result gets rounded. A GPU computes a sum like a matrix multiplication by splitting it across thousands of threads and combining partial results, and the order in which those partials combine is not fixed. Worse, the provider batches your request with whatever other requests arrived at the same moment, and batch size determines which kernel implementation and which split strategy get used. Same weights, same prompt, different neighbors in the batch, bit-level different logits. Usually irrelevant. But when the top two tokens are nearly tied, a difference in the twelfth decimal place flips the argmax, and greedy decoding dutifully walks down a different path.

      3. The model changed under you. If you call an alias like -latest, the snapshot behind it can be replaced. Providers also update system prompts, safety layers, and serving stacks without a version bump you can see. Your input was identical; the thing you sent it to was not.

      4. Infrastructure-level variation. Large models are increasingly mixture-of-experts: instead of every token passing through every parameter, a router sends each token to a small subset of specialized sub-networks. Under batched serving, experts have finite capacity per batch, so which expert a token actually reaches can depend on the other requests sharing that batch. Speculative decoding, quantized kernels, and heterogeneous hardware in the same fleet add more of the same flavor of variation.

      5. Your prompt was not actually identical. The most common cause in real products, and the most embarrassing. A timestamp in the system prompt. A retrieval step that returned the same documents in a different order. A conversation history one turn longer. A tool result with a new request ID. You compared what you typed, not what was sent.

      The idea in one line. Model output is a sample from a distribution served by non-deterministic infrastructure, so you cannot write assert output == "expected string" and expect it to hold. Evaluation has to test properties — does it parse, does it contain the right entity, does a judge rate it acceptable — across many runs. Chapter 7 builds that out.

      The context window is a desk, not a memory

      The context window is the maximum number of tokens the model can process in a single forward pass. It is a hard architectural limit, expressed in tokens, and it is shared by everything involved in the call:

      • the system prompt, including every instruction and policy you wrote;
      • the definitions of any tools the model can call — names, descriptions, and full argument schemas, which get large fast;
      • the entire conversation history you chose to resend;
      • any retrieved documents or file contents you pasted in;
      • the user’s actual message;
      • and the space the output needs, because generated tokens are appended to the same sequence.

      That last item catches people. If you fill 127,000 tokens of a 128,000-token window with input, the model has 1,000 tokens left to answer in. Budget the output explicitly.

      Now the part that reframes how you think about products built on these models. The API is stateless. The model retains nothing between calls. It has no memory of your last message. When a chat product appears to remember the conversation, what is happening is that the client resends the whole transcript every turn. The model reads it fresh, as if for the first time, because it is the first time.

      So the desk analogy earns its place only after the mechanism: everything the model can use has to be laid out on the desk before the forward pass, and the desk is wiped clean afterward. “The model forgot what I told it” is, nine times out of ten, “your application stopped sending that part.”

      It also explains a cost curve that surprises teams. If every turn resends the full history, a 20-turn conversation does not cost 20 units — turn 20 pays for turns 1 through 19 all over again. Total input tokens grow with the square of the number of turns.

      Overflowing, and the tax on long windows

      What happens when your request exceeds the window depends entirely on which layer notices first, and the three behaviors are dangerously different.

      A hard error. Raw API calls reject the request outright, with a message along the lines of “maximum context length exceeded.” This is the good case. It is loud, and you find it in testing.

      Silent truncation. Many chat interfaces and SDK helpers quietly drop the oldest messages to make room. The call succeeds. The answer looks plausible. The constraint the user stated in turn two is simply no longer in the request, and nothing in the response tells you.

      The middle disappears. Some frameworks and routers keep the start and the end and remove content from the middle, on the reasoning that the system prompt and the recent turns matter most. Often correct, occasionally catastrophic, and almost never surfaced in a log you read.

      Common misconception. Hitting the context limit is not reliably an error. Silent truncation is a normal, documented behavior of several popular layers, and it is the reason a bug report saying “the assistant ignored my requirement” should send you to look at the request payload before you look at the model.

      The mitigations, and what each one costs:

      • Sliding window. Keep the last N turns. Cheap, trivial to implement, and it throws away exactly the early turns where users tend to state constraints.
      • Summarization or compaction. Periodically replace old turns with a model-written summary. Preserves the gist within a much smaller budget, but it costs an extra model call, it is lossy in ways you cannot predict, and summaries of summaries drift further from the original with every cycle.
      • Retrieval instead of stuffing. Keep the corpus outside the window and fetch only the relevant pieces per turn. Scales to unbounded knowledge, and buys you a whole new subsystem that can fail by fetching the wrong thing. Chapter 5 is about that trade.

      Even when everything fits, position inside the window matters. Models attend more reliably to content near the beginning and near the end of a long context than to content buried in the middle. Plot accuracy at finding a planted fact against where that fact sits, and you get a U-shape. The effect is known as lost in the middle. It has been reproduced across many models and architectures, its severity varies by model and by task, and no one should claim it is fully solved.

      Two consequences follow. First, a bigger window is not a free win: a million-token context does not mean a million usable tokens, and stuffing everything in because it fits will make retrieval quality worse, not better. Second, the fix is often just ordering — put instructions at the top, the most important retrieved document at the bottom nearest the question, and repeat any hard constraint at the end.

      Then there is the bill. Chapter 1 showed that attention compares every token to every other token, so its compute grows with the square of the sequence length. Doubling the context roughly quadruples the attention work needed to read it. Providers charge per token, not per unit of compute, so the price of input scales roughly linearly — but the compute shows up as latency, especially in time-to-first-token while the model reads your prompt, and it shows up in the premium pricing tiers that long-context models tend to carry.

      The idea in one line. “Just use the million-token model” is a budget decision with three separate costs — money per call, latency per call, and degraded attention to the middle. Deciding what earns a place in the window is the actual work, and Chapter 4 is about how to do it.

      “What does temperature actually control?”

      Temperature divides the logits before softmax, which changes how flat the next-token probability distribution is. It is a parameter of the sampler, not of the model.

      The model’s forward pass produces a logit — a raw score — for every token in the vocabulary, and softmax converts those into probabilities. Temperature T makes it softmax(z / T). Below 1, the gaps between scores widen, softmax exponentiates them, and the top token takes almost all the mass. Above 1, the gaps compress and the distribution slides toward uniform. With logits of 3.0, 1.0, 0.5, and 0.0, the top token holds about 79% at T = 1.0, effectively 100% at T = 0.2, and only about 53% at T = 2.0. The model produced identical logits in all three cases.

      Going deeper: refusing the framing that temperature is a creativity or confidence dial, and naming the second knob. Top-p truncates the tail — keep the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability reaches p and delete the rest — so it sets a floor on how bad a token may be, where temperature rescales how much the model’s ranking counts at all.

      “What is the difference between temperature 0 and temperature 2?”

      T = 0 is greedy decoding: always take the highest-probability token, so output is near-deterministic. T = 2 flattens the distribution so far that low-probability tokens get drawn and the output degrades into incoherence.

      Zero is a special case in the code, since you cannot divide by it — implementations skip sampling and take the argmax each step. Two is typically the top of the exposed range. The reason it breaks down is scale: a real vocabulary has around 100,000 tokens, nearly all of them nonsense at any given position, so flattening moves mass out of the few sensible tokens into that tail. And because each generated token is fed back into the context, one bad draw conditions everything after it, so errors compound rather than average out. In practice: 0 to 0.2 for extraction, classification, and routing, where variation is pure downside; 0.7 to 1.0 when you want genuinely different drafts to choose between; nothing real at 2.

      Going deeper: saying “near-deterministic” rather than “deterministic” for T = 0, and being able to explain why — floating-point non-associativity plus variable request batching on the provider side can shift the logits in the last bits, which flips the argmax whenever the top two tokens are nearly tied.

      “Why do two identical prompts give different answers?”

      Five reasons, and only the first is sampling. Random draws above T = 0, floating-point and batching non-determinism in the serving stack, a silently changed model or system prompt, infrastructure variation such as mixture-of-experts routing, and prompts that were not actually identical.

      Sampling randomness is the obvious one and the only one a parameter fixes. The deeper one is arithmetic: GPUs sum in non-deterministic thread order, floating-point addition is not associative, and the provider batches your request with other traffic, which changes which kernel runs. Identical input, bit-level different logits. Third, a -latest alias or an updated safety layer means the system you called changed. Fourth, mixture-of-experts models route tokens to sub-networks with per-batch capacity limits, so your batch neighbors can influence your result. Fifth and most common in real products: a timestamp, a reordered retrieval result, or one extra history turn means you compared what you typed, not what you sent.

      Going deeper: the product consequence. You cannot write an equality assertion against model output, so tests have to assert properties — valid schema, required entity present, a rubric or judge score above threshold — measured across repeated runs rather than once.

      “What is a context window and what happens when you exceed it?”

      The context window is the total token budget for one forward pass, shared by the system prompt, tool definitions, history, retrieved documents, the user message, and the output. Exceed it and you get either a hard API error or, worse, silent truncation.

      The critical point is that the API is stateless: the model remembers nothing between calls, so a conversation only persists because the client resends the entire transcript each turn. That is also why long conversations get expensive — total input tokens grow with the square of the turn count. On overflow, behavior depends on the layer. A raw API call errors out, which is the good case because it is loud. Chat interfaces and SDK helpers often drop the oldest messages, so the call succeeds and a constraint from turn two is quietly gone. Some frameworks remove the middle instead. Mitigations are a sliding window (cheap, loses early constraints), summarization or compaction (compact, lossy, costs an extra call, drifts when repeated), or retrieval (scales, adds a subsystem that can fetch the wrong thing).

      Going deeper: adding that a bigger window is not a free upgrade. Models recall content at the start and end of a long context more reliably than the middle, so position is a design variable, and because attention cost grows with the square of sequence length, long contexts are slower and more expensive. Choosing what earns a place in the window beats buying more window.

      Two different failures wearing one name

      A hallucination is output that is fluent, confident, and not supported. Every word is load-bearing. Fluent, because it reads exactly like the true sentences around it. Confident, because the model gives you no signal that this sentence differs from any other. Not supported, because nothing stands behind the claim.

      That last phrase splits into two axes, and sloppy answers mash them together. Keeping them apart is most of what the OpenAI “define hallucinations” question is probing. Factuality asks: is the claim true about the world? Faithfulness — also called grounding — asks something narrower: is it supported by the material I put in the prompt? The two are independent. A summary can be factual yet unfaithful, when the model adds a true fact that was never in the document. It can be faithful yet false, when the document was wrong and the model copied it honestly.

      Why care about a distinction this fine? The axes cost different amounts to check. “Is this true about the world?” needs an oracle you do not have. “Is this supported by the documents I retrieved?” compares two strings you already hold, so a machine can do it on every response.

      The idea in one line. Good product design converts factuality problems into faithfulness problems, because faithfulness is the one you can actually measure.

      Why it happens, mechanically

      From Chapter 1: the model computes a probability distribution over the next token, given everything before it, and samples from it. Repeat a few hundred times and you have an answer. Four properties of that loop produce hallucination.

      One: there is no truth check in it. The computation that produces the next token does not consult a database, and no step compares the sentence so far against the world. The model optimizes for plausibility — how typical this continuation is. Plausible and true overlap heavily, which is why any of this works, but they are not the same quantity, and nothing in the architecture prefers the second.

      Two: there is no abstain action. “I don’t know” is not a special move. It is another string, competing on plausibility against the answer-shaped string. In the training corpus a question is almost always followed by an answer; text where someone asks for a case citation and gets a polite refusal is rare. The answer wins on the statistics before your instructions are considered.

      Three: the confidence signal you want does not exist. The model does produce a probability for every token, and it is tempting to read a high one as certainty. That number says the continuation is typical, not correct. Worse, most output lives in the high-confidence region, so that is where most wrong answers live in absolute terms. Confident-and-wrong is not separable from confident-and-right by probability alone.

      Four: training rewards answering over abstaining. Post-training and almost every public benchmark score whether the answer was right. An abstention scores zero; a guess scores more than zero in expectation. So the pressure is “always produce something.” It is a multiple-choice exam with no penalty for wrong answers: leaving a question blank is strictly dominated, and the model learned that.

      One more piece from Chapter 1 tells you where it breaks. The weights are a lossy compression of the corpus, not an index. Facts repeated ten thousand times survive compression; facts seen once or twice do not — they are stored as a blurry region of plausible-shaped answers. So the failure rate is not uniform. It spikes on rare, precise specifics: citations, case numbers, dosages, dates, API parameter names, any figure with a decimal point.

      Common misconception. Hallucination is not noise sprinkled evenly over the output. It is a systematic failure with a predictable shape — the rarer and more precise the fact, the higher the rate. That tells you where to build your eval set.

      Why a longer, sterner prompt cannot fix it

      The favorite follow-up is a version of “so why not just tell it not to?” The answer is no, for three reasons.

      First, “do not hallucinate” is not a followable instruction. Obeying it would require detecting which of its own claims are unsupported — precisely the capability it lacks. An instruction can direct behavior the model can perform; this one names an outcome. It is like telling a person “do not misremember.”

      Second, it adds no information. If the fact is not in the context and not reliably in the weights, no wording conjures it.

      Third, force has side effects. Capitals and threats do shift the distribution — usually toward hedging and refusal. Fewer wrong answers, more useless ones. Sometimes the right trade, but be honest it is one: you moved failures into a category where users leave silently.

      Prompt move What it actually changes What it still does not fix

      What actually works: a layered defense

      Here is the shape of a strong answer to the deployed-model question. You do not remove hallucination; you engineer two numbers down, the rate and the cost of one occurrence. Every layer below does one or the other, and every layer leaks.

      Reduce the need to recall. Retrieve the relevant documents into the context, so facts come from a source rather than from compressed memory. This is the largest lever, and it is Chapter 4. It leaks twice: retrieval can fetch the wrong document, and the model can still drift from what it retrieved.

      Constrain the task. A model choosing among six enumerated labels cannot invent a seventh if you validate the output. Fixed fields beat free prose. Ask for less, get less invention.

      Make it verifiable. Require every claim to carry a quoted span from the source, then check in code that the quoted string appears there. That is a string match — deterministic, cheap, no model involved — and it eliminates the fabricated-citation class outright. What leaks: a real quote paired with an inference it does not support.

      Verify after generation. A second pass whose only job is judging each claim against the source. A schema validator. A lookup confirming the product ID exists. And for anything that must be exact — pricing, dosage, eligibility, tax — a rules engine or database query, because a language model should never be the thing computing a number someone relies on. What leaks: your judge is also a model, and one from the same family shares the generator’s blind spots.

      Give it an exit. An explicit abstain path the product understands — “I could not find this in your documents” plus a search box — and a route to a human. That converts a wrong answer into a slow one, nearly always the cheaper failure.

      Design the interface for it. Sources inline beside the claim they support. Honest hedging where confidence is low. Output editable by default. Generated text never written into a record of authority without a human action. None of this lowers the rate; all of it lowers the damage and recruits the user as your last verifier.

      Measure and contain. A faithfulness metric on a real eval set, watched every release (Chapter 7). Then a deliberate choice about where the feature lives: the same model at the same rate is fine drafting a reply a human reads before sending, and unacceptable auto-approving a claim.

      The idea in one line. You do not ship a hallucination fix. You ship an error rate you have measured, at a cost per error you can absorb.

      Grounding, and how you actually measure it

      Grounding is the practice of constraining output to the sources you provide. A grounded claim is one a specific span of source text supports. Everything else — however true — is ungrounded, and in an auditable product that is a defect by itself.

      The measurement is claim-level, not response-level, because “was this answer good?” is not a question two annotators answer the same way. The recipe: take an eval set of real inputs with their sources, decompose each output into atomic claims, and label each claim supported, unsupported, or contradicted. Report the support rate, and report contradictions separately — a claim that fights the source is a different bug from one the source is silent about.

      Three ways to produce those labels, each with a failure mode. Human annotation is your ground truth: slow, expensive, and worthless without a written rubric, since without one two annotators disagree on half the borderline claims. An LLM as judge, given a rubric and required to cite the supporting span, scales to every release — but must be validated against human labels on a sample, and re-validated whenever you change models, because a judge from the generator’s own family shares its blind spots and rewards fluency. Entailment checks ask a small specialized model whether a source sentence entails the claim; fast and cheap, brittle on long contexts and arithmetic.

      Two more honesties. Decomposing text into claims is a judgment call, so fix the procedure and hold it constant. And a support rate hides severity: one contradicted dosage is not one-tenth as bad as ten unsupported adjectives. Segment by claim type, or weight it.

      Common misconception. “We improved grounding” with no eval set behind it means “we changed the prompt and it felt better.” No metric without an eval set, and the eval set is the real asset — it outlives the prompt, the model, and the vendor.

      Upstairs: error budgets and redlines

      Explaining it to an exec

      No mechanism, no jargon, and a number. Roughly this, in forty seconds:

      “The model is a very good guesser, not a lookup table. It always produces an answer, and it has no way to tell us when it is guessing. On our eval set of two hundred real customer questions, six percent of answers contain a claim our documents do not support. We have capped what that costs: every answer shows its sources, nothing reaches a customer without a human click, and pricing comes from the database rather than the model. We report that six percent every release. It will not reach zero.”

      Then the reframe that lands: this is reliability engineering with an error budget, like uptime. Nobody asks when hundred-percent uptime ships. They ask what the target is, what moving it costs, and what happens when we breach it. Ask for those three things here and the conversation becomes tractable.

      What not to say. “We’ll fix it in the prompt” promises a fix you cannot deliver and teaches leadership that prompting is a reliability lever. “The next model will fix it” hands your roadmap to a vendor and confuses a lower rate with a solved property. “It’s a known limitation of LLMs” is true and useless. And never quote a rate you cannot source.

      Redlines

      A redline is a category of output that is never acceptable at any quality bar. It is not a threshold on a metric you optimize; it is binary, and when it is violated the correct response is to block, degrade, or roll back the feature rather than tune it. Most of your surface is a curve you push. A small named set is a wall.

      A good redline is specific enough to write a test for, tied to a concrete harm rather than a feeling, owned by a named person, attached to a pre-agreed action on violation, and gated by an adversarial test suite written by someone whose job is to break it. Agree all of that before launch, while nobody is under shipping pressure.

      Two examples for an assistant answering clinician questions over patient records. First: it never states a medication dose that is not copied verbatim from the record. Test: three hundred adversarial prompts that invite a dose; any generated dose is a violation; the field renders as a source quote or renders blank. Second: it never asserts that a patient does not have a condition, since absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — negative claims route to “not found in the records provided.” And one for any retrieval product: no reference that fails to resolve to a real document reaches the user, and if the strip rate crosses a threshold the feature goes off.

What prompting genuinely does: it makes abstention an explicit, permitted move; it restricts the model to the provided text; it requires quotes, which gives you something a program can verify; and it constrains format so there is less room to invent. Notice that every one of those either makes the safe action available or produces an artifact you can check. Prompting is a steering tool, not a correctness mechanism.

      Going deeper: pointing out that raising refusals is a failure too, so “the stern prompt reduced hallucinations” may just mean you moved failures into a category nobody reports.

      “What is grounding and how do you measure it?”

      Grounding is constraining output to the sources you provided. You measure it claim by claim: decompose the output, check whether a source span supports each claim, and report a claim-level support rate on a labeled eval set.

      Concretely: assemble an eval set of real inputs with their source material, weighted toward the hard cases. Split each output into atomic claims. Label each one supported, unsupported, or contradicted, and report support rate plus contradiction rate separately, because a claim the source contradicts is a different bug from one the source is silent about. Labels come from three places: human annotation with a written rubric as ground truth; an LLM judge that must cite the supporting span, validated against human labels on a sample and re-validated whenever the model changes; and entailment-style checks from a small specialized model, which are cheap but brittle on long context and arithmetic. The judge’s failure mode matters: same-family judges share blind spots with the generator and reward fluency. And no metric without an eval set — the eval set outlives the prompt, the model, and the vendor.

      Going deeper: saying that a support rate hides severity, so you segment by claim type — one contradicted dosage is not one-tenth as bad as ten unsupported adjectives.

      “OpenAI: Define hallucinations in LLMs”

      A hallucination is output that is fluent and confident but not supported — either false about the world, or unsupported by the sources provided. Those are two separate axes: factuality and faithfulness.

      Factuality asks whether the claim is true about the world. Faithfulness, or grounding, asks whether the claim is supported by the material in the prompt. They are independent, and conflating them is the usual mistake. A summary can be factual and unfaithful, when the model adds a true fact that was never in the document — fine for a chat toy, a defect in an auditable product. A summary can be faithful and false, when the source itself was wrong and the model copied it honestly. The distinction is practical, not academic: faithfulness is cheap to check, because both strings are in front of you and a program can compare them, whereas factuality needs an oracle you do not have. So you measure them separately and you design the product to turn as much of the factuality problem as possible into a faithfulness problem. I would also note that confidence is part of the definition: the model presents an unsupported claim in exactly the same register as a supported one.

      Going deeper: giving the two axes with a concrete example in each off-diagonal quadrant, then explaining why the distinction changes what you build.

      “Anthropic: Handling hallucinations in a deployed AI model?”

      You do not eliminate it, you engineer it: a layered defense that lowers the rate, plus product decisions that lower the cost of each occurrence, plus a metric you watch every release.

      The stack, in order of leverage. Reduce the need to recall — retrieve sources into the context so facts do not come from compressed memory. Constrain the task — narrow scope, enumerated options, structured output. Make it verifiable — require quoted source spans and check in code that the quote actually appears. Verify after generation — a second-pass claim check, a schema validator, and a rules engine or database for anything that must be exact, like pricing or dosage. Give it an exit — an explicit abstain path and a route to a human, which converts a wrong answer into a slow one. Design the interface for it — sources inline, honest hedging, editable output, never presenting generated text as an authoritative record. Measure it — claim-level faithfulness on a real eval set, monitored continuously. And contain the blast radius: put the feature where a wrong answer is cheap. In production I would also watch abstention rate and human-override rate, because a falling hallucination rate with a climbing refusal rate is not progress.

      Going deeper: naming what leaks through each layer — retrieval fetches the wrong document, a real quote can support a bad inference, a same-family judge shares the generator’s blind spots.

      “Anthropic: How would you define a ‘redline’ for a model?”

      A redline is a category of output that is never acceptable at any quality bar, where the correct response to a violation is to block or degrade the feature rather than tune it. The property that makes it a redline is that you accept a worse product to hold it.

      Contrast it with a quality metric. A support rate above ninety-two percent is a curve: you push it, you trade it against latency and cost, and eighty-eight percent still ships while you work on it. A redline has no gradient. It is binary, it is tested adversarially, and the action on violation is agreed before launch, when nobody is under shipping pressure. A good one is specific enough to write a test for, tied to a concrete harm rather than a feeling, owned by a named person, attached to a pre-agreed consequence, and gated by a dedicated adversarial suite written by someone trying to break it. For a clinical assistant: never state a medication dose that is not copied verbatim from the record, and never assert that a patient does not have a condition, because absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. For any retrieval product: no reference that fails to resolve to a real retrieved document ever reaches the user. Keep the list short. A long list of redlines is a list of KPIs with dramatic names.

      Going deeper: the test for whether something truly is one — if the answer to “can we trade this for launch date?” is “let’s look at the numbers,” it was never a redline.

      RAG, end to end

      Retrieval-augmented generation — RAG — is one sentence long. Before you send the question to the model, search an external body of text for the passages most likely to contain the answer, paste those passages into the prompt, and instruct the model to answer using them. Nothing about the model changes. You changed what it can see.

      The idea in one line. RAG does not teach the model anything. It puts the right paragraph in front of the model at the moment the question is asked.

      The thing candidates blur is that RAG is two systems that run at different times. Phase one, indexing, happens offline, ahead of any user. Phase two, querying, happens online, once per request, in a few hundred milliseconds. They fail differently, they cost differently, and they are owned by different people. If you describe RAG as one loop, an interviewer hears someone who has only used a tutorial.

      Phase 1 — indexing (offline)

      Load the sources: PDFs, help-center articles, Confluence pages, tickets, a codebase. Parse and clean them into plain text — strip navigation chrome and boilerplate footers, run optical character recognition on scanned pages, decide what to do with tables and images. Chunk: cut each document into passages of a few hundred tokens. Embed each chunk — run it through an embedding model that maps text to a fixed-length list of numbers, a vector, where texts about similar things land near each other (chapter 2 built this idea; nothing new here). Store the vector alongside its metadata and, critically, the original text, because the text is what you will later paste into the prompt.

      Phase 2 — querying (online)

      Optionally rewrite the query — resolve “does it also do that?” into a standalone question using the conversation so far, or expand an acronym. Embed the query with the same model used at indexing time, because two embedding models produce incompatible coordinate systems. Search for the nearest vectors. Optionally retrieve more than you need and rerank them with a slower, more accurate model. Assemble the prompt: instructions, the chosen chunks, the question. Generate. Then attach citations by mapping each chunk back to its source pointer.

      Walk one question through it. A support agent asks: “What does error E-4021 mean on the Model X pump?” Offline, 8,400 pages of manuals became roughly 40,000 chunks, each turned into a 1,536-number vector. Online, the query is rewritten to include the product name from the conversation, embedded into its own 1,536-number vector, and compared against all 40,000. The store returns 20 candidates ranked by cosine similarity — the top one scoring, say, 0.82, the twentieth 0.61 — and a reranker reorders those 20 and keeps 4. Those 4 chunks, about 1,600 tokens, go into a prompt with instructions to answer only from them and to cite. Total prompt: around 3,800 tokens. The answer comes back with two footnotes pointing at manual sections.

      Now count the decisions in that paragraph: cleaning rules, chunk size, chunk overlap, embedding model, whether to rewrite the query, how many candidates to fetch, whether to rerank and with what, how many chunks survive, what order they sit in, the wording of the instruction, the generation model, and how citations are rendered. That is a dozen knobs, each with a measurable effect. RAG is not a feature you switch on.

      The store and the chunks

      A vector database is a store built for one operation: given a query vector, find the nearest vectors among millions. Nearest means largest cosine similarity, and doing it exactly means comparing against every stored vector — millions of multiply-adds over 1,536 dimensions, per request. That is too slow. So these systems build an approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) index: a graph or a partitioning of the space that lets a search visit a small fraction of the data and still usually find the true neighbors. You trade a little recall — sometimes it misses the actual best match — for one to two orders of magnitude of speed.

      They also store what makes retrieval usable: the original chunk text, metadata for filtering (product, language, version, tenant, timestamp), and a pointer back to the source so you can cite it. Metadata filtering matters more than people expect, because “only documents this customer is allowed to see” is a hard requirement, not a ranking preference.

      Common misconception. Vector search is not always the best search. Embeddings are weakest on exactly the strings users type when they are precise: error codes, SKUs, version numbers, surnames. E-4021 and E-4201 embed almost identically. Keyword search — BM25, a decades-old term-frequency ranking — nails them. Hybrid search runs both and fuses the rankings, and it usually beats either alone. Also: for a few thousand documents you may not need a vector database at all. An in-memory array and a brute-force scan is milliseconds and zero operational burden.

      Chunking is the decision about where to cut documents before embedding, and it sets your quality ceiling. The reason is mechanical: a chunk is the unit of retrieval, so a chunk is what the embedding must summarize and what the model gets to read. Too small, and the chunk loses the context that made it meaningful — “set the value to 3” is useless without knowing which value. Too large, and one vector has to represent five topics at once, so it is mediocre at matching any of them, and each retrieved chunk eats context budget you could have spent on a second, better passage.

      Three strategies, in increasing order of effort. Fixed size with overlap: cut every 400 tokens with 50 tokens repeated across the boundary so a sentence straddling a cut survives somewhere. Cheap, universal, blind to structure. Structural splitting: cut on the document’s own headings, list items, or code blocks, so a chunk is a section. Usually the best return on effort. Semantic splitting: walk the document and cut where consecutive sentences stop being similar to each other. Elegant, slower, and it can orphan content from its heading.

      Three practical rules survive contact with real corpora. Keep a parent-document pointer on every chunk, so you can retrieve on the small chunk but feed the model the surrounding section when it needs more. Repeat the headings inside the chunk text — a chunk that begins “Model X pump > Error codes >” embeds better and reads better. And keep tables intact, even when that makes a chunk oversized, because half a table is worse than no table.

      When RAG breaks: exactly two failure modes

      This one has a crisp answer, so give it crisply. Either the right information never reached the model, or it reached the model and the model still got it wrong.

      Retrieval failure. The correct passage was not in the assembled context. Three sub-causes, and they need different fixes: it is not in the corpus at all (an ingestion or coverage problem); it is in the corpus but not matched by the query (a vocabulary mismatch — the doc says “thermal cutout,” the user says “overheat shutdown” — fixed by hybrid search, query rewriting, or better chunking); or it was matched but ranked below the cutoff (fixed by raising k, adding a reranker, or fusing rankings).

      Generation failure. The correct passage was in the context and the answer is still wrong. The model ignored it, contradicted it, blended it with its own parametric memory, picked the wrong one of two retrieved passages, or hedged into uselessness. Fixes live in the prompt, the ordering of evidence, the number of chunks (more is not better — noise crowds out signal), the model choice, and citation-checking after generation.

      The idea in one line. The two failure modes have completely different fixes, so you must measure them separately — retrieval recall on one dashboard, answer correctness on another. Report one blended “accuracy” number and you will spend a quarter tuning the wrong half of the system.

      The diagnostic move, and the sentence that wins the interview: before touching the prompt, check whether the correct chunk was in the context. Log the retrieved chunk IDs for every request. When someone reports a bad answer, look at the log first. If the chunk was not there, no prompt in the world will save you.

      RAG, fine-tuning, or prompting — and keeping it fresh

      Three ways to change what a model does, and they answer different questions. Prompting changes the instruction. RAG changes the evidence. Fine-tuning — continuing to train the model on your own examples so its weights shift — changes the model’s defaults.

      Which lever to pull, and what it costs you
      Lever Use it for Update cost Can cite sources? Main failure
      Prompting Behavior you can describe in words: tone, format, refusals, reasoning steps Seconds — edit text No Long, brittle instructions the model partly ignores
      RAG Knowledge that is external, private, large, or changing Minutes — re-index the changed documents Yes, natively Retrieval misses; an extra system to operate
      Fine-tuning Form, format, and style the model will not hold reliably; also cutting latency and cost on one narrow task Hours to days, plus a data set and an eval No Stale facts baked into weights; drift from the base model

      Common misconception. Fine-tuning is a bad way to teach facts. It is expensive to update, it cannot cite, and it does not reliably make a fact retrievable — training on a document teaches style and statistical association far more dependably than it installs a lookup you can trust. If the requirement is “the answer must be correct and attributable,” that is retrieval, not training. Fine-tuning is for how the model answers; RAG is for what it knows.

      These compose, and most real systems use two or three: a fine-tuned small model that reliably emits your JSON schema, fed retrieved evidence, wrapped in a carefully worded system prompt. The interesting question is never “which one” but “which one for this part of the problem.”

      Frequently changing information

      This is where RAG wins by construction, and the reason is one sentence: to update knowledge you re-index a document, you do not retrain a model. A price change is a write to a store, not a training run.

      Freshness has mechanics, though, and naming them is what separates a real answer. Incremental indexing with change detection — hash each source, re-embed only what moved. Deletion and tombstoning — when a document dies, its chunks must actually leave the index, or be marked dead and filtered at query time. Cache invalidation — a cached answer built on a superseded chunk is a stale answer with a fresh timestamp. Timestamps in metadata plus recency-aware ranking — retrieve on relevance, then break ties toward the newer version. And tell the model the current date in the system prompt, because it has no clock.

      Two failure cases people forget. First, orphan chunks: the source page was deleted or rewritten months ago and its old chunks are still sitting in the index, perfectly retrievable. Second, contradictory versions: v2.1 and v3.0 of the same policy both retrieved into the same prompt, with nothing in the text telling the model which one is current. The model will pick one, confidently, and it may pick wrong. The fix is version metadata plus an explicit rule — filter to the current version, or label each chunk with its effective date and instruct the model to prefer the latest.

      Latency, relevance, cost

      Every knob in RAG trades against the other two. State the direction of effect for each one and you sound like someone who has run the experiment.

      Levers, and which way each one pushes
      Lever Turn it up and…
      Chunks retrieved (top-k)Recall rises, then relevance falls as noise crowds the prompt; tokens and latency rise linearly

      Now design one. Target: an internal support assistant, p95 under two seconds to first token, 50,000 questions a day. Budget the parts. Query embedding is small and cacheable, call it 10–40 ms. ANN search over 40,000 chunks, tens of milliseconds. Reranking 20 candidates with a cross-encoder, a couple hundred milliseconds — or 90 ms if you rerank only 8. Generation to first token dominates everything: hundreds of milliseconds to over a second, and it grows with prompt length, which is exactly what top-k controls. Streaming the rest is user-perceived reading time.

      The trade I would make: keep the reranker but only over 8 candidates, use a smaller generation model, cache query embeddings and full answers for the head of the query distribution, and stream. To know whether I was right, I would watch two numbers together — retrieval recall@k on a fixed labeled question set, and answer correctness graded on the same set — alongside p50 and p95 time-to-first-token and cost per resolved question. If recall@8 with reranking matches recall@20 without it, the cheaper design is strictly better and the argument is over.

      Context engineering: the window is a budget

      Prompt engineering is the craft of wording the instruction: what you ask for, how you phrase it, what examples you show, what format you demand. Context engineering is the craft of deciding what information occupies the finite context window — which items, in what order, in what format, at what token cost, and, when space runs out, what gets evicted.

      The idea in one line. Prompt engineering optimizes the sentence. Context engineering optimizes the allocation. Once products became agentic — multi-turn, many tools, long histories, retrieved documents — the allocation became the harder and more valuable problem.

      The reason is contention. The window is a single shared resource, and five claimants want it: the system prompt and policy, tool definitions (which can run to thousands of tokens on their own), conversation history, retrieved documents, and the output you have to leave room to generate. Everything is competing, and every token you spend on a low-value item is a token unavailable to the passage that actually answers the question. Worse, from chapter 2: attention is not uniform across position. Models attend best to the beginning and the end of a long context and measurably worst to the middle — the “lost in the middle” effect. Position is a resource too.

      So order deliberately. Stable instructions early: they are also the part that caches best across requests, which saves real money. Low-value material in the middle, where degradation costs you least — compacted history, background. The strongest retrieved evidence and the user’s actual question at the end, closest to where generation begins. If you retrieved four chunks, put the top-ranked one last, not first.

      The discipline has two halves. A token budget per section, written down: this much for tools, this much for evidence, this much reserved for output. And an eviction policy decided in advance — when the request would overflow, you already know that history gets recompacted first, then summarized, then the lowest-ranked chunk goes, then unused tool definitions. If you do not decide this, your framework decides it for you, usually by silently truncating the oldest tokens, and you will discover the policy in production.

      When RAG is overkill

      Retrieval is not free. It is a second system to own: an ingestion pipeline, an index, a freshness guarantee, monitoring, and a whole extra failure mode. Skip it when the corpus fits comfortably in the window — if the knowledge is twenty pages, paste the twenty pages and cache the prefix. Skip it when the task needs no external knowledge, like rewriting, translating, or classifying text the user already supplied. Skip it when the source of truth is a structured database, where generated SQL or a plain API call is more precise than fuzzy passage matching. Skip it when a deterministic lookup is correct and cheaper — an order status belongs to a function call, not a similarity search. And skip it when the latency budget cannot absorb a retrieval hop at all.

      Common misconception. “Add RAG” is not a default. A cheaper answer usually exists for narrow, structured, or already-in-hand knowledge, and the hidden cost of RAG is not the vector database licence — it is the permanent obligation to keep an index true.

      “What is RAG and when would you use it?”

      RAG means searching an external corpus at question time and putting the best passages into the prompt, so the model answers from evidence it can cite instead of from memory.

      The model is unchanged — you changed what it can see. Use it whenever the knowledge needed is external to the model: private company data, documents that change, a corpus far too large for the context window, or anything where the answer must be attributable to a source. It is the standard fix for the three “the model does not know” problems: knowledge cutoff, private data, and hallucination on facts. It also gives you access control and auditability, because retrieval respects metadata filters and produces citations, which a fine-tuned model cannot.

      Going deeper: naming what RAG does not fix. It does not improve reasoning, tone, or output format, and it introduces a whole retrieval subsystem you now have to keep fresh and monitor.

      “Walk through a RAG pipeline end to end.”

      Two phases: offline indexing — load, parse, chunk, embed, store; and online querying — rewrite, embed, search, rerank, assemble, generate, cite.

      Offline you load the sources, clean them into text, cut them into chunks of a few hundred tokens, embed each chunk with an embedding model, and store the vector next to its metadata and its original text. Online you optionally rewrite the query into a standalone question, embed it with the same model, run approximate nearest-neighbor search for the top candidates, optionally rerank those candidates with a slower and more accurate model, assemble a prompt from instructions plus the surviving chunks plus the question, generate, and map each chunk back to a source pointer for citations. Concretely: 8,400 pages become 40,000 chunks, the store returns 20 candidates, a reranker keeps 4, and the prompt lands around 3,800 tokens.

      Going deeper: insisting on the two-phase split and naming what is tunable at each step. There are roughly a dozen decisions in that walk — chunk size, overlap, embedding model, k, rerank or not, chunk order — and saying so signals you have actually tuned one.

      “RAG vs fine-tuning vs prompt: when to pick each?”

      Prompting for behavior you can describe, RAG for knowledge that is external or changing, fine-tuning for form and style the model will not hold reliably — plus latency and cost reduction on a narrow task.

      Prompting is seconds to change and should always be tried first. RAG is the answer whenever correctness depends on facts you own or facts that move, because updating means re-indexing a document rather than retraining a model, and because it can cite. Fine-tuning earns its keep when you need a consistent output shape or house style across thousands of calls, or when you want a small cheap model to match a large one on one narrow task. They compose: a fine-tuned small model that reliably emits your schema, fed retrieved evidence, wrapped in a good system prompt. Most production systems use two of the three.

      Going deeper: explicitly killing the common error — fine-tuning is a bad way to teach facts. It is expensive to update, it cannot cite, and training on documents does not reliably make their contents retrievable.

      “What is a vector database and why do you need one?”

      A store optimized for nearest-neighbor search over high-dimensional vectors, using an approximate index that trades a little recall for one to two orders of magnitude of speed.

      Exact search means comparing your query vector against every stored vector — millions of dot products over a thousand-plus dimensions, per request. Too slow. Approximate nearest-neighbor indexes, built as navigable graphs or space partitions, visit a small fraction of the data and usually still return the true top matches. They also store what makes retrieval usable: the original chunk text, metadata for filtering by tenant, product, version, or language, and a source pointer for citations. The index type and its parameters are your explicit recall-versus-speed dial.

      Going deeper: two honest caveats. For a few thousand documents you may not need one at all — brute-force scan of an in-memory array is milliseconds. And hybrid search, dense vectors fused with BM25 keyword scoring, usually beats pure vector search, because exact strings like error codes and product names are precisely where embeddings are weakest.

      “What is chunking and why does it matter?”

      Chunking is deciding where to cut documents before embedding, and it sets your quality ceiling, because the chunk is the unit of both retrieval and reading.

      The tension is direct. Chunks that are too small lose the context that made them meaningful — “set the value to 3” without knowing which value. Chunks that are too large force one vector to represent several topics, so it matches all of them mediocrely, and each retrieved chunk burns context budget you could have spent on a better passage. Strategies, in increasing effort: fixed size with overlap; structural splitting on headings, lists, or code blocks; and semantic splitting where consecutive sentences stop resembling each other. In practice, keep a parent-document pointer so you can retrieve small and read large, repeat the heading path inside the chunk text, and keep tables intact even when that makes a chunk oversized.

      Going deeper: saying plainly that most RAG quality is won or lost here, before any prompt tuning, and that you would test two chunking policies against a labeled question set rather than argue about token counts.

      “What are the two failure modes of RAG?”

      Retrieval failure — the right information never reached the model; and generation failure — it did reach the model and the answer is still wrong.

      Retrieval failure has three sub-causes with different fixes: the passage is not in the corpus (ingestion and coverage), it is in the corpus but the query did not match it (vocabulary mismatch, fixed by hybrid search, query rewriting, or better chunking), or it matched but ranked below the cutoff (raise k, add a reranker, fuse rankings).       “How do you balance latency, relevance, and cost in RAG?”

      Set a budget for each, then move the specific knobs that trade between them — top-k, reranking, model sizes, index parameters, caching, hybrid search, and streaming — and measure retrieval recall separately from answer quality.

      Directions of effect: raising top-k lifts recall but adds tokens, latency, and noise that can reduce relevance; reranking buys precision for tens to a couple hundred milliseconds; larger generation models buy synthesis for multiples of cost and latency; the ANN index type is an explicit recall-versus-speed dial; hybrid search is a cheap accuracy win on exact terms; caching embeddings, retrieval results, prompt prefixes, and whole answers cuts both cost and latency at the price of invalidation risk; and streaming plus precomputing common queries improves perceived latency without changing total work.

      Going deeper: knowing where the time actually is. Embedding and search are tens of milliseconds; generation is hundreds to thousands. So the real latency levers are the ones that shorten the prompt or shrink the generator, not the ones that shave milliseconds off search.

      “What is context engineering vs prompt engineering?”

      Prompt engineering is wording the instruction. Context engineering is deciding what information occupies the finite window, in what order, in what format, at what token cost — and what gets evicted when it overflows.

      The window is a single scarce resource contended for by five claimants: the system prompt, tool definitions, conversation history, retrieved documents, and the space you must reserve for output. Every token spent on a low-value item is a token unavailable to the passage that answers the question. Position matters too, because attention is not uniform — models attend best to the start and end of a long context and worst to the middle. So context engineering is allocation and ordering, backed by a written token budget per section and an eviction policy chosen in advance.

      Going deeper: explaining why the discipline shifted. Single-turn chat needed good wording. Agentic products — many tools, long histories, retrieved documents, multi-step loops — made allocation the binding constraint, which is why context engineering became the more important skill.

      “What goes into the context window and in what priority?”

      Stable instructions first, tool definitions next, compacted history in the middle, then the strongest retrieved evidence and the user’s actual question last, with output space reserved from the start.

      The ordering follows from the lost-in-the-middle effect: put what must be obeyed at the beginning, where attention is strong and where prefix caching saves money, and put what must be used at the end, immediately before generation begins. If you retrieved four chunks, place the top-ranked one closest to the question, not first. Park the least load-bearing material — background, summarized history — in the middle, where degradation costs least. Then write down a token budget per section: for example, of a 10,000-token budget, 800 for instructions, 1,200 for tools, 800 for compacted history, 4,000 for evidence, 1,200 for recent turns, 200 for the question, 1,800 reserved for output.

      Going deeper: a pre-decided eviction order — recompact history, then summarize recent turns, then drop the lowest-ranked chunk, then prune unused tool definitions, never the instructions or the question. If you do not decide it, your framework silently truncates for you and you learn the policy in production.

      “When is RAG overkill?”

      When the knowledge fits in the window, needs no external source, lives in a structured database, has a deterministic lookup, or when the latency budget cannot absorb a retrieval hop.

      If the corpus is twenty pages, paste the twenty pages and cache the prompt prefix — simpler, more accurate, no index to keep true. If the task is transformation of text the user already supplied, like summarizing, translating, or classifying, there is nothing to retrieve. If the source of truth is a relational database, generated SQL or a plain API call is more precise than fuzzy passage matching, and it can aggregate, which retrieval cannot. If the question is “where is my order,” that is a function call with an exact answer. And if you have a hard sub-second budget, a retrieval hop plus a longer prompt may not fit.

      Going deeper: naming the cost people miss. RAG adds an entire retrieval system you must own, monitor, and keep fresh — ingestion, index, deletions, cache invalidation, evals — plus a second failure mode to debug. That ongoing obligation, not the infrastructure bill, is the real price.

      “Perplexity: Explain how RAG works.”

      Retrieve first, then generate, and cite: at question time you search a corpus, select the passages most likely to contain the answer, and condition generation on them so every claim traces to a source.

      Give the two phases. Offline: load and clean the sources, chunk them, embed each chunk into a vector, and store the vector with metadata and the original text. Online: rewrite the query if the conversation makes it ambiguous, embed it with the same model, run approximate nearest-neighbor search, rerank the candidates, assemble a prompt of instructions plus the surviving chunks plus the question, generate, then map each chunk back to its source for citations. For a search product specifically, add the parts that matter at web scale: hybrid retrieval so exact names and codes are not lost, freshness ranking so today’s page beats last year’s, and a grounding instruction that forbids claims not supported by the retrieved text.

      Going deeper: treating citations as an architectural requirement rather than a garnish. Citations are only possible because you kept the original text and source pointer next to the vector — and they are the thing that makes retrieval failure and generation failure separately debuggable.

      “Nvidia: Design a RAG system with latency, relevance, and cost trade-offs.”

      I would fix the budget first — say p95 under two seconds to first token at 50,000 questions a day — then assemble the pipeline from parts whose latency I can add up, and buy relevance only where it is cheap.

      Baseline: embed the query (10–40 ms, cacheable), hybrid retrieval over the index (tens of milliseconds), rerank the top candidates (a couple hundred milliseconds for 20, roughly 90 for 8), then generate, which dominates at hundreds of milliseconds to over a second and grows with prompt length. My trade: keep the reranker but only over 8 candidates, use a smaller generation model, cache query embeddings and full answers for the head of the query distribution, tune the ANN index toward speed since a reranker recovers precision, and stream the output so perceived latency is the time to first token, not to completion. That takes an illustrative 2.65 seconds total down to about 1.5, and time-to-first-token from roughly 1.25 to 0.55 seconds.

      Going deeper: saying what you would measure to know you were right. Retrieval recall@k on a fixed labeled set and answer correctness on the same set, plotted against p50 and p95 time-to-first-token and cost per resolved question. If recall@8 with reranking equals recall@20 without it, the cheaper design wins on evidence rather than on taste.

      A tool call is text the model asks you to run

      One mechanical fact holds up this entire chapter, so here it is before the explanation: the model never executes anything. It cannot open a network connection, run a database query, or send an email. It predicts tokens — that is the whole of what it does. When you hear that an AI feature “called a tool,” what physically happened is that the model emitted some structured text naming a function and its arguments, and your code read that text and did the work.

      Here is the full round trip. The interview answer is essentially this list, in order.

      1. You send the request. Your application posts the prompt plus a list of tool definitions to the model — each one a name, a description, and a typed schema for its arguments.
      2. The model emits a request, not an action. Instead of prose, it predicts a block of structured output: a tool name and a JSON object of arguments. Then it stops. Nothing has happened yet anywhere in the world.
      3. Your code parses and validates it. Does that tool exist? Do the arguments type-check? Is this user allowed to do this? Is the amount within limits? Your code decides whether to run it at all.
      4. Your code executes. It calls the function, hits the API, queries the database — with your credentials, your rate limits, your timeouts, your audit log.
      5. Your code sends the result back into the conversation as a new message, tagged as the result of that specific call.
      6. The model continues with that result now sitting in its context, and either answers the user or requests another tool. Loop as many times as needed.

      The idea in one line. The model is choosing, not doing. It emits a request; your application is the thing with hands.

      That has a consequence worth stating out loud, because it decides where half of your engineering work lives. Permissions, authentication, spend caps, retries, idempotency (not charging the card twice on a replay), confirmation before irreversible actions, and the audit trail all live in your code. A model that “decided” to refund a customer $10,000 did not refund anything — an application that executed the request without a check did.

      Common misconception. “We gave the model access to our database.” You did not. You gave it a text description of a function, and gave your own service the ability to run that function when the model asks. If your service runs whatever it is asked without checking, that is a bug in your service.

      The tool definition is a prompt, not documentation

      A tool definition has three parts. A name, which should read like a function name. A description in natural language. And a parameter schema — almost always JSON Schema, which is just a JSON description of allowed shapes: field names, types, which fields are required, enumerated options (“one of: standard, express, overnight”), numeric ranges, and defaults.

      Now the part people miss. That definition is the only thing the model knows about your tool. It cannot read your implementation, your comments, or your database schema. Choosing a tool and filling in its arguments is next-token prediction conditioned on those descriptions and nothing else. So a tool definition is not documentation. It is a prompt, and it behaves like one.

      Which is why a vague description is a real, ticket-worthy bug rather than a style problem. Three failure modes come out of it. The model picks the wrong tool, because two descriptions overlap and it has no basis to prefer one. It produces malformed or invented arguments — a date in the wrong format, a currency you do not support, a plausible-looking customer ID it made up — because the schema never said. Or the tool is never called at all, because the description does not mention the words a real user would use. Worse, all three fail probabilistically: the tool works when you try it by hand, then misfires on some fraction of production traffic that depends on how the user phrased their message.

      The operational point follows directly. Tool descriptions belong in version control, in code review, and in your eval suite — the automated set of test cases you re-run on every change (Chapter 8). Editing one word of a description changes model behavior exactly the way editing a prompt does, silently and across every user. Two practical notes: keep the tool list small, since selection accuracy degrades as the menu grows; and when two tools overlap, merge them or write the disambiguation into both.

      Prompt, tool, skill — three shapes of the same substance

      These three words get used as if they were rival technologies. They are not. A prompt is instructions and content placed in the context window. A tool is a capability with a name and a schema that the model can request and your code executes. A skill is a packaged bundle — instructions, plus optional reference files and scripts — that gets loaded into context when it looks relevant to the task, so you do not have to carry every procedure in every request.

      The idea in one line. All three are context and configuration. None of them touch the weights.

      So: do skills retrain the model? No. Nothing about the model changes. The weights were frozen at the end of training (Chapter 1) and are identical for you, for the next customer, and for the request you send a second later. What “loads at runtime” is text: your application, or the harness around the model, selects the relevant skill content and tool definitions and inserts them into the context window for that one request. When the request ends, they are gone. If a skill contains a script, note the pattern from the last section — the script is executed by the runtime, not by the model.

      This ties back to the context budget from Chapter 2, and it is a real design constraint. A rich tool definition might run a few hundred tokens; a detailed skill can run thousands. Those numbers are illustrative, but the shape holds: every tool and skill you add spends window you no longer have for retrieved evidence, history, or the user’s document. “Just add another tool” is never free.

      Chatbot, chain, agent: the difference is who controls the control flow

      Strip the marketing away and this is a claim about control flow — the order in which steps execute, and who decides that order.

      In a chatbot, the path is fixed and the human drives. A turn goes in, a turn comes out, and the human decides whether there is a next step. In a prompt chain, you the developer fixed the path in advance: step one summarizes, step two extracts fields, step three drafts a reply, always in that order. In an agent, the model decides the next step. It is given a goal and a set of tools, it plans, it acts, it observes what came back, and it loops until some stop condition is met — goal satisfied, step budget exhausted, or a human intervenes. The model is inside the control flow, not just inside a step of it.

      That loop buys flexibility on tasks you cannot script. It costs four things. Step count is unbounded, so latency and spend are unbounded unless you cap them. Errors compound across turns, because turn five reasons over whatever turn two returned. Evaluation is much harder: there is no single correct output, only a trajectory that may reach the goal by an unexpected route. And the blast radius is real — an agent with a payments tool and a bad loop can spend money repeatedly.

      The honest test for whether you need an agent. If you can enumerate the steps ahead of time, write the steps. A fixed chain is cheaper, faster, debuggable, and testable. Reach for an agent when the number and order of steps genuinely depend on what you find along the way.

      Prototyping a prompt chain for a multi-step task

      There is a method here, and it is not “write a long prompt and hope.” First, decompose the task into steps and write an explicit input and output contract for each: this step receives these fields, it must return these fields. Second, do the whole thing by hand once, start to finish. If you cannot do it manually, your decomposition is wrong and no amount of engineering fixes that. Third, make each step’s output structured — JSON against a schema — and validate it in code before passing it on, so a broken step fails loudly at step two instead of quietly at step five. Fourth, test each step in isolation with fixed inputs, so you know its individual reliability. Only then wire them together.

      Error compounding, with the arithmetic

      This is the single most useful thing a PM can internalize about chains and agents. Reliability multiplies. A five-step chain where every step is 95% correct ends up at 0.955 ≈ 77% end to end. At 90% per step it is 59%. Even a stellar 99% per step, run for ten steps, lands at about 90% — which means one in ten runs is wrong somewhere. Nobody notices this in a demo of three happy-path runs.

      What you do about it: shorten the chain; validate at each boundary so errors are caught rather than propagated; retry the failed step rather than the whole run; checkpoint state after expensive steps so a retry is cheap; and put a human in front of anything irreversible or externally visible — sending the email, issuing the refund, merging the code. Cheap and reversible steps can run unattended. Expensive and irreversible ones get a confirmation.

      Memory is four different things

      “Give the agent memory” is under-specified, and the strong answer is to split it into four mechanisms before choosing any of them. Remember that the model itself is stateless: it remembers nothing between calls. Every kind of memory is something you build outside the model and re-insert into the context window.

      Four kinds of agent memory, what each is good for, and where each fails
      Kind What it physically is Good for Fails at
      In-context / working The context window of this one call Everything in the current task; free and exact Finite, and gone the moment the call ends
      Session The running transcript, stored and resent each turn Continuity inside one conversation Grows without bound; needs compaction or summary, which loses detail
      Long-term semantic Facts extracted and stored externally, retrieved by relevance — RAG over the user’s own history Preferences, context, color: “prefers aisle seats” Retrieval misses; stale or contradictory facts; not authoritative
      Structured state A real database row or profile record Anything that must be right: plan tier, balance, address, consent You must design the schema, and it only holds what you modeled

      Then come the parts nobody mentions in the pitch. What is worth remembering? Saving everything makes retrieval noisy and expensive. What happens on contradiction? The user said they were vegetarian in March and ordered steak in June; you need recency rules or explicit confirmation, not two facts side by side. Forgetting and correcting must be first-class, not an afterthought. Privacy and deletion are obligations: a stored memory is personal data, it needs consent, visibility, and a working delete path. And every retrieved memory occupies window that retrieved evidence would otherwise use.

      Default architecture. Structured state for anything that has to be correct. Semantic memory for preferences and color. Session transcript for the current conversation, with summarization when it outgrows the budget. Never let a fuzzy retrieved “memory” be the source of truth for a fact your database already knows.

      MCP: turning N × M integrations into N + M

      Start with the problem, because MCP is a response to a specific, boring, expensive one. Before it, every AI application wrote its own integration code for every tool and data source it wanted to reach. Your app needed bespoke glue for GitHub, for Postgres, for your internal ticketing system; the app next door wrote all of it again, differently. With N applications and M tools you got N × M integrations, and not one of them was reusable.

      The Model Context Protocol is an open protocol that standardizes how an application exposes tools (functions the model can request), resources (data it can read), and prompts (reusable templates) to a model. One side is a server, which wraps a system and advertises what it offers in the standard shape. The other side is a client — the AI application — which discovers those offerings at runtime and turns them into tool definitions for the model. Write the server once and any compliant client can use it. N × M becomes N + M.

      Now the analogy, in its proper place at the end: MCP is USB-C for tools. The value is not the connector, it is that nobody has to ship a new cable for every pair of devices.

      MCP server versus a one-off API integration
      Dimension MCP server One-off integration
      ReuseWrite once, any compliant client can use itTied to your app; rewritten elsewhere
      EcosystemExisting servers for common systems; swap clients or models freelyYou build and maintain every connector
      ControlConstrained by the protocol’s shape and versioningTotal control over payloads, batching, caching
      DependenciesA protocol layer and a server process to run and secureNothing beyond your own code and the vendor SDK
      TuningGeneric interface, generic performanceBespoke optimization for your exact query pattern
      Best whenMany tools, many surfaces, or you want the ecosystemOne integration you will never reuse — genuinely simpler

      What MCP does not solve. It standardizes the plumbing, not the thinking. Your tool descriptions still have to be good, because the model still selects on text. Authentication, permissions, and scoping are still yours to design — and a server that exposes broad access to a system is now a broad attack surface. Standardizing the protocol does not make an agent reliable; the compounding-error math in Fig 5.4 does not care how the tool was wired up.

      One last thing, since it is asked directly: interviewers at companies building this will ask whether you have built agentic systems. With no job history, say so plainly and then talk about something you actually made — a small tool-using loop you can describe honestly, including where it broke and what you changed. Naming your own failure modes is the signal. Chapter 9 covers that project in full.

      Try answering each one out loud before you open it. The gap between what you said and what is written below is the part you have not learned yet — that gap is the whole point of this section.

      “An AI feature calls a tool. What actually happens?”

      The model emits structured text naming a tool and its arguments, then stops. My code parses it, validates it, decides whether to run it, runs it, and sends the result back into the model as a new message.

      Said in full: I send the prompt plus a list of tool definitions. The model predicts a tool name and a JSON argument object instead of prose, and generation ends there — nothing has happened in the world yet. My application parses that output, type-checks the arguments against the schema, and applies its own authorization and limits. Then my code executes: the API call, the database query, whatever it is. I put the result back into the conversation tagged as the result of that call, and the model continues with the result in its context, either answering or requesting another tool. That loop repeats until no tool is requested or my step cap is hit.

      Going deeper: naming what lives in your code because of this — authorization, spend caps, idempotency, retries, the audit log, and a confirmation step before anything irreversible.

      “Does the model execute the function? Who does?”

      No. The model only emits a request. My application layer executes it.

      The model is a text predictor with no ability to run code, open connections, or touch a database. What it produces is a name and a JSON object. The execution is done by the application, the agent framework, or the MCP server — code you own and deploy, running with your credentials. This is not a pedantic distinction: it is where the entire safety story lives. Because your code is the executor, it is your code that checks whether this user may take this action, whether the amount is in range, whether the call has already been made, and whether a human should confirm first. A model cannot exceed its permissions, because it has none. Your executor has them.

      Going deeper: the one-liner — the model chooses, your app acts — plus the observation that “the model did something dangerous” always means an application executed a request without a check.

      “Why is a vague tool description a real bug?”

      Because tool selection is next-token prediction conditioned on those descriptions. A vague description makes the model pick the wrong tool, invent arguments, or skip the tool entirely — and it fails only some of the time.

      The description and schema are the only information the model has about the tool; it cannot read the implementation. So a vague or overlapping description degrades three things at once: which tool gets chosen, whether the arguments are well-formed, and whether the tool fires at all when a user phrases the request unusually. The nasty part is that these are probabilistic failures. Your manual test passes, the demo passes, and then some percentage of real traffic misroutes in a way that depends on wording. That is why descriptions belong in version control, in code review, and in an eval suite — changing one is a behavior change deployed to every user, exactly like changing a prompt.

      Going deeper: saying what a good description contains — what it does, when to use it, when explicitly not to, argument semantics and units, and how it differs from the tool next to it.

      “What’s in a tool definition (name, description, schema)?”

      A name, a natural-language description of what it does and when to use it, and a typed parameter schema — usually JSON Schema, with required fields, enums, and constraints.

      The name should read like a function name and hint at scope: search_order_history, not search. The description carries the routing logic: purpose, trigger conditions, explicit non-triggers, and disambiguation from neighboring tools. The schema constrains what the model can even produce — field names and types, which fields are required, enums for closed sets, ranges and defaults for numbers, and format notes such as ISO-8601 for dates. Treat the schema as your first line of validation, not your only one; your code still validates on receipt. And remember the definition is the entire interface: if a rule is not in the name, description, or schema, the model does not know it exists.

      Going deeper: pointing out that the definition costs context tokens on every single request, so tool count and description length are a budget decision, not a free one.

      “What makes something an agent vs chatbot?”

      Control flow. A chatbot’s path is fixed and the human drives it. An agent decides its own next step, uses tools, observes results, and loops until a stop condition is met.

      A chatbot is turn in, turn out; the human decides whether there is a next step. A prompt chain is multi-step but the developer fixed the order in advance. An agent is given a goal and tools and runs a loop — plan, act, observe, repeat — where the model chooses which tool to call next based on what came back. That means the model is inside the control flow. The costs are all downstream of the loop: unbounded steps, therefore unbounded latency and spend; errors that compound across turns; evaluation over trajectories instead of single outputs; and real blast radius when the tools can move money or send messages.

      Going deeper: the honest test — if you can enumerate the steps ahead of time, write the steps. A fixed chain is cheaper, faster, and testable, and most “agents” in production should have been chains.

      “What’s the difference between a prompt, a tool, and a skill?”

      A prompt is instructions in context. A tool is a capability with a schema that the model can request and my code executes. A skill is a packaged bundle of instructions and resources loaded into context when it is relevant.

      A prompt shapes behavior with words. A tool extends reach: it lets the model ask for something to happen outside itself, and the execution belongs to the application. A skill is about packaging and selective loading — instructions plus optional reference files and scripts that get pulled in for the tasks where they apply, so you are not carrying every procedure in every request. The load-bearing point is what they have in common: all three are context and configuration. None of them modify the weights. That is also why they share one budget — the context window — and why adding a skill or a tool always has a cost measured in tokens.

      Going deeper: noting that a skill may contain scripts, and that those scripts are run by the runtime, not by the model — the same choose-versus-do split as tool calling.

      “Do skills retrain the model? What loads at runtime?”

      No, they do not retrain anything. Nothing about the model changes. At runtime, text is selected and inserted into the context window for that one request.

      The weights were frozen when training ended and are identical across users and requests. What a skill changes is the input, not the network. When the harness judges a skill relevant, it loads that skill’s instructions — and possibly references to files or scripts — into the context window for that call. When the call ends, it is gone; the model retains nothing. Same for tool definitions and retrieved memory. The direct consequence is budget: every skill, tool definition, and remembered fact you add occupies window you no longer have for retrieved evidence or the user’s own content. So skill and tool sprawl shows up as degraded retrieval quality and higher cost per call.

      Going deeper: connecting it to the only things that do change weights — pretraining and fine-tuning — and saying that if someone needs behavior baked in permanently, prompts, skills, and tools are the wrong lever.

      “How would you give an agent memory?”

      I would split “memory” into four mechanisms — working context, session transcript, long-term semantic memory, and structured state — and use structured state for anything that must be correct.

      In-context working memory is the window itself: free, exact, finite, and gone at the end of the call. Session memory is the running transcript, stored and resent each turn, eventually summarized or compacted when it outgrows the budget. Long-term semantic memory means extracting facts, storing them externally, and retrieving them by relevance — RAG over the user’s own history, good for preferences and color, never authoritative. Structured state is a database row or profile: the plan tier, the balance, the shipping address. The model is stateless, so all of this is something I build outside it and re-inject as text.

      Going deeper: the hard parts — deciding what is worth storing, resolving contradictions with recency rules or confirmation, making forgetting and correction first-class, honoring deletion and consent obligations, and accepting that retrieved memory competes for the same window as retrieved evidence.

      “How would you prototype a prompt chain for a multi-step task?”

      Decompose the task into steps with an explicit input and output contract each, do it by hand once to prove the decomposition, make every step’s output structured and validated, then test steps in isolation before wiring them together.

      The manual pass matters most: if I cannot complete the task myself in a chat window, the decomposition is wrong and engineering will not save it. Then each step gets a schema, so a bad output fails at step two rather than surfacing as nonsense at step five. I measure each step’s reliability alone, because end-to-end reliability is the product of them: five steps at 95% is about 77%, ten steps at 99% is about 90%. Shortening the chain usually beats improving a single step. Around that I add validation gates, per-step retries rather than whole-run retries, checkpoints after expensive steps, and a human approval before anything irreversible.

      Going deeper: doing the compounding arithmetic out loud, and saying where the human belongs — unattended for cheap and reversible steps, confirmation for anything that sends, pays, or deletes.

      “How does MCP compare to building one-off API integrations?”

      MCP trades bespoke control for reuse: write a server once and any compliant client can use it. For a single integration you will never reuse, a one-off is genuinely simpler.

      MCP wins on reuse, ecosystem, and swappability — existing servers for common systems, and freedom to change client or model without rewriting connectors. One-off integrations win on control and minimal dependencies: you own the payload shape, the batching, the caching, and you can optimize for your exact query pattern with nothing between you and the vendor SDK. The deciding questions are how many tools and how many surfaces. One app, one tool, one hot path: write the integration. Several surfaces, a growing tool list, or a system other teams also need to reach: write the server. And whichever you choose, the tool descriptions and the permission model are still work you have to do well.

      Going deeper: refusing the framing that one is simply better, and naming the security angle — a broadly scoped server is a broadly scoped attack surface, and auth design does not come free with the protocol.

      “How do tool/function calling work under the hood?”

      Tool definitions go into the context as text; the model predicts a structured block containing a tool name and JSON arguments instead of prose, and stops; the runtime executes it and feeds the result back as a new message.

      Mechanically there is no new capability inside the network. The definitions are serialized into the model’s input. The model has been trained to produce a specific structured format when a tool fits, and the API surfaces it as a separate field with a distinct stop reason so your code can branch on it cleanly. Some providers constrain decoding against the schema so the JSON is well-formed by construction; even then, semantic correctness is not guaranteed — a valid date in the wrong timezone is still valid JSON. The model may request several tools at once, and results come back tagged with the call identifier so the model can match result to request across a multi-step loop.

      Going deeper: the reminder that it is still token prediction, so tool choice and arguments are influenced by the descriptions, the examples, and the conversation — and that schema validity is not the same thing as being right.

      “What is MCP and why does it exist?”

      MCP is an open protocol for exposing tools, resources, and prompts to models in a standard shape. It exists because every application was writing bespoke integration code for every tool — N × M integrations, none reusable.

      A server wraps a system — a database, a repository, a file store — and advertises the tools, readable resources, and prompt templates it offers. A client, meaning the AI application, discovers them at runtime and presents them to the model as tool definitions. Because the interface is standard, the server is written once and reused by any compliant client, which turns N × M into N + M. USB-C for tools is a fair analogy once the mechanism is clear: the win is not the connector, it is that nobody ships a new cable per pair of devices. It also means swapping models or clients no longer invalidates your integration work.

      Going deeper: stating what it does not solve — description quality, permissions and auth, and agent reliability are all still yours, and a standard protocol makes none of them automatic.

      The four layers, and which two of them you own

      One model with tools in a loop is a single unit of work. Real products coordinate many units: several calls, several models, steps that run for minutes and must survive a crash. That coordination has a place in the stack. Bottom to top:

      Layer 1, infrastructure. Compute. GPUs and similar accelerators, the data centers holding them, and the serving software that turns trained weights into a fast endpoint. Running a trained model to get an output is inference; producing the weights is training. Owned by clouds and chipmakers. The cost is capital expenditure at the scale of billions, which is why almost nobody owns it. It moves fast, but the pace is set by chip fabrication and power, not software.

      Layer 2, model. The weights themselves: foundation models — large, general-purpose models trained on broad data — plus fine-tunes and smaller specialists like embedding models that turn text into vectors, rerankers, and classifiers. Owned by the frontier labs, and by your ML team if you have one. Priced per token and rented. It changes monthly, and the direction is deflationary: capability per dollar keeps rising.

      Layer 3, orchestration. Everything that decides what to send a model, in what order, with what context, and what to do with the answer: prompts and templates, chains, tool calling, retrieval, routing, memory, caching, guardrails, the evaluation harness. This is the layer PMs mostly work in. It costs engineering time plus the token spend your design causes, and it changes as fast as you decide, because you own it.

      Layer 4, application. The part a human touches: the interface, the workflow it slots into, the defaults, how errors surface, and the feedback loop that turns usage into data you can evaluate on. Owned entirely by you, paid for in design time, and worth keeping for years.

      The idea in one line. You rent layers one and two and you build layers three and four, so your defensibility has to live in the parts you build.

      That is the strategic read. Compute and weights are converging toward utilities: many suppliers, falling prices, near-identical interfaces, switching cost measured in days. What does not commoditize is knowledge of your users’ workflow, the evaluation set encoding what “good” means in your domain, the retrieval corpus, the integrations, the feedback loop, and taste in the interface. It is also the answer to the Chapter 8 question — models improve monthly, what stays constant? These two layers do.

      Other people slice this stack differently — a separate data layer, an agent-infrastructure layer, orchestration folded into the application. None of those are wrong. What an interviewer tests is not whether your diagram matches theirs, but whether your framing is coherent, every component has exactly one home in it, and you can say what follows from it.

      Chains, graphs, and the plain API call

      Layer three is yours. What do you build it out of? Three honest answers.

      What LangChain is and what it buys you

      LangChain is a software framework: a library of abstractions for building applications on language models. It gives you prompt templates (a prompt with typed holes in it), chains (an operator that pipes one step’s output into the next step’s input), retrievers (one interface over vector stores and search back ends), tool wrappers, memory objects that carry state between turns, output parsers that coerce text into structured data, and a large integration catalog. Running a chain fills your template, calls the API, parses the response, and passes it on.

      What it genuinely buys you: prototyping speed, since a retrieval-augmented question-answering demo is a dozen lines instead of a day; standard interfaces, so swapping a vector database or a provider is a config change; breadth of connectors.

      What it costs is indirection. Your prompt is assembled inside the library, so the string you wrote is not the string that was sent, and your token spend is not what you estimated. When quality drops you debug through a layer you did not write, and interfaces churn between versions. A subtler cost: a team can become expert in a framework rather than in the system, and those are not the same asset.

      The honest recommendation: excellent for exploration and for gluing many integrations together. Be deliberate before it sits in a hot production path — the code serving live traffic, where latency, cost, and debuggability all bite.

      LangChain versus LangGraph: the computational model, not the feature list

      The real difference is not the connector count. It is the shape of computation each can express. LangChain is chain-shaped: a directed sequence, step one to step two to step three, which fits when the path is known before you run anything. LangGraph models the application as a graph: explicit nodes, explicit edges including conditional ones that pick the next node from a value, and a shared state object every node can read and write.

      That change of shape unlocks what a chain cannot do cleanly: cycles, so a node can loop back and retry; conditional branching; bounded retries; interruption and resumption, so a run can pause, persist its state, and continue after a deploy; and human-in-the-loop checkpoints, where the graph waits for approval.

      The idea in one line. Use a chain when the path is known ahead of time; use a graph when the path depends on what happens at run time — which is exactly the agent condition from Chapter 5.

      When a raw SDK call beats any framework

      A raw SDK call means using the provider’s own client library directly: you build the prompt, send it, and validate the response. Choose that when the task is a single call or a short fixed sequence; when you need exact control over prompt text and token budget; when latency and cost are tight and indirection is overhead; when debuggability beats development speed; when you want few dependencies.

      Here is the point that lands in an interview, because most candidates are afraid to make it: a great many production language-model features are a well-tested prompt, a validated output schema, and one API call in a retry wrapper. Choosing that deliberately is a senior signal, not a naive one. The naive move is reaching for the framework you have heard of.

      The general trade-off of abstraction layers

      Every abstraction is the same bargain. You gain leverage and consistency: less code, faster onboarding, a paved road for the common case. You give up control and transparency, because when it fails it fails in a layer you do not own and the failure arrives translated. Then upgrade risk: the abstraction lags the underlying API, so either you wait for the wrapper or you reach around it and lose the point of having it. And the danger specific to this domain: abstractions hide the prompt — and the prompt is the product. If you cannot print the exact bytes you sent, you cannot reason about quality or cost.

      The test to apply. Does this abstraction hide something I will need to debug at 3am? If yes, keep it out of the hot path or make it log the exact request it sent. An abstraction you can see through is fine; an opaque one in the critical path is a future outage with your name on it.

      When orchestration earns its place

      Orchestration machinery is not free. It adds latency, moving parts, failure modes, and a mental model every new engineer must load before they can help. It should be triggered by something, not adopted by default. These are the triggers.

      Triggers that justify orchestration, and the smallest machinery that covers each one
      Trigger What it looks like in the product Smallest thing that covers it
      Multi-step work with dependencies Extract invoice fields, look up the vendor, draft the reply A chain
      Branching on intermediate results If it is a contract, run the clause checker; otherwise summarize A conditional edge
      Heterogeneous models per subtask Small model classifies, embedding model retrieves, strong model drafts A router
      Retries, fallbacks, compensation Tool times out, provider errors, a write must be undone A retry wrapper, then a graph
      Long-running work needing durable state A twenty-minute migration that must survive a deploy A graph with persisted state
      Parallel fan-out with a join Check one claim against six sources at once, then reconcile A graph with a join node

      The counter-rule. If you cannot name which trigger applies, you do not need it yet. Premature orchestration is a leading cause of AI features that are slow, expensive, and unexplainable when they go wrong.

      Model routing, and the fact that most requests are easy

      Model routing means classifying each incoming request and dispatching it to the cheapest model that can handle it, escalating to a stronger one when confidence is low or the first answer fails a check. Two facts make it pay. The spread inside a model family is large: cheapest and strongest differ by one to two orders of magnitude in price per token, and similarly in latency. And difficulty is very unevenly distributed — a large share of traffic is genuinely easy, so paying frontier prices for it hands your gross margin to your provider.

      The signals are mundane: task type (classify, extract, summarize, reason, write code), input length, required capability (tool use, long context, vision), risk tier (customer-facing, regulated, irreversible), customer tier, historical difficulty for similar requests. Two patterns sit on top. Escalation is deliberate: a verifier or confidence score says the cheap answer is weak, so you retry on a stronger model. Fallback is defensive: the chosen model is rate-limited or down, so you fail over to keep the feature alive.

      Designing the router: what the classifier has to be

      The constraint set is demanding, and naming it precisely is what a good answer sounds like. The classifier must be much cheaper and much faster than the models it routes to, or it eats its own savings — one costing a tenth of a frontier call and adding 400ms has already lost. So: a small fine-tuned classifier, or embeddings plus a logistic regression, not a frontier model call, because then you paid for the expensive model to decide whether to use the expensive model.

      It must be calibrated — when it says 0.8 it is right about 80 percent of the time — because otherwise a confidence threshold is a number you cannot act on. It must fail toward the stronger model: on low confidence, an unknown task type, or an error inside the router itself, the default is up, not down, because a wrong escalation costs money while a wrong demotion costs trust. It must be measurable: routing accuracy against a labeled set, escalation rate, cost per resolved request. And versioned and re-evaluated, because the request distribution drifts as users learn what your product can do.

      The honest trade. A router is a new component that can be wrong, plus latency, plus a second thing to evaluate. If your volume is small or your traffic is uniformly hard, the right answer is one good model and no router. Routing is a scale-earned optimization.

      Unit economics at millions of calls

      Do the arithmetic; this is where the argument is won. All numbers below are illustrative and rounded — the shape matters, not the digits. Ten million requests a month at $0.020 all-in on a frontier model is $200,000.

      Now route. Seventy percent to a small model at $0.0005 → $3,500. Twenty-five percent to a mid model at $0.004 → $10,000. Five percent to the frontier model at $0.020 → $10,000. The router itself at $0.00002 per request → $200. And be honest about escalations: say six percent of cheap answers come back weak and get retried on the frontier model → roughly $8,400. Total: about $32,100, a six-fold reduction — with escalations the second-largest line item. Double that rate and you have eaten a third of the saving.

      The metric that matters. Cost per resolved request, not cost per API call. A cheap model that fails and escalates charges you twice and gives the user a worse experience, so a change that lowers cost per call while raising cost per resolved request is a regression dressed up as a win.

      At scale a small per-request delta is a strategy, not a rounding error: $0.020 to $0.0032 on ten million requests a month is roughly $2 million a year, often the difference between a software-like gross margin and a services-like one. It is also why “cheap model plus orchestration” so often beats “expensive model, none” commercially.

      Many agents, one job each

      Sometimes a problem genuinely needs several agents with non-overlapping jobs — research, draft, verify — each stage needing different tools and a different definition of success. These principles separate a working system from a demo that impresses once.

      One responsibility and one success criterion per agent. If you cannot write the job and its pass condition in a sentence, the decomposition is wrong. Explicit typed contracts. Agent A does not send prose to agent B; it emits a defined structure with named fields, validated at the boundary, so a malformed handoff fails loudly at the seam instead of poisoning step four. A shared state or artifact store, not conversation. Agents write records and pass references; one growing transcript overflows the context window. A supervisor owns sequencing. One orchestrator decides who runs next and when to stop; agents negotiating with each other is nearly impossible to debug or bound. Parallelize only genuinely independent work, and design the join — the hard part is what you do when branches disagree. Per-agent step and spend budgets, where hitting either is a normal, handled outcome. Checkpoints, so a failure at step four resumes at step four.

      Now the Chapter 5 warning, with numbers. Reliability compounds multiplicatively. An agent that gets each step right 95 percent of the time finishes a ten-step task about 60 percent of the time (0.95 to the tenth power is roughly 0.60). Four such agents at eight steps each — 32 sequential steps — is roughly 19 percent. Hence the effort real systems spend on verification, checkpoints, and shrinking the step count rather than on adding agents.

      Multi-agent is usually a last resort. Every extra agent multiplies in another failure probability, another handoff that can be malformed, and more latency. The strongest answer names the specific reason one agent with more tools would not do — conflicting system prompts, tool sets that must be isolated for security, incompatible context needs, or a verifier that must not be the thing being verified.

      Why evaluation is the whole job

      In ordinary software, correctness is settled before you write code. A spec says what a function should return; a test asserts that it returns it. Two moving parts, both under your control.

      An AI feature breaks that in three places at once. The output is non-deterministic — the same input can produce different text on two consecutive calls, because the model samples from a probability distribution rather than looking up an answer. Quality is graded rather than binary — a summary can be excellent, adequate, or subtly misleading, and no equality check separates them. And often there is no single right answer: five rewrites of an email can all be good.

      So “does it work?” stops being a yes/no engineering question and becomes a measurement question: on which inputs, how often, how badly when it fails, and compared to what. Deciding what “good” means for this user, on this task, at this failure cost, is the judgment nobody else on the team is positioned to make.

      The eval set is the most durable asset the team builds. Prompts get rewritten monthly, models get swapped quarterly, the retrieval stack gets replaced. A curated set of real inputs, each with a defensible notion of what a good response looks like, survives all of it — and it is the only thing that tells you whether those changes helped. Your prompt is a draft. Your model is a vendor decision. Your definition of good is the product.

      This is why Meta asks candidates to whiteboard an evaluation framework live. Anyone can tweak a prompt until a demo impresses. Few people can say, with numbers, whether last week’s change helped.

      Measuring a system that answers differently every time

      Non-determinism does not make measurement impossible. It makes it statistical — the same shift a physics student makes when they stop reading a single instrument and start reporting a mean with an error bar.

      Five moves follow. Run n samples per input, not one: call the model three to ten times per test case and record the distribution, because a case that passes seven times out of ten is a different animal from one that passes ten out of ten, and a single run cannot tell them apart. Score with a grader matched to the output type rather than string equality. Report a pass rate with its spread — “82% ± 3” is a result, “82%” is a rumor. Pin what you can: temperature, seed if the provider exposes one, model version, prompt version, index snapshot. And accept that the metric itself has noise.

      Common misconception: any improvement in the number is an improvement in the product. Your metric carries error from the finite sample of test cases and from the model’s own randomness. If re-running the identical system twice moves the score two points, a two-point gain from your clever new prompt is not evidence of anything. The rule: a change must clear measurement noise before you believe it. Estimate that noise floor once and post it next to the dashboard forever.

      Building the framework, in build order

      Here is what to draw on the whiteboard. The value is in the ordering, so say it out loud: dataset before metric, metric before tooling. Teams that build tooling first end up with a beautiful dashboard measuring the wrong thing.

      (a) Define the user outcome and what failure costs, before any metric. “The support agent resolves the ticket without a human” is an outcome; “BLEU score” is not. Then price the failures: a clumsy sentence costs nothing, a wrong refund amount costs money, a fabricated medical claim costs a company. That ordering drives every threshold later.

      (b) Decompose the pipeline into measurable stages. You cannot debug a blended score. For a retrieval-augmented feature (Chapter 4), retrieval and generation get measured separately; for an agent, tool selection, tool arguments, and final synthesis.

      (c) Build the dataset. Real user inputs first, sampled from logs rather than invented. Stratify across the segments you care about and across difficulty, so easy cases cannot drown the hard ones. Add adversarial and edge cases deliberately: injection attempts, empty inputs, ambiguous requests, out-of-scope questions. Then carve out a frozen regression set that never changes, so today’s score is comparable to next year’s.

      (d) Define graders per stage, cheapest reliable one first — the ladder in the next section. (e) Set thresholds and a release gate. Some metrics trade off; the redlines from Chapter 3 do not, and a safety or privacy redline blocks the release however good the quality numbers look. (f) Wire it into CI, because an eval you have to remember to run is an eval you will not run. (g) Close the loop from production back into the dataset, so every real failure becomes a permanent case that can never silently return.

      The trap: a framework whose numbers nobody acts on is theater. The test is not whether it produces a dashboard. It is whether a red number has ever stopped a release.

      Handed any unfamiliar feature, that method compresses into eight lines:

      1. User outcome, and the cost of each failure mode.
      2. Stages you can measure separately.
      3. 100–300 real inputs, stratified, plus adversarial cases; freeze a regression slice.
      4. Cheapest reliable grader per stage; calibrate any judge against human labels.
      5. A baseline, and the noise floor.
      6. Thresholds and blocking redlines agreed before seeing results.
      7. CI, then a canary slice with a rollback path.
      8. Production failures wired back into the dataset.

      The testing pyramid for an AI product

      The classic pyramid says: many cheap tests at the bottom, few expensive ones at the top. It survives the move to AI almost unchanged — you just add middle layers for the things that are graded rather than asserted.

      The principle: push correctness down into the cheap deterministic layers, and spend human judgment only where it is irreplaceable. Does the JSON parse, does the citation resolve to a real document, did the guardrail block the disallowed category — all deterministic checks. Only questions like “is this explanation actually helpful to a confused customer?” deserve a person.

      Graders, and how far to trust a model as judge

      The grader ladder — climb it only as far as you must
      Grader What it can check Cost How far to trust it
      Deterministic / schema Valid JSON, required fields, forbidden strings, numeric ranges, citation resolves Near zero Completely, inside its narrow scope
      Reference-based Match against a known answer, classification accuracy, recall against labeled chunks Low High where one ground truth exists
      LLM-as-judge Rubric quality, faithfulness to a source, tone, preference between two outputs An extra model call per case As far as its measured agreement with humans
      Human review Anything, including whether the rubric itself is wrong High and slow The anchor everything else is validated against

      LLM-as-judge means using a language model, given a written rubric, to grade outputs at scale: you hand it the input, the output, sometimes a reference or the retrieved source, plus explicit criteria, and it returns a verdict. It is the only way to grade tens of thousands of free-text responses without a comparable pile of human labor.

      It works for relative comparisons (“which of these two is better?”), coarse rubric quality, faithfulness against a provided source, and triage that routes suspicious cases to humans. It fails at fine distinctions, and it carries named biases: position bias, preferring whichever answer came first; verbosity bias, mistaking length for quality; self-preference for its own model family; poor calibration, so a 7 versus an 8 means nothing stable; and sensitivity to rubric wording.

      The discipline that makes it usable: validate it against a few hundred human labels and report that agreement as a number you can be challenged on. Keep humans grading a sampled subset permanently so drift shows up. Prefer pairwise comparison with randomized order over absolute scoring. Use a different model family than the one under test. Version the rubric like code — changing it invalidates every historical score.

      Trust a model judge as a scalable proxy you have calibrated, never as ground truth you have assumed. The sentence that signals seniority: “our judge agrees with human raters 87% of the time, so I trust it for movements larger than a few points and nothing finer.”

      Precision, recall, and F1 in stakeholder language

      One feature, carried all the way through: a model that flags messages as toxic for a moderator to review. Of 10,000 messages, 200 are genuinely toxic. The model flags 100, and 80 of those flags are correct.

      In plain words: precision is, of the things we flagged, how many were right — 80 of 100, or 0.80. Recall is, of the things we should have flagged, how many we caught — 80 of 200, or 0.40. Precision is about wasting the moderator’s time and annoying innocent users. Recall is about toxic content staying up.

      Now the part that matters in a product conversation: the trade-off is a dial, not a bug. Flag aggressively and recall rises while precision falls; flag conservatively and the reverse. No setting maximizes both, so which way you turn it is a business decision about which error is more expensive. For child-safety content you buy recall. For auto-deleting a paying customer’s post you buy precision.

      F1 is the harmonic mean, 2PR/(P+R), here 0.53. It exists so you can rank systems with one number, and its limitation is that it silently assumes both errors cost the same — almost never true. If a false negative costs a hundred times a false positive, F1 is actively misleading; weight it (an Fβ score) or report the pair against a stated threshold.

      Never present precision and recall without saying which error you have chosen to make more often, and why. That sentence is the difference between reporting metrics and making a decision.

      Retrieval quality versus faithfulness

      For any retrieval-augmented feature, two independent things can go wrong and one blended “quality” score cannot tell them apart. Retrieval quality asks whether the right source material was found: measure it against known-relevant chunks with recall at k (of the relevant chunks, how many appear in the top k), precision at k (of the k results, how many are relevant), and rank-aware measures like MRR or NDCG, which reward putting the good chunk first rather than eighth. Faithfulness asks whether the answer is supported by the context given: break it into claims and check each against the retrieved text — independent of whether that text was any good.

      After launch: swaps, observability, and the feedback loop

      Catching regressions after a model swap

      Swapping the underlying model is the most dangerous routine change in an AI product, for two reasons. A new model can raise average quality while destroying one specific behavior your product depends on. And your prompts are quietly overfit to the model they were written against — phrasing, examples and format instructions were all tuned until that model complied. A different model has different habits.

      The method: run the frozen regression suite before any swap. Diff outputs on identical inputs rather than only comparing aggregate scores — read the cases that changed, not the mean. Report per segment, always. Shadow or canary the new model on real traffic before cutover. Pin an explicit version, and keep the previous one live so rollback is a config change.

      Observability: logging enough to reproduce a bad answer

      A status code says the request succeeded. It says nothing about whether the answer was good. Per request, log the full prompt actually sent including retrieved context, the model name and version, sampling parameters, every tool call with its arguments and result, token counts and cost, latency broken down by stage, the output, and any grader or guardrail verdicts — all tied together by one trace id.

      The reason is blunt: without the exact context that produced it, a bad output is unreproducible, and an unreproducible bug cannot be fixed. “A user said the summary was wrong yesterday” is not a bug report; a trace id is. On top of the logs sits monitoring: online quality proxies, cost and latency per stage, tool error and retry rates, refusal and fallback rates, and drift in the input distribution. There is also a tension to handle honestly — prompts contain user data, so full-fidelity logging collides with privacy and retention policy. The usual resolution: short retention for raw prompts, redacted identifiers, a consented subset kept longer for eval use, strict access control — decided with legal before an incident forces it.

      Human-in-the-loop feedback

      Two different things share the name. HITL as a quality gate means a human reviews or approves before the system acts — and critically, that decision is captured as a label, so the safety mechanism doubles as a labeling pipeline. HITL as a data flywheel means collecting signal to improve the system.

      Design specifics. Make the feedback control cheap and specific: “wrong facts”, “missed my question”, “too long” is worth far more than a lone thumbs-down, which tells you a user was unhappy and nothing about what to change. Capture implicit signals too — did the user edit the output, retry, abandon, or accept and send it? Those are higher-volume and less biased, because only annoyed people click thumbs-down. Route flagged cases to reviewers with the full trace attached. And make labels flow into the eval set and the roadmap.

      The failure mode is collecting feedback nobody ever uses. A thumbs-down button writing to a table no one queries is worse than no button, because it looks like a feedback system and quietly buys the team permission to stop thinking.

      Diagnosis: when quality drops, and when the evals lied

      Step 2 includes their version, not just yours: providers update models behind an alias, so a system nobody touched can degrade overnight — the argument for pinning. And step 6 catches boring causes that masquerade as quality problems: truncated context, a silent fallback to a smaller model when the primary rate-limits, a retrieval timeout returning zero chunks and a confidently ungrounded answer.

      Offline evals passed but users are unhappy

      This is normal, not a scandal: offline evals are a filter for obvious breakage, not proof of user value. Diagnose in order. Suspect the eval set first — wrong distribution, too easy, stale. Then suspect you are measuring the wrong construct, optimizing a proxy rather than the outcome users care about. Then look outside the model: latency, UX, expectation setting, error handling, how failure is presented. Then segment, because an aggregate pass hides a badly served cohort. Then check for a production-versus-eval gap: different prompt template, different retrieval config, unpinned model version, context that only exists in the real request path. Whatever you find becomes a permanent case in the eval set — that is how the gap narrows.

      “How would you eval this feature before launch?”

      Start from the user outcome and the cost of failure, build a dataset of real inputs before I pick any metric, grade each pipeline stage with the cheapest reliable grader, then gate launch on thresholds plus hard redlines — and canary it rather than trusting offline numbers alone.

      Concretely: define what success means for the user and what each failure mode costs. Decompose the feature into measurable stages. Assemble 100–300 real inputs sampled from logs, stratified across segments and difficulty, plus deliberate adversarial and edge cases, and freeze part of it as a regression set. Choose graders per stage — deterministic and schema checks first, reference-based where ground truth exists, a model judge with a written rubric for graded quality, humans as the calibration anchor. Establish a baseline and a noise floor by running the unchanged system repeatedly. Set launch thresholds and redlines with stakeholders before I see any results. Run it in CI, then canary on a small traffic slice with online metrics and a rollback path.

      Going deeper: naming the baseline and the noise floor unprompted, and saying explicitly that offline evals are a filter for breakage rather than evidence of user value — which is why the canary is part of the pre-launch plan, not an afterthought.

      “Whiteboard an evaluation framework from scratch”

      Seven boxes in build order: user outcome and failure cost, pipeline decomposition, dataset, graders per stage, thresholds and release gate, CI, and a loop from production back into the dataset. The ordering is the answer — dataset before metric, metric before tooling.

      I draw the pipeline first so the stages are visible, then hang a grader off each stage. The dataset gets the most whiteboard space, because that is where the judgment lives: real inputs sampled from logs, stratified across the segments and difficulty levels I care about, adversarial cases added on purpose, and a frozen regression slice so today’s number is comparable to next quarter’s. Graders climb a cost ladder and I stop as low as I can. Thresholds separate into tradeable metrics and hard redlines that block a release outright. CI makes it automatic. The return arrow from production makes it compound, because every real failure becomes a permanent case.

      Going deeper: saying out loud that a framework whose numbers nobody acts on is theater, and offering the test for it — has a red number ever stopped a release here?

      “What is LLM-as-judge and when do you trust it?”

      Using a model with a written rubric to grade outputs at scale. I trust it as a proxy I have calibrated against human labels, and I report that agreement as a number — never as ground truth I have assumed.

      It earns its place on relative comparisons, coarse rubric-based quality, faithfulness against a provided source, and high-volume triage into a human queue. It is unreliable at fine distinctions and carries known biases: position bias toward whichever answer came first, verbosity bias toward long confident text, self-preference for its own model family, poor calibration between adjacent scores, and real sensitivity to rubric wording. The discipline: validate against a few hundred human labels and publish the agreement rate, keep humans grading a permanent sampled subset to catch drift, prefer pairwise comparisons with randomized order over absolute scores, use a different model family than the one under test, and version the rubric like code because changing it invalidates history.

      Going deeper: quoting a concrete agreement number and then deriving the resolution limit from it — if the judge agrees with humans 87% of the time, it cannot adjudicate a two-point difference.

      “Precision, recall, F1: explain them to a stakeholder”

      Precision is: of the things we flagged, how many were right. Recall is: of the things we should have flagged, how many we caught. They trade off against each other, and choosing where to sit is a business decision about which mistake is more expensive.

      Worked example: 10,000 messages, 200 truly toxic. We flag 100 and 80 are correct. Precision is 80/100 = 0.80 — four in five flags are justified. Recall is 80/200 = 0.40 — we miss three in five toxic messages. F1 blends them, 2(0.8)(0.4)/1.2 = 0.53, and its purpose is ranking systems with one number. Its limitation is that it assumes a false positive and a false negative cost the same, which is almost never true. If missing toxic content costs a hundred times more than an unnecessary review, F1 points the wrong way, and I would weight it or report the pair against a stated threshold instead. Lowering the threshold moves us up the recall axis and down the precision axis; that dial is ours to set.

      Going deeper: never presenting the pair without stating which error you have chosen to make more often and why, in the language of cost to the business.

      “How do you eval a non-deterministic system?”

      Stop measuring a point and start measuring a distribution: run n samples per input, report a pass rate with its spread, pin everything you can pin, and only believe a change once it clears measurement noise.

      Concretely: for each test case run the model several times and record the outcome distribution, because a case that passes 7/10 is a different risk from one that passes 10/10 and a single run cannot tell them apart. Score with a grader matched to the output type rather than string equality. Report the pass rate with a confidence interval, or at least the observed spread. Freeze the variables you control — temperature, seed if available, model version, prompt version, index snapshot — so fewer things can explain a move. Then establish the noise floor by running the unchanged system several times and seeing how much the score wobbles on its own. That becomes the bar: a change smaller than the noise floor is not a result, no matter how good the story around it is.

      Going deeper: volunteering that your own metric is noisy and that you have quantified it. Most candidates treat the eval as an oracle and worry only about the model’s randomness.

      “What’s your testing pyramid for an AI product?”

      Wide base of fast deterministic tests, schema validation and guardrails; a middle layer of component evals on retrieval, tool selection and individual prompts; a narrower layer of end-to-end scenario evals; a thin top of human review and online experiments.

      The principle is unchanged from ordinary software: push as much correctness as possible down into the cheap deterministic layers, and spend expensive human judgment only where it is genuinely irreplaceable. Does the JSON parse, is the required field present, does the citation resolve to a real document, did the guardrail block the disallowed category — all of that belongs at the base and costs milliseconds. Component evals let me attribute a failure to a stage. End-to-end scenario evals check that the stages compose into the user outcome. Human review and online experimentation sit at the top because they are the only things that can tell me the rubric itself is wrong.

      Going deeper: pointing out that most AI teams have an inverted pyramid — all vibes at the top, nothing at the base — and that the first week of work on a struggling AI product is usually building the base, not tuning the prompt.

      “How do you catch regressions after a model swap?”

      A frozen regression suite run before the swap, output diffing on identical inputs, per-segment reporting, then a canary or shadow deployment with the old version kept live for rollback.

      A model swap is uniquely dangerous because a new model can improve average quality while breaking one specific behavior the product depends on, and because prompts are quietly overfit to the model they were written against. So I do not compare aggregate scores alone. I diff the actual outputs on the same inputs and read the cases that changed. I report every metric per segment, because an average hides a broken cohort. I shadow the new model on real traffic, or canary a small slice, before cutover. In production I pin an explicit model version with a documented upgrade path, and I keep the previous version live so rollback is a config change rather than a project.

      Going deeper: naming the trap concretely — aggregate went up four points so we shipped, and the workflow generating most of the revenue silently collapsed. Per-segment analysis is what makes an aggregate safe to read at all.

      “What is observability for AI products?”

      Logging enough per request to reproduce any output exactly — the full prompt actually sent including retrieved context, model and version, sampling parameters, tool calls with arguments and results, tokens and cost, per-stage latency, the output, and grader verdicts, all under one trace id.

      The reason is that without the exact context that produced it, a bad output is unreproducible and therefore undebuggable. A status code tells you the call succeeded and nothing about whether the answer was any good. On top of the logs sits monitoring: online quality proxies, cost and latency broken down by stage, tool error and retry rates, refusal and fallback rates, and drift in the input distribution. There is a real tension here, because prompts contain user data — so I want short retention on raw prompts, redaction of identifiers, a consented longer-lived subset for eval use, and access control, decided with legal in advance rather than during an incident.

      Going deeper: the trace id and the privacy trade-off. Anyone can list fields to log; connecting the chain end to end and admitting that full-fidelity logging conflicts with retention policy is the senior move.

      “How would you design a human-in-the-loop feedback system?”

      Decide first whether the human is a quality gate in the flow or a source of improvement signal, then design so that every human decision is captured as a label and flows back into the eval set and the roadmap.

      As a gate: a reviewer approves before the system acts, and the approve/reject/edit decision is logged as a label with the full trace attached — the safety mechanism doubles as a labeling pipeline. As a flywheel: make explicit feedback cheap and specific, so users pick “wrong facts” or “missed my question” instead of a bare thumbs-down that tells me nothing actionable. Then instrument implicit signals — edits, retries, abandonment, acceptance — because they are far higher volume and less biased than explicit ratings, which only annoyed users leave. Route flagged cases to reviewers with full context so review costs seconds. And close the loop: labels become eval cases and prioritized work.

      Going deeper: naming the failure mode — collecting feedback nobody ever uses. A thumbs-down writing to a table no one queries is worse than no button, because it buys the team false comfort.

      “Offline evals passed but users are unhappy. What now?”

      Assume the eval set is wrong before assuming the users are. Check representativeness first, then whether I am measuring the wrong construct, then everything outside the model, then segments, then a production-versus-eval environment gap.

      In order: is the eval distribution actually like real traffic, or is it too easy or stale? Am I optimizing a proxy rather than the outcome users care about — accuracy when they wanted a single-turn answer, fluency when they wanted brevity? Is the unhappiness even about model quality, or is it latency, unclear expectations, bad error handling, or a UI that presents failure badly? Does an aggregate pass hide a cohort that is served badly? And is there a gap between eval and production — different prompt template, different retrieval config, an unpinned model version, context that only exists in the real request path? Whatever I find becomes a permanent case in the eval set.

      Going deeper: framing the gap as expected rather than embarrassing. Offline evals are a filter for obvious breakage, not proof of user value; the job is to shrink the gap case by case, not to be surprised by it.

      “How do you eval faithfulness vs retrieval quality?”

      Separately, always. Retrieval gets recall at k, precision at k and rank-aware measures like MRR or NDCG against known-relevant chunks. Faithfulness gets claim-level checking against the retrieved context, independent of whether that context was any good.

      The separation buys a diagnosis rather than a grade. Bad retrieval with faithful generation means the model did its job and the index failed — fix chunking, embeddings, k, or filters. Good retrieval with unfaithful generation means the answer was sitting in the context and the model invented something else — fix the grounding instructions, force citations, or change the model. Bad on both means fix the index first and re-measure, because you cannot judge generation on garbage input. For faithfulness I decompose the answer into individual claims and check each against the retrieved text, which also gives me a per-claim failure rate rather than a vague score.

      Going deeper: stating plainly that a single blended quality score tells you which of those four situations you are in — never. That is the whole reason for the split.

      “What would you check first when quality drops?”

      First that the drop is real and not measurement noise or a dashboard bug. Then what changed and when — my prompt, my retrieval, my code, or the provider’s model version.

      Then in order: did the input distribution change rather than the system, which is a different problem entirely; segment to see whether it is everywhere or in one cohort; decompose by stage to localize it to retrieval, tool calls, or generation; and check infrastructure symptoms that masquerade as quality problems — timeouts, truncated context, rate limits, silent fallbacks to a smaller model. Providers update models behind an alias, so a system nobody touched can degrade overnight, which is the argument for pinning versions and keeping a frozen regression suite you can rerun on demand to separate “the world changed” from “we changed”.

      Going deeper: saying why the runbook exists. Under pressure the default move is to start rewriting the prompt, which is simultaneously the least likely cause and the change hardest to attribute afterward, because it alters everything at once.

      “Meta: Whiteboard an evaluation framework for an AI feature”

      I would draw the pipeline, hang a grader off each stage, and talk through seven steps in build order — outcome, stages, dataset, graders, thresholds, CI, production loop — and say up front that the dataset is the deliverable and the metric comes second.

      Pick their feature and make it concrete rather than abstract. State the user outcome and the cost of each failure mode. Draw the stages so failures are attributable. Then spend most of the time on the dataset: real inputs from logs, stratified by segment and difficulty, adversarial cases added deliberately, a frozen regression slice. Ladder the graders from deterministic checks up to human review, and calibrate any model judge against human labels. Split thresholds into tradeable metrics and blocking redlines. Wire it into CI so every prompt or model change runs it. Close the loop from production failures back into the dataset. Finish with what would make me distrust the framework: no noise floor, no per-segment view, and no red number that has ever blocked a ship.

      Going deeper: talking about organizational behavior, not just architecture. Who owns the eval set, who is allowed to change the rubric, and what happens when the gate goes red on a date the company has already announced.

      Worked example: summarize my unread messages

      The same eight moves answer the Microsoft and Google versions of this question.

      1. User and job. Take the heavy case: 40 to 200 unread messages across 25 threads after a vacation. The job is not “read my mail,” it is “tell me what needs me, in what order, and let me act.” That turns a summarizer into a triage tool.

      Clarify “unread.” Unread is a lie in most systems — a preview pane, a notification or a sync bug marks messages read. Define it as arrived since the last session, in threads the user participates in, excluding bulk. A product decision, and it moves quality more than model choice does.

      2. Quality bar and cost of failure. A good summary is not shorter text; it is who needs what from me, by when. The bar is recall on action items and deadlines, not fluency. The expensive failure is a missed obligation, so an unsure model shows the original rather than guessing, and every claim links to its source message.

      3. Data flow, and the hard parts. Input size varies by three orders of magnitude — two lines in one thread, 80 messages in another — so no single prompt shape works. Clean threads first: strip quoted history (the “> On Tuesday you wrote” blocks that duplicate the thread inside every reply) and signatures. On a long thread, summarize the old part once, cache it, and feed only new messages plus that summary.

      Ordering, and when not to summarize. Rank with a cheap explainable score, not a model: is the user directly addressed, is a question aimed at them, is there a deadline, how important is the sender. Summarize the top three to five; the rest get one line each, because a wall of summaries is another inbox. Under roughly 40 words, show the thread verbatim.

      4. Model and privacy. Start with a hosted frontier model. But personal messages are the most sensitive data most people own, so the design must answer: is content sent to a third party, retained, trained on, can the user switch it off. Zero-retention terms in writing, in-region processing, metadata logged rather than content. If those terms are unavailable, that is the real case for self-hosting open weights.

      5 and 6. Freshness, latency, cost. Cache keyed on (user, thread, last message id). Precompute on arrival rather than on app open: summarize when the user taps and you owe them two to four seconds; summarize on receipt and you owe a 150 ms cache read — the biggest lever here. For cost, five threads per active user per day at roughly 3,000 input and 200 output tokens each, at illustrative prices near $3 and $15 per million tokens, is about six cents a day: survivable in a $12 subscription, fatal on a free tier.

      7. Evaluation, and when it is wrong. The eval set is 200 threads with human-written summaries, scored for action-item recall. Assume errors: a one-tap “this was wrong” writes to that set, low confidence renders the original rather than a number, and the feature takes no actions — a wrong summary is recoverable, a wrong auto-archive is not.

      8. The v1 you actually ship. Direct messages and threads where the user is on the To line, top three threads, one model call each, precomputed on arrival, extractive fallback of the two latest lines on failure, one surface, paid tier only. Cut: the learned priority model (a hand-written score is 80% as good and debuggable), per-user style tuning (no eval set yet), the cross-thread digest (a different product), attachment reading (cost, privacy). None is on the path to learning whether triage-by-summary is worth anything.

      Latency, quality, cost — pick two, then cheat

      These three form a triangle: fast and good is expensive, cheap and good is slow, fast and cheap is worse. The senior move is to name which corner this feature sacrifices, and why.

      Then the levers and their direction. A bigger model buys quality and costs latency and money. Longer prompts and deeper retrieval buy quality and cost both. Every extra sequential call adds its full latency, so two 800 ms calls in a row is a 1.6 second feature. Capping output length cuts latency and cost together, because output tokens are the slow, expensive ones. Batching raises throughput per GPU and lowers cost per request, at a little latency. Caching at several layers — response, retrieved chunks, prompt prefix — cuts cost and latency with no quality loss, which is why it is built first.

      The idea in one line. Real latency is how long the work takes. Perceived latency is how long the user waits for something useful. Streaming, optimistic UI and precomputation change the second without touching the first — usually the highest-leverage move available.

      Then the discipline: budget backward from the experience, not forward from what you built. Decide the card must appear in 300 ms, allocate 50 ms network and 30 ms cache read, and anything that cannot fit its slice gets precomputed or removed. Measuring what you built and calling it the budget is how products end up with a four-second p95 nobody owns.

      Buy, fine-tune, build — and when not to use a model at all

      The default is buy. A hosted frontier model and a careful prompt is the cheapest way to find out whether the feature is worth anything. Most AI features die on “do users care,” not on “is the model good enough.”

      Fine-tune when prompting keeps drifting off a required format, or to test whether a small cheap model can match a big one on a narrow task. It means continuing training on a few hundred of your own examples, so behavior lives in the weights, not in every prompt. Preconditions: an eval set that can prove it won, and a retraining treadmill you now own when the base model moves. It teaches behavior, not facts — if the model lacks your data, use retrieval.

      Build or self-host only when something forces it: residency rules, volume where per-token pricing loses to owning hardware, or a latency floor an API cannot meet. Be honest about total cost of ownership: the model is rarely the expensive part — the data pipeline, evals, monitoring and the engineers maintaining them are. Then: differentiator, or table stakes? Rent table stakes; own what customers choose you for.

      Choosing a model for a task is the same reasoning, smaller. Write down what the task needs — reasoning or formatting, 8k of context or 200k, reliable JSON and tool calls, latency budget, cost ceiling, residency, license — then run your own eval set across three candidates and take the cheapest that clears the bar. Public benchmarks are a shortlist, never a decision.

      Common misconception. “Use the best model everywhere.” That spends ten times the money to reach the same quality on the 90% of requests that were easy. Route by difficulty: small model by default, escalate on low confidence.

      When you should not use an LLM at all

      The strongest senior signal in the round: you are solving the problem, not deploying the technology. No LLM when the task is deterministic with a correct answer — arithmetic, sorting, date math, validation, written business rules. Not when the question is really a database query. Not when the latency budget is single-digit milliseconds, or volume makes per-call cost absurd. Not where you need auditability and reproducibility, as in a regulated decision. And not where a regex, a lookup table or a small classical model already works, especially when labels are abundant and crisp.

      The idea in one line. An LLM is the right tool for ambiguous language tasks, and an expensive, slow, unreliable tool for anything with a known algorithm.

      Churn prediction, and why trees win on tables

      Churn prediction is supervised binary classification: years of rows, one per customer per month, with features like tenure, plan, logins, tickets and invoice failures, and a crisp label. Abundant labels, structured input, a number out. That is the shape a gradient-boosted decision tree (XGBoost, LightGBM) is built for.

      A tree splits on thresholds — “logins < 3” — so heterogeneous units and types need no scaling or encoding. Real tabular signal is largely threshold-and-interaction effects (“invoice failed and logins collapsed”), which two splits capture natively and a text model must infer from a sentence. Boosting adds hundreds of small corrective trees, giving robustness to irrelevant features and missing values. And it is trained on your outcome, so the output is a calibrated probability plus feature importances you can show a regulator. An LLM sees the row as a sentence: no calibration, orders of magnitude more cost, slower, drifts across versions, never trained on your label.

      What a hybrid system looks like

      Generalize it: a classical or deterministic core makes the decision, and LLMs sit at the edges where language lives — parsing messy input in, generating explanation out, handling the ambiguous residual. Fraud review: a trained scorer decides, an LLM drafts the analyst memo from the free-text note. Support routing: a small classifier takes the obvious 85%, the LLM takes the rest, and the rule is written down — “classifier confidence below 0.8 escalates.” Analytics: an LLM writes SQL and the database computes the numbers. The principle: put the LLM where ambiguity lives, and keep it out of any path where correctness is checkable.

      The cost floor: GPUs, tokens, and ten million users

      Deep learning runs on GPUs because training and inference are dominated by large matrix multiplications, and every element of the result can be computed independently. A CPU has a few very fast general cores, ideal for branchy sequential logic; a GPU has thousands of simple cores plus roughly an order of magnitude more memory bandwidth, which is what thousands of parallel multiply-accumulates need. Generating tokens is usually bandwidth-bound rather than arithmetic-bound: each token streams the weights past the compute units. That is why weights must fit in VRAM, the GPU’s own memory — spilling to system memory collapses throughput.

      Separate two costs. Training is huge, one-time and capital-like: thousands of GPUs for weeks. Inference is small per unit, recurring, and scales with usage, so it lands in gross margin every month. Serving economics are batching and utilization: a decode step barely uses arithmetic capacity while it waits on memory, so many requests together cost almost the same as one, and cost per request falls steeply with batch size. Renting per token means someone else eats idle capacity and you pay a markup; owning hardware means paying for 3am.

      Common misconception. That an estimation question wants the right number. It wants your structure and your assumptions, said out loud, and a sanity check at the end.

      The method for “estimate inference cost for 10M users”: state assumptions; funnel from total users to actives to requests per active per day; estimate input and output tokens, remembering that input carries the system prompt, tool definitions, history and retrieved context and therefore usually dominates; price input and output separately, because output costs several times more; multiply to daily and monthly. Then the forgotten multipliers: retries, chains and agent loops, and eval traffic. Then check against revenue, and if inference eats 40% of it, name the levers — cache, route to a smaller model, shorten context, precompute, rate-limit the heaviest 1%.

      Failure, redlines, and what survives the next model

      Assume every model output is untrusted input, and validate it like a form submitted by a stranger on the internet. Schema-validate every tool argument and every structured output; on failure retry once or twice with the validation error fed back, then fall back rather than loop. Timeout every hop with a defined fallback. Give every side-effecting call an idempotency key, and a confirmation gate before anything irreversible. Cap steps and spend per task. Add a circuit breaker that degrades to a simpler non-AI path. And fail honestly: “I could not read that document” beats a confident summary of a document the model never saw.

      Redlines, in a design frame, means saying where the guardrail sits. Some categories are absolute and no business case reopens them: help creating weapons capable of mass casualties, child sexual abuse material, targeted harassment, a real person’s likeness used for deception. Everything else is a threshold set by cost of failure. The mechanics: input filtering, output filtering, tool-level permissions for what the system can do — which matters far more than what it can say — and approval gates on the highest-stakes actions. Test adversarially with a red-team set you re-run every release, and name the owner: safety or legal holds the block, not the PM. And the honest trade-off: guardrails add latency, cost and false positives, so tune the threshold against the cost of each error type.

      What stays constant when models improve monthly

      Almost everything that matters. The user problem does not change when a model improves, nor does the workflow around it. Your eval set and your definition of quality are durable and they compound — they are how you know a new model is better for you. So are proprietary data and the feedback loop, distribution and trust, the orchestration layer where most of the product lives, and your judgment about where AI belongs. Disposable: a specific prompt, a specific model, a specific framework, and any advantage that consists only of having called the API first.

      “Design an AI feature: summarize unread messages”

      The job is triage, not summarization: tell me what needs me, in what order, with a link to the original. So I rank threads first, summarize only the top few, precompute on message arrival, and fall back to raw text whenever the model is unsure.

      I would clarify “unread” first — I would define it as arrived since the last session, in threads the user is part of, excluding bulk. Quality is measured as recall on action items and deadlines, because the expensive failure is a missed obligation, not an inelegant sentence. Pipeline: assemble the thread and strip quoted history and signatures, score threads with a cheap explainable rule (directly addressed, question aimed at the user, deadline present, sender importance), summarize the top three to five, show anything under 40 words verbatim, cache on (user, thread, last message id), and precompute when the message lands so app-open is a 150 ms cache read instead of a three-second wait. Cost is roughly six cents per active user per day at illustrative prices, so v1 is paid-tier only. Privacy is a first-class requirement: zero retention, in-region processing, no training on content, easy opt-out.

      Going deeper: naming the input-size spread of three orders of magnitude, refusing to summarize short threads at all, and stating that the feature takes no actions — a wrong summary is recoverable, a wrong auto-archive is not.

      “Latency vs quality vs cost: how do you trade them off?”

      I set a budget backward from the experience, pick which corner this feature is allowed to sacrifice, and spend first on the moves that improve perceived latency, because those are close to free.

      The three form a triangle: fast and good is expensive, cheap and good is slow. The levers and their directions: model size buys quality at the cost of both others; prompt and retrieval length buy quality at the cost of both; every sequential model call adds its full latency; capping output length cuts latency and cost together; batching cuts cost per request and adds a little latency; caching cuts cost and latency with no quality loss. Then the distinction that matters most: real latency is how long the work takes, perceived latency is how long until the user sees something useful. Streaming, optimistic UI and precomputation move the second without touching the first. So the order is: cache, stream, precompute, then shorten context, then route easy requests to a smaller model, and only then consider a bigger model.

      Going deeper: budgets in milliseconds per stage derived from the UX, quoted at p95 rather than average, with a named component that has to be precomputed because it cannot fit its slice.

      “Build vs buy vs finetune: walk me through the call”

      Default to buying a hosted frontier model with a good prompt, because it is the cheapest way to learn whether the feature is worth anything. Fine-tune only when I have an eval set proving it wins on format or on cost at equal quality. Build only when residency, volume or latency forces it.

      Fine-tuning is right for consistent format and style, or to check whether a small cheap model can match a big one on one narrow task — and it teaches behavior, not facts, so if the gap is knowledge the answer is retrieval instead. It also creates a retraining treadmill every time the base model moves. Self-hosting is justified by hard constraints, not by pride: data that legally cannot leave, volume where per-token pricing loses to owning hardware, or a latency floor an API cannot hit. On total cost of ownership, the model is rarely the expensive part — the data pipeline, evals, monitoring and the people are. Then the strategic frame: is this a differentiator or table stakes? Rent table stakes, own what customers choose you for.

      Going deeper: making the eval set the precondition for every move. Without one you cannot tell whether the fine-tune helped, so you are buying a maintenance burden on a hunch.

      “When do you not use an LLM at all?”

      Whenever the task has a known algorithm or a checkable correct answer. An LLM is the right tool for ambiguous language, and an expensive, slow, unreliable tool for anything else.

      Concretely: arithmetic, sorting, date math, validation and explicit business rules go in code. Questions about your data are a database query. If the latency budget is single-digit milliseconds, no network model call fits. If volume makes per-call cost absurd relative to the value of the call, it is not viable. If the decision must be auditable and reproducible — credit, medical, hiring — you need something that returns the same answer twice and can be explained to a regulator. If a regex, a lookup table or a small classical model already solves it, that is the answer, especially when labels are abundant and crisp. The practical version of this discipline is the hybrid design: classical core for the decision, LLM at the edges for language.

      Going deeper: volunteering a case in your own product where you removed a model. Saying “we replaced the classifier prompt with twelve lines of rules and quality went up” lands harder than any architecture.

      “Why XGBoost over an LLM for tabular prediction?”

      Because tabular prediction is a supervised problem with crisp labels, and a boosted tree is trained directly on your outcome — so it wins on accuracy, cost, latency, calibration and auditability at once.

      The mechanism matters. Trees split on thresholds, so heterogeneous units and types need no scaling or encoding. Real tabular signal is mostly threshold-and-interaction effects, which two splits capture natively. Boosting adds hundreds of small corrective trees, giving robustness to irrelevant features and to missing values. Because it is fit to your label, the output is a calibrated probability you can threshold on, with feature importances you can defend. An LLM sees the row as a sentence, produces a probability-shaped string that is not a measured frequency, costs orders of magnitude more per prediction, is slower, drifts when the provider updates, and was never trained on your outcome.

      Going deeper: saying why trees fit tables rather than asserting it, then immediately naming where the LLM does belong — converting unstructured text into features that feed the tree.

      “What does a hybrid system look like in practice?”

      A deterministic or classical core makes the decision, and LLMs sit at the edges where language lives: parsing messy input, generating explanation, and handling the ambiguous residual.

      Three shapes. Churn: an LLM turns tickets and call transcripts into features, a boosted tree produces the probability, a fixed threshold picks the playbook, and an LLM drafts the outreach. Support routing: a small classifier handles the obvious 85% and the LLM handles the rest, with the explicit routing rule “classifier confidence below 0.8 escalates.” Analytics: an LLM writes SQL, the database computes the numbers, and the query is shown to the user, so the model never does arithmetic. In each case the routing rule is written down, not implied, and the number that drives money never comes out of a language model.

      Going deeper: naming the routing rule and its threshold, plus what happens when the LLM edge fails — the classical core keeps working with slightly worse features rather than the feature going dark.

      “Role of GPUs in your cost structure?”

      Training is capital-like: huge and one-time. Inference is recurring and scales with usage, so it sits directly in gross margin — and its unit cost is set by batching and utilization.

      Serving a token is mostly memory-bandwidth-bound: the weights have to stream through the compute units, so arithmetic capacity sits idle while memory delivers. That is why batching many requests together costs almost the same as serving one and cost per request drops steeply with batch size, and why utilization is the whole game. Renting per token from an API means someone else carries idle capacity and you pay a markup, with cost that tracks usage exactly. Owning GPUs flips it: cheaper at high steady volume, but you pay for 3am and for every reserved instance nobody used. As a PM the number I care about is cost per active user per month against revenue per active user per month, and the levers are caching, routing to smaller models, shorter context, and precomputation.

      Going deeper: connecting it to gross margin rather than to hardware trivia, and noting that inference cost per token has fallen fast and repeatedly, so a feature that is marginal today may be fine in a year.

      “Estimate the inference cost for 10M users”

      I will state assumptions out loud and funnel. 10M registered, 20% daily active gives 2M DAU, three requests each gives 6M requests a day, 4,000 input and 400 output tokens each, at roughly $3 and $15 per million tokens gives about $108,000 a day, so call it $3.2M a month and about $4.5M with retries, chains and eval traffic.

      Every number there is illustrative and round, and I would flag that. The two places people go wrong: forgetting that input includes the system prompt, tool definitions, conversation history and retrieved context and therefore usually dominates the token count; and forgetting the multipliers — retries, agent loops that turn one user action into five calls, and your own evaluation runs. Then the sanity check, which is the actual point: if 10% of users convert at $10 a month, that is $10M of monthly revenue and inference is 45% of it, which does not ship. So the levers: cache aggressively, route easy requests to a small model, shorten context, precompute on write instead of on read, cap steps per task, and rate-limit the heaviest 1%.

      Going deeper: pricing input and output separately, and ending on unit economics rather than on a total. The number is a means to “does this business work.”

      “How do you design for failure (bad tool calls, timeouts)?”

      I treat every model output as untrusted input from a stranger. Schema-validate every tool argument and every structured output, retry once or twice with the validation error fed back, then fall back to something simpler and honest.

      Per failure mode: malformed output gets schema validation plus a bounded repair retry; a bad tool call gets argument validation, allowlisted tools and a dry-run for anything destructive; timeouts get an explicit limit at every hop with a defined fallback rather than an error page; duplicate side effects get idempotency keys so a retry cannot send the email twice; irreversible actions get a human confirmation gate; runaway loops get step and spend caps; and a provider outage or error spike trips a circuit breaker that degrades to a non-AI path, such as keyword search instead of retrieval or the raw message instead of the summary. Finally the UI has to fail honestly — “I could not read that file” beats a confident summary of a file that was never read.

      Going deeper: bounding the retry. Unbounded self-correction loops are how a $0.02 request becomes a $6 request, so retries carry a counter and a spend cap.

      “Where do you draw the safety redlines?”

      A small set of categories is absolute and no business case reopens them. Everything else is a threshold I set from the cost of the failure, enforced in the architecture rather than in the prompt.

      Absolute: help with weapons capable of mass casualties, child sexual abuse material, targeted harassment, and generating a real person’s likeness or voice for deception. Below that, the mechanics decide the outcome: input filtering for obvious abuse, output filtering for what the model produced, tool-level permissions for what it can actually do — which matters much more than what it can say — and human approval gates on the highest-stakes actions. I test them adversarially with a red-team set that gets re-run every release, and I name the owner: a safety or legal reviewer holds the block and the PM does not overrule it. And I state the trade-off, because pretending it is free is the weak answer: guardrails add latency and cost, and their false positives frustrate legitimate users, so the threshold is tuned against the cost of each error type.

      Going deeper: putting the strongest guardrail on tools rather than on text. Restricting what the system can do is enforceable; instructing a model not to misbehave is a preference.

      “Models improve monthly. What stays constant?”

      The user problem, your eval set and definition of quality, your proprietary data and feedback loop, your distribution and trust, and the orchestration layer around the model. The prompt and the model are disposable.

      Any advantage that consists only of having called the API first evaporates on the next release. What compounds is the loop: users produce data, data improves the eval set and the product, the product attracts users. That is why I architect for swappability — every model call goes behind one internal interface, model versions are pinned so nobody silently upgrades me, and the eval set is the contract a candidate model has to pass. The payoff is that a better model becomes an upgrade I take on a Tuesday rather than a rewrite I fear, and I can benefit from a competitor of my provider without touching product code.

      Going deeper: the inversion — if a 10× better model would kill your product, your product was the model. Say what you would still own in that world.

      “LLM or ML model for churn prediction? Why?”

      A gradient-boosted tree, not an LLM. Churn is supervised binary classification over structured history with abundant labels, and a tree beats an LLM on accuracy, cost, latency, calibration, explainability and reproducibility.

      The features are tenure, plan, logins, tickets, invoice failures; the label is cancelled or not; you have years of both. A tree is trained on that label directly, so it emits a calibrated probability you can threshold at a business-chosen cut, plus feature importances that survive an audit. An LLM would cost orders of magnitude more per prediction, be slower, produce an uncalibrated number, and change behavior when the provider updates. The hybrid is the answer worth giving: use an LLM on the parts that are language — turning support tickets and call transcripts into sentiment, complaint-category and escalation features — and let those feed the tree. Then use it again at the end to write the account manager a readable explanation.

      Going deeper: that the interesting question is not model choice but whether the prediction changes any action. A perfectly calibrated churn score with no retention playbook attached is a dashboard, not a product.

      “How do you decide which model to use for a task?”

      I write down what the task needs, shortlist on those requirements, then run my own eval set across candidates and pick the cheapest model that clears the quality bar.

      The requirements list: does the task need reasoning or just fluent formatting; how much context does it need; must it emit valid structured output and call tools reliably; what is the latency budget and the cost ceiling per call; where may the data be processed; and is the license and provider stability acceptable. Public benchmarks give a shortlist and nothing more — they are contaminated, averaged over tasks that are not mine, and measured with prompts I would not use. In production I expect a portfolio, not a winner: a small fast model for classification and extraction, a mid-tier model for the default path, a frontier model behind a router for the hard minority, and everything pinned to a version behind one interface so swapping is a config change.

      Going deeper: treating cheapest-that-clears as the objective rather than best-available, and having a routing rule that decides at runtime which bucket a request falls into.

      “Nvidia: Explain how GPUs are used in deep learning”

      Training and inference are dominated by large matrix multiplications, where every output element can be computed independently. A GPU has thousands of simple cores and very high memory bandwidth, which fits that shape; a CPU has a few fast general cores, which does not.

      In training, a forward pass is a chain of matrix multiplies and elementwise functions, and backpropagation is another chain of matrix multiplies computing gradients, run over batches for weeks across thousands of GPUs connected by fast interconnects because the model and the data do not fit on one device. In inference, prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel and is compute-heavy, while decoding produces one token at a time and is usually memory-bandwidth-bound: each token requires streaming the weights past the compute units. That is why the weights must fit in VRAM — spilling to system memory collapses throughput — and why batching many concurrent requests is nearly free and is the core economics of serving.

      Going deeper: naming memory bandwidth rather than only core count, and separating prefill from decode. That distinction is exactly why input tokens are cheap and output tokens are expensive.

      “Microsoft: Explain trade-offs in model selection”

      Capability, latency, cost, context, structured-output and tool reliability, data residency, and provider risk — and you cannot maximize all of them, so you pick the cheapest, fastest model that clears the quality bar on your own eval set.

      The specific tensions: a bigger model raises quality and raises both latency and cost; a longer context window helps recall but costs tokens on every call and often degrades attention to the middle of the prompt; a fine-tuned small model can beat a large general one on a narrow task while losing all flexibility; an open-weights self-hosted model buys residency and control and costs you idle capacity and an ops burden; a newer frontier model buys capability and costs you stability, since your prompts were tuned against the old one. There is also a strategic axis — concentrating on one provider is faster to build and a real dependency risk, which is why the abstraction layer and the pinned versions are part of the selection decision, not an afterthought.

      Going deeper: saying that model selection is a per-task decision inside a portfolio, not a company-wide standard, and that the eval set is what makes the trade-off measurable instead of aesthetic.

      “Microsoft: Walk through the system design of an AI experience”

      I use the same eight moves every time: user and job, quality bar and cost of failure, data flow end to end, what is even a model, failure modes with fallbacks, latency and cost budgets with numbers, evaluation, and what I cut for v1.

      Made concrete on the unread-summary example: the user is someone with 200 unread messages who needs to know what needs them; the quality bar is recall on action items because a missed deadline is the expensive failure; the flow is assemble and clean the thread, rank cheaply, summarize the top few, cache, render with citations to the source message; the ranker is deliberately rules-based rather than a model; failure modes are timeout, malformed output and hallucinated obligations, each with a fallback to raw text; the budget is 300 ms perceived via precomputation and about six cents per active user per day; evaluation is 200 human-written summaries scored for action-item recall plus a thumbs-down stream; and v1 is direct messages, top three threads, paid tier, one language.

      Going deeper: checking in after the framing step, and narrating the trade-off at each choice rather than presenting a finished architecture. They are scoring the reasoning, not the boxes.

      “Google: Design a high-level system for Gemini responding”

      Request in, then: safety and abuse checks, cache lookup, intent and difficulty routing, context assembly, model call with streaming, tool calls if needed, output validation and safety filtering, response with citations, and logging into evaluation. Every stage has a timeout and a fallback.

      Walking it: authenticate and rate-limit, classify the request for policy and for difficulty, and check the response cache and the prompt-prefix cache before spending anything. Assemble context — system instructions, tool definitions, relevant history, retrieved documents — under an explicit token budget, since this is where cost is decided. Route by difficulty: small model for the majority, frontier model for the minority that needs it. Stream tokens so the first word arrives in under a second. If tools are needed, validate every argument against a schema, run them in parallel where independent, cap the number of steps. Filter and validate the output, attach citations, then log the request, the model version and the user signal into the eval and monitoring pipeline. Fallbacks throughout: a smaller model on capacity failure, retrieval-free answers when retrieval times out, and an honest error rather than a fabricated answer.

      Going deeper: putting caching and difficulty routing before the model call rather than after, and treating the logging path as a first-class component — that is the loop that improves the system.

      “OpenAI: What to do with a model with 10x capability at 10x cost?”

      Not a yes or no — a segmentation and unit-economics question. Route the small share of requests that genuinely need the capability to it, serve everything else cheaply, and check on my own eval set which segments actually improve.

      Request difficulty is unevenly distributed, so most traffic gets nothing from 10× and a minority is transformed by it. First I find that minority. Then I ask where value per request can support the price: some tasks are worth almost nothing more when done better, while others are unlocked entirely — a task nobody could automate at 85% accuracy becomes automatable at 99%. That is the real question: does this enable a new tier, a new segment, or a workflow that was previously impossible, rather than a slightly better version of today’s feature. Then I check whether willingness to pay actually exists rather than assuming a better product justifies a higher price. And I remember the time axis: frontier capability gets much cheaper on a fairly reliable schedule, so part of the decision is what I learn by building now versus what I can simply wait for.

      Going deeper: the eval-driven test. 10× on a public benchmark is frequently 1.1× on your task, so you measure per segment on your own set before committing a single dollar of margin.

      CH·09 / The Interview

      Explaining what you know — and building something worth explaining

      Everything before this chapter was knowledge. This one is delivery: a repeatable shape for explaining a technical idea, a script for the moment you do not know something, and a method for describing your own work with enough rigour that people believe you. Interviews run as the worked example throughout, because they are the most demanding version of the problem — but the underlying skill is explaining AI to people who do not build it, which you will need far more often.

      What you will be able to explain after this chapter

      • Walk me through an AI feature you shipped and the model choices behind it
      • Google: What AI agents have you built to be more productive?
      • Glean: Have you built any end-to-end agentic systems?
      • Amazon: When did you go deep into an ML system to diagnose a problem?

      What the room is actually scoring

      The sheet you have been working from looks like sixty facts to memorize. It is not. Almost none of those questions have a single right answer, and an interviewer who wanted recall would give you a written test instead of an hour of their time. Each question is a probe. The probe is cheap to ask and expensive to fake, and it is measuring four things.

      One: do you reason about systems and trade-offs. Every real decision in an AI product costs something. More retrieval costs latency. A bigger model costs money. Higher recall costs precision. The interviewer wants to hear you name the cost without being asked, because a product manager who only sees upside will ship things that fall over.

      Two: do you know where your knowledge ends. This is calibration. A candidate who says “I am confident about this part and genuinely unsure about that part” is safe to trust with a roadmap. A candidate whose confidence is flat across everything they say is not, because you cannot tell their guesses from their facts.

      Three: can you explain a technical thing to a non-technical person. This is not a soft skill bolted onto the job. It is a large part of the job. You will spend more hours translating between engineers and executives than doing anything else.

      Four: do you decide with evidence rather than vibes. The tell is whether numbers appear in your answers unprompted — a measurement, a rate, a baseline, a sample size — or whether everything is “it felt better.”

      Reframe the whole sheet in that light and it collapses. The retrieval, cost, evaluation and agent questions all reduce to the same three moves: name the mechanism, name the trade-off, name the measurement. Make those three moves reliably and you can also answer the questions that are not on the sheet, which is what will actually happen.

      The idea in one line. They are not testing whether you already have the answer. They are testing what you do with a question.

      The shape of an answer

      Under pressure, people either freeze or ramble. The cure for both is a shape you have rehearsed so many times that it runs without attention. Here is the one to use. Four beats, roughly one minute total, then silence.

      Beat 1 goes first for an unglamorous reason. The interviewer is deciding, within about ten seconds, whether you know this or not, and then spending the rest of the answer confirming that judgment. If you build up to your conclusion, the first ten seconds sound like someone assembling an opinion in real time. Even when the ending is excellent, the read is uncertainty. Put the conclusion in the first sentence and everything after it is heard as supporting evidence.

      Here is the shape on a question from the sheet: what is RAG and when would you use it?

      Direct: “RAG means you retrieve relevant documents at query time and paste them into the prompt, so the model answers from text you chose instead of from memory. You use it whenever the answer depends on facts that change or that the model was never trained on.” Mechanism: “You chunk your documents, embed each chunk into a vector, and at query time embed the question and pull the nearest chunks by similarity. Those chunks go into the context window with an instruction to answer only from them.” Limit: “It only fixes knowledge, not behavior or format — that is what fine-tuning is for. And retrieval quality caps the whole system: if the right chunk is not retrieved, no model can save the answer. Retrieval failure is the dominant failure mode in every RAG system I have built.” Instance: “In mine, chunking on fixed character counts split tables across chunks and accuracy dropped about fifteen points on table questions until I chunked on document structure instead.”

      And on a second one: how would you detect hallucination in production?

      Direct: “You cannot detect it perfectly, so you measure a proxy: how often the answer is unsupported by the retrieved source.” Mechanism: “Sample real traffic, and for each answer check whether every claim is entailed by the retrieved chunks — graded by humans on a small set, then by a model judge calibrated against those human grades so you can run it continuously.” Limit: “The judge is itself a model and agrees with humans maybe eighty to ninety percent of the time, so I treat the number as a trend line, not a truth.” Instance: “On my own hundred hand-labeled questions the judge and I disagreed on eleven, and nine of those were ambiguous sources rather than a wrong model.”

      Common misconception. Longer answers are not safer answers. Past about ninety seconds unprompted, every extra sentence is a new surface to be probed on, and you chose it yourself. Answer, then stop, and let them decide which part deserves more.

      The depth ladder, and what to do on the rung above your knowledge

      Technical interviewers do not ask random questions. They pick one topic and climb. Definition, then mechanism, then trade-off, then edge case. Each rung is a level deeper than the last, and they stop climbing when you stop being able to follow. That is the measurement: not whether you got to the top, but how high you got before the answers thinned out.

      Learn to hear which rung you are on, because the right length and register differ by rung. The cue is the question word. “What is” is rung one, and the answer should be one or two sentences — padding a definition reads as stalling. “How does” is rung two, and now you owe them the actual machinery. “Why did you choose” or “why not just” is rung three, and the answer must contain a comparison and a cost, not a preference. “What happens if” is rung four, and there is usually no clean answer — they want to watch you think.

      The sheet flags one specific place where candidates fall off the ladder and the interview ends: transformers and attention. It is the most common rung-two question in the field and the most commonly frozen on, because people learn the phrase “attention lets the model focus on relevant tokens” and have nothing underneath it. The moment the interviewer asks how the focusing works, there is silence. Chapter 1 exists precisely so that you can answer that: queries and keys producing dot-product scores, softmax turning those scores into weights that sum to one, weights mixing the value vectors. If you can say that out loud in thirty seconds without notes, you have removed the single most likely way this goes badly.

      When you do not know

      You will hit a rung above your knowledge in every interview; that is by design, since the interviewer is looking for your ceiling. The moment is scored heavily and most candidates handle it badly. There is a sequence that handles it well.

      Say plainly what you do not know. “I have not worked with quantization directly, so I do not know the accuracy loss at four bits.” Five seconds, no hedging, no apology. State what you do know that is adjacent. “What I do know is that it is a memory-and-speed trade against precision, and that the win comes from fitting a bigger model on smaller hardware.” Reason out loud toward a candidate answer. “So I would expect the loss to be small for generation quality on common tasks and larger for anything numerical or long-tail, because those depend on fine distinctions in the weights.” Label your confidence. “I would put that at maybe sixty percent — it is reasoning, not something I have measured.” Say what you would check. “I would run our own eval set at full precision and at four bits and look at where the gap concentrates, rather than trusting a published average.”

      That answer contains no facts about quantization and it is a good answer. It demonstrates calibration, first-principles reasoning, and an instinct to measure — three of the four things being scored.

      The single most damaging failure is bluffing. Technical interviewers probe exactly one level deeper than whatever you just said. That is the whole method. So a fabricated answer does not survive the next question, and when it collapses it retroactively poisons everything you said before it, because now they have to wonder which of your confident statements were also invented. One honest “I do not know” costs you almost nothing. One caught bluff costs you the interview.

      Explaining it to someone who does not care how it works

      Two questions on the sheet are explicitly translation tasks: explain hallucination to an executive, explain precision and recall to a stakeholder. They are there because this is the job, and because it is startlingly hard for people who have just learned the material. New knowledge wants to be displayed.

      The method has five steps and the last one is the hardest. Lead with the business consequence — what will go wrong, for whom, how often. Use one analogy, and only one; a second analogy does not add clarity, it signals that the first one failed. Quantify with a number they already care about: support tickets, hours, dollars, percent of users, not F1 or perplexity. State the trade-off as a choice that belongs to them, because that is what makes them a participant instead of an audience. Then stop.

      What you cut: the mechanism, the architecture, the model names, the benchmark, and your own cleverness. None of it survives contact with a person who has eleven minutes and four other decisions today. If they want the mechanism they will ask, and then you get to give it, and it lands much better as an answer to a question than as an unrequested lecture.

      Describing your own work credibly

      Now the hard part. Several questions on the sheet assume a professional history: walk me through a feature you shipped, what agents have you built, have you built end-to-end agentic systems, when did you go deep to diagnose an ML problem. If you have not shipped one inside a company, pretending otherwise is dishonest and, practically, fatal — a fabricated shipping story fails at rung three, when they ask why you chose that model over the obvious alternative.

      The genuinely useful answer is that these questions ask about demonstrated judgment, and judgment does not require a payroll. It requires having made decisions under real constraints, watched something break, measured the break, and changed your mind. You can manufacture all of that in a weekend at small scale. A well-analyzed project with three hundred users of one — you — described with real failure modes and real numbers, beats a vague retelling of a large company’s roadmap every single time, because the second one has no rung three.

      The idea in one line. Scale is not what makes a story credible. Specificity is. Build three or four small things where you can speak to every decision you made and every one you rejected.

      Here is what to build. Each one maps onto a chapter of this site, so building it doubles as studying, and each one unlocks a specific question on the sheet.

The candidate who inflates gets the opposite: expectations set high, then a slow collapse over four follow-up questions.

      Questions this chapter answers

      Try answering each one out loud before you open it. The gap between what you said and what is written below is the part you have not learned yet — that gap is the whole point of this section.

      “Walk me through an AI feature you shipped and the model choices behind it”

      “I have not shipped at company scale, so let me walk you through something I built and put in front of real users: a question-answering system over my school’s five hundred pages of policy documents, used by about forty students.”

      Then the structure. Situation: students could not find rules that existed, so the feature was retrieval-augmented answering with a citation on every response. Decisions and rejections: I started with the strongest available model to establish a quality ceiling, then tested a cheaper, smaller model against it on a hand-labeled set of eighty questions. The gap was about four points on answer accuracy and roughly eight times the cost, so I shipped the cheap model for retrieval-grounded questions and routed only multi-document synthesis questions to the expensive one. I rejected fine-tuning outright, because the problem was missing knowledge, not wrong behavior, and the policies change every term. What broke: fixed-size chunking split tables of deadlines in half, and accuracy on date questions was near zero until I chunked on headings instead. What I measured: retrieval hit rate at top five, answer accuracy against my labels, and cost per question. What I would do differently: build the eval set before writing any code, because I spent two weeks tuning against my own impressions.

      Going deeper: naming the option you rejected and why, and having a real number attached to the model choice. “I picked the cheaper model because it was good enough” is a vibe. “Four accuracy points for eight times the cost, and here is where I put the boundary” is a decision.

      “Google: What AI agents have you built to be more productive?”

      “One that I actually use daily: an agent that triages my reading backlog. It has three tools — fetch a URL, search my notes, and append to a file — and a hard cap of six steps per item.”

      Say what it does, then say what you learned from its failures, because that is the real question. Mine reads a saved link, checks whether I already have notes on the topic, and writes a three-line summary plus a decision: read now, skim, or drop. The interesting part is the guardrails, and they all came from failures. It looped on paywalled pages, retrying the fetch tool forever, so I added a per-task step budget and a rule that a tool failing twice ends the task with an explicit error rather than a retry. It sometimes called the file-append tool with malformed arguments, so I validate tool arguments against a schema and hand the validation error back to the model as an observation, which recovers about four times in five. And it was expensive per item until I stopped feeding the whole page into context and started retrieving only the sections that matched my notes. Cost per item dropped by roughly two thirds with no drop in my agreement with its decisions, which I checked on thirty items by hand.

      Going deeper: talking about termination, error handling and cost per task rather than about capability. Anyone can describe an agent that works on a good day. Describing the stopping rule, the retry policy and the cost curve is what signals you have run one for more than an afternoon.

      “Glean: Have you built any end-to-end agentic systems?”

      “Not in production, and I want to be clear about that. I have built an end-to-end loop in the technical sense — plan, act, observe, repeat, with real tools, a stopping rule and a fallback — running for one user at small volume.”

      Draw the loop, because “end-to-end” is the operative word and they want to know whether you understand what it includes. Mine takes a research question, decomposes it into sub-questions, runs a search tool on each, writes intermediate findings to a scratch file, and produces a cited summary. End-to-end means the parts people skip: a defined input contract, a state store that survives between steps so the model is not re-deriving context, schema validation on every tool call, an explicit termination condition, and a fallback that returns the partial findings with a warning rather than nothing when the budget runs out. The honest limits: no concurrency, no permissions model, no human-in-the-loop approval step, and I have never run it against a corpus large enough for retrieval quality to degrade. Those four are exactly what I would expect to be hard at Glean’s scale, and I would rather name them than pretend I have solved them.

      Going deeper: volunteering the boundary before they find it. Saying “no permissions model, no approval step, and I have not stressed retrieval at scale” converts a gap into evidence of judgment. Waiting for them to discover the same gap converts it into evidence of overclaiming.

      “Amazon: When did you go deep into an ML system to diagnose a problem?”

      “My document QA system started giving confidently wrong answers about deadlines, and it took me a week to find out that the model was fine and retrieval was the problem.”

      Tell it as a diagnosis, in order, because the order is the skill being tested. First I reproduced it: collected fourteen failing questions instead of working from a feeling that it was worse. Then I isolated the stage. I fed the model the correct source text by hand, and it answered all fourteen correctly, which ruled out generation and pointed at retrieval. Then I measured: for each failing question I checked whether the correct chunk was in the top five retrieved, and it was in only three of fourteen. Then I looked at the chunks themselves and found that deadlines lived in tables, and my fixed-length chunking had split every table so that the dates ended up in one chunk and the column headers in another — the retrieved text was literally meaningless. The fix was structure-aware chunking plus keeping headers with rows. Then I verified rather than assuming: retrieval hit rate on those fourteen went from three to twelve, and I re-ran the full eighty-question set to make sure I had not broken anything else, which I had not. The generalizable lesson is that in a multi-stage system, you localize before you fix, and the loudest symptom is almost never where the bug lives.

      Going deeper: the ruling-out step. Bypassing retrieval to test generation in isolation is what a debugger does; guessing and changing the prompt is what everyone else does. Also: re-running the full eval after the fix, so you can say you did not trade one failure for another.

      Twenty lines that answer a hundred questions

      These are not slogans. Each one is a mechanism stated in a sentence, and almost every question on the sheet is a consequence of one or two of them. Hold these properly and you can reason your way to an answer you have never rehearsed.

      1. The model predicts one token at a time and has no plan. Structure, arguments and conclusions are what next-token prediction looks like from the outside after ten thousand repetitions; nothing in the architecture holds an intention about sentence forty.
      2. Weights are frozen during inference. Your conversation, your documents and your tool results change nothing inside the model. The only thing that ever changes at runtime is the text in the context window.
      3. The context window is the entire working memory, and it is a budget you allocate. System prompt, tool schemas, history, retrieved chunks and the user’s question compete for the same finite space, which is why context engineering is a prioritization job, not a writing job.
      4. Temperature changes the flatness of a probability distribution, not creativity. It divides the logits before softmax: low values sharpen the model’s existing preference, high values flatten it — and flatter includes flatter toward nonsense.
      5. Temperature zero is not reproducibility. Greedy decoding removes the sampling noise, but batching, floating-point non-associativity on GPUs, a silently updated model version and any change upstream in your prompt all remain.
      6. The model never executes a tool — your code does. It emits a name and a JSON argument object; your runtime decides whether to run it, with which credentials, and what happens when it fails. That boundary is where permissions and audit live.
      7. Assume model output is untrusted input. Anything the model writes may have been shaped by a retrieved document or a user message, so validate it against a schema and authorize the action in deterministic code — never let a sentence be the authorization.
      8. Fine-tuning teaches form; retrieval teaches facts; prompting teaches neither durably. “Wrong tone, wrong format, wrong shape” is a fine-tuning problem. “Wrong or stale information” is a retrieval problem. Confusing the two burns a quarter.
      9. Hallucination is an error rate you engineer, not a defect you patch. A system trained to produce plausible continuations has no internal flag for “unsupported”, so you lower the rate with grounding, constrained output and permission to abstain — then you measure what is left.
      10. A sterner prompt cannot fix a mechanism. Instructions can shift behavior at the margin, but no wording gives the model access to a fact it does not have or a self-check it does not possess.
      11. Measure retrieval and generation separately or you will fix the wrong half. Retrieval gets recall at k and rank-aware scores; generation gets faithfulness against the retrieved text. One blended “quality” number tells you nothing actionable.
      12. Retrieval is a search problem with decades of prior art. Chunking, lexical matching, hybrid scoring and reranking come from information retrieval, and BM25 is a serious baseline that embeddings frequently fail to beat on their own.
      13. The eval set outlives every prompt and every model. Prompts and model versions are consumables; the labeled cases that define “good” for your product are the only asset that compounds, and they are what makes a model swap a Tuesday instead of a crisis.
      14. Offline green plus unhappy users means your eval set is not your traffic. The gap is a bug in the eval, not in the users. Sample real failures, label them, and the set becomes the thing that would have caught it.
      15. A chain’s reliability is the product of its steps. Ninety-five percent per step over ten steps is about sixty percent end to end, so the fix is almost always fewer steps and a verification gate — not more agents.
      16. Non-determinism means you test distributions and properties, not equality. Run the case many times and assert on pass rates, invariants, schema validity and cited-source overlap; assert output == expected is not available to you.
      17. “Agent” is a claim about control flow, not about intelligence. If the model decides what happens next and the loop runs until a goal is met, it is an agent. If you decided the order in advance, it is a chain, however clever the prompts are.
      18. Latency is a budget you allocate, with a p95 and a named owner for each slice. Retrieval, first token, generation and post-processing each get a number; streaming buys you tolerance rather than speed, and perceived latency is what the user actually judges.
      19. Cost per resolved request is the metric, not cost per call. A cheaper model that retries twice and then escalates to a human is the expensive option, and a more expensive model that ends the conversation can be the cheap one.
      20. The senior answer sometimes contains no language model. Structured features plus historical labels means gradient-boosted trees on cost, latency, accuracy and auditability; knowing when not to reach for an LLM is the clearest seniority signal in the room.

      If you remember one thing from the twenty. Two of them are the spine: the model only predicts tokens, and everything else in the product is code you wrote around that fact. Almost every question is asking which of those two you are talking about.

      The traps

      Each of these is a specific claim that collapses under one follow-up question. They are all reasonable-sounding, which is exactly why they are tested.

      • Trap: “Temperature controls how creative the model is.” Actually: it divides the logits before softmax, flattening or sharpening a distribution the model already had. It cannot add an idea that was not already in the top of the ranking.
      • Trap: “Set temperature to zero and the output is deterministic.” Actually: greedy decoding only removes the sampling step. Batch composition, floating-point ordering on GPUs, a silently updated model version and any upstream prompt change all still move the output.
      • Trap: “The model learns from our conversations and gets better at our domain.” Actually: weights are frozen at inference. Nothing persists between calls except what your code puts back into the context window.
      • Trap: “RAG solves hallucination.” Actually: it changes the failure mode from invention to unfaithfulness — a confident answer that the retrieved documents do not support, or a confident answer built from the wrong document. That failure needs its own metric.
      • Trap: “We will fine-tune it so it knows our documentation.” Actually: fine-tuning teaches form, not facts. The result is stale on the day it ships, cannot cite a source, and must be redone every time the docs or the base model change. Retrieve instead.
      • Trap: “The model calls our API.” Actually: the model emits a function name and a JSON argument object. Your code parses it, checks permissions, calls the API, and feeds the result back. That is where authorization and audit live — not in the prompt.
      • Trap: “We will add a stronger instruction to the system prompt.” Actually: a prompt cannot give the model a fact it lacks or a self-check it does not have. Prompt changes are a real tool at the margin and a non-answer to a mechanism problem.
      • Trap: “We are at 95% accuracy, so we are ready to launch.” Actually: accuracy on which set, collected how, and which 5% is failing. A single number with no error-cost asymmetry and no provenance for the test set is not a launch decision.
      • Trap: “Context windows are huge now, so we can drop retrieval.” Actually: attention work grows with the square of the sequence, so cost and latency rise steeply, relevance degrades when the important span is buried in the middle, and you lose citations and access control. Retrieval is a precision tool, not a workaround for a small window.
      • Trap: “We use an LLM as judge, so the evaluation is objective.” Actually: the judge is a model with position bias, a preference for longer answers, and failure modes correlated with the system it is grading. It is useful once you have calibrated it against human labels and know its agreement rate.
      • Trap: “It is unreliable, so we will add another agent to check it.” Actually: each additional step multiplies in another failure probability, and a checker that shares the generator’s blind spots checks nothing. Prefer fewer steps and a deterministic verification.
      • Trap: “Prompt injection is a jailbreak problem — a content filter’s job.” Actually: injection targets your tools and your data. The attack arrives inside a retrieved document or a user file and asks the model to use the permissions you gave it. The mitigation is least-privilege tools and deterministic checks, not a better filter.
      • Trap: “Let us pick the best model first, then build.” Actually: the frontier moves monthly. What stays constant is your eval set, your data pipeline, your product surface and your unit economics — build those so that swapping the model is a config change and a test run.

      The pattern behind all thirteen. Every trap replaces a mechanism with a wish. The correction is always the same move: name what the mechanism can and cannot do, then put the fix in the layer that can actually hold it.

      Glossary

      Every technical term this site uses, defined to be understandable cold. If you can give a version of each of these out loud in one breath, you know more than the sheet asks for.

      Agent
      A system where the model decides what happens next — which tool to call, whether to continue — in a loop that runs until a goal is met or a limit is hit. The distinction from a chatbot is control flow, not intelligence.
      Attention
      The mechanism that lets each position in the sequence read from every other position. Each token produces a query vector, compares it against every token’s key vector by dot product, normalizes those scores into weights that sum to one, and takes that weighted mix of value vectors. A soft, learned lookup table.
      Autoregressive
      Generating output one unit at a time, where each new unit is conditioned on everything produced so far. It is why generation cannot be parallelized across output tokens, and why long answers take proportionally longer.
      Batching
      Running many requests through the model together to keep the hardware busy. It is the main reason inference is affordable, and one reason identical prompts can produce different outputs: your request’s neighbors change the arithmetic.
      BM25
      A classical keyword-ranking function that scores documents by term frequency, adjusted for how rare each term is and how long the document is. Decades old, cheap, and a serious baseline that embedding search often fails to beat alone — especially on names, codes and exact terms.
      Calibration
      Whether a system’s stated confidence matches its actual accuracy. A well-calibrated model is right about 70% of the time when it says 70%. Language models are typically fluent regardless of correctness, which is why their tone is not a confidence signal.
      Chain
      A fixed sequence of model calls that you defined in advance, each step’s output feeding the next. Reliability multiplies across the steps, so shorter chains are better chains.
      Chain of thought
      Prompting the model to produce intermediate reasoning before its answer. It helps because the intermediate tokens become part of the context the final tokens condition on — it is computation, not introspection, and the stated reasoning is not a reliable account of what happened inside.
      Chunking
      Splitting source documents into passages small enough to retrieve and inject. The chunk is the unit of retrieval, so bad chunk boundaries silently cap how good the whole system can be. Overlapping chunks reduce the risk of cutting an answer in half.
      Circuit breaker
      A pattern that stops calling a failing dependency after a threshold of errors, returning a fallback immediately instead. It keeps one slow model or tool from consuming every worker and taking the product down with it.
      Confusion matrix
      The two-by-two table of true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives. Every classification metric is a ratio drawn from these four cells, which is why you should draw it before arguing about a number.
      Context engineering
      Deciding what occupies the model’s finite input on each call, and in what priority: instructions, tools, history, retrieved evidence, the user’s question. Prompt engineering is wording; context engineering is allocation, and it is the larger discipline.
      Context window
      The maximum number of tokens the model can attend to in one call, counting input and output together. It is the entire working memory of the system; nothing outside it exists as far as that call is concerned.
      Cosine similarity
      A similarity score between two vectors equal to the cosine of the angle between them, so it measures direction and ignores length. The standard way to compare embeddings: 1 is identical direction, 0 is unrelated.
      Decoding
      The process of turning the model’s probability distribution into actual output tokens, one at a time. Greedy decoding always takes the top token; sampling draws from the distribution as shaped by temperature and top-p.
      Distillation
      Training a small model to imitate a larger one’s outputs. It is how a cheap fast model gets close to an expensive one on a narrow task, and a common way to make routing pay off.
      Embedding
      A list of numbers — typically a few hundred to a few thousand — representing a piece of text, image or token as a point in space, positioned so that similar meanings sit close together. It is what makes semantic search possible.
      Eval set
      A curated collection of inputs with agreed-upon expectations, used to measure quality repeatably. It is the asset that outlives every prompt and model in your product, and the thing that turns a model swap into a test run.
      F1
      The harmonic mean of precision and recall, 2PR/(P+R). A single number for ranking systems, which assumes both error types cost the same — state that assumption whenever you use it.
      Faithfulness
      Whether every claim in a generated answer is supported by the evidence provided to the model. Distinct from correctness: an answer can be true in the world and unfaithful to the retrieved documents, which is still a bug in a grounded system.
      Fallback
      The pre-decided behavior when the primary path fails: a smaller model, a cached answer, a keyword search, a human, or an honest error message. Designing the fallback is part of designing the feature, not an afterthought.
      False positive / false negative
      A false positive is acting when you should not have; a false negative is failing to act when you should have. Nearly every product decision in this domain is a choice about which of these two you would rather make more often.
      Few-shot
      Including a handful of worked examples in the prompt to show the model the pattern you want. It changes nothing in the weights; it changes what the next token is conditioned on.
      Fine-tuning
      Continuing to train a model on your own examples so it produces a specific form, style or narrow behavior by default. It teaches shape, not knowledge, and produces a versioned artifact you then have to maintain.
      Forward pass
      One complete run of the input through the network, producing scores for the next token. Every generated token is a fresh forward pass over the whole context, which is why both latency and cost scale with length.
      Function calling
      The interface where you describe available functions to the model and it responds with a structured request naming one and supplying arguments. The model never runs anything — the request is text your code chooses to honor.
      Golden dataset
      The trusted, human-verified subset of your eval set: inputs with reference answers everyone agrees are correct. Small, expensive, and the anchor against which cheaper automated judges are calibrated.
      GPU
      A processor with thousands of simple cores and very high memory bandwidth, suited to the huge parallel matrix multiplications that both training and inference are made of. In most products you rent it by the token rather than buying it.
      Gradient descent
      The training procedure: measure the prediction error, compute which direction each weight should move to reduce it, take a small step, repeat billions of times. This happens only during training — never during a conversation.
      Graph
      An orchestration pattern where steps are nodes with explicit shared state and edges that can branch, loop and checkpoint. Worth the complexity for long-running or resumable work; overkill for anything linear.
      Grounding
      Constraining an answer to specific supplied evidence, and being able to point at the span that supports each claim. Grounding is measurable — that is its main advantage over asking the model to be careful.
      Guardrail
      A deterministic check around the model: input filters, output schema validation, allowed-value lists, permission checks, rate limits, redlines. It belongs in code, because a model cannot reliably police a failure it shares.
      Hallucination
      Output that is fluent, confident and unsupported — either false about the world or unsupported by the provided evidence. It is a rate to be measured and reduced, not a bug with a fix, because the mechanism that produces good text also produces this.
      Human in the loop
      A design where a person approves, corrects or handles the cases the system is not confident about. Done well it is also your best data source: every edit is a labeled example.
      Hybrid search
      Combining keyword scoring such as BM25 with embedding similarity, then merging the ranked lists. It wins because the two methods fail differently: keywords nail exact identifiers, embeddings catch paraphrase.
      Idempotency
      The property that performing an operation twice has the same effect as performing it once, usually via a client-supplied key. Essential with agents, because retries and duplicate tool calls are normal, and a double refund is not.
      Inference
      Using a trained model to produce output. All weights are frozen; the only variables are your input and the sampling settings. Nearly all of your production cost lives here.
      Instruction tuning
      The post-training stage that turns a raw text predictor into something that follows requests, by training on many examples of instruction and good response. It is why a base model completes your sentence while a tuned model answers your question.
      JSON Schema
      A machine-readable specification of what a JSON object must contain: fields, types, required keys, allowed values. It is how you describe tool arguments to a model and how you validate what comes back.
      KV cache
      The stored key and value vectors for tokens already processed, so each new token does not recompute the whole sequence. It trades memory for speed, and its size is a major consumer of VRAM at long context lengths.
      Latency budget
      An explicit allocation of the total time a user will tolerate across the stages of the request — retrieval, first token, generation, post-processing — usually stated at p95 rather than average. Without one, latency is discovered rather than designed.
      LLM
      Large language model: a neural network with billions of parameters trained to predict the next token over vast amounts of text. Everything it does at runtime is that one operation, repeated.
      LLM-as-judge
      Using a model to grade another model’s output against a rubric. Cheap and scalable enough to run on every commit, but it has position and verbosity biases and failure modes correlated with the system it grades, so it must be calibrated against human labels.
      Logits
      The raw, unbounded scores the model outputs — one per token in the vocabulary — before softmax turns them into probabilities. Temperature is applied to these numbers.
      MCP (Model Context Protocol)
      An open protocol that standardizes how applications expose tools, data and prompts to models, so one server can serve any compatible client. It converts an N-times-M integration problem into N plus M.
      Mixture of experts
      An architecture where each token is routed to a small subset of specialized sub-networks rather than the whole model. It gives a very large total parameter count at a much smaller per-token compute cost, which is why parameter counts alone tell you little about speed or price.
      Model routing
      Classifying each request and sending it to the cheapest model that can handle it, escalating only when needed. The lever that most often fixes unit economics at scale — and the classifier itself needs its own eval.
      MRR (mean reciprocal rank)
      A retrieval metric: for each query, take one divided by the rank of the first relevant result, then average across queries. It rewards putting a good result at the very top.
      Multi-head attention
      Running several attention computations in parallel with different learned projections, then combining them. Different heads end up tracking different kinds of relationship — syntax, coreference, position — in the same layer.
      NDCG
      Normalized discounted cumulative gain: a ranking metric that credits relevant results more when they appear higher, and normalizes against the best possible ordering. Use it when relevance is graded rather than yes-or-no.
      Nearest neighbor
      The search operation behind semantic retrieval: given a query vector, find the closest vectors in the index. At scale it is done approximately, trading a little recall for large gains in speed.
      Next-token prediction
      The single training objective behind language models: given a sequence, predict what comes next. Everything that looks like reasoning, planning or style is a consequence of doing this extremely well.
      Observability
      Being able to answer, after the fact, what happened on a specific request: the exact prompt, the retrieved chunks, the tool calls, the tokens, the cost, the latency, the output, the user reaction. Without it, quality debugging is guesswork.
      Offline vs online eval
      Offline evaluation runs a fixed set of cases before shipping, so it is repeatable and blocks regressions. Online evaluation measures real traffic after shipping — edit rates, escalations, thumbs, task completion — so it is honest but slow and confounded. You need both, and the gap between them is information.
      Overfitting
      When a model or a prompt is tuned so tightly to the examples in front of you that it performs worse on anything new. In this domain it happens most often to prompts and to eval sets that have been optimized against too many times.
      p50 / p95 latency
      The median and the 95th percentile of response time. The p95 is the number that decides whether a product feels broken, because the slow tail is where the complaints and the timeouts are.
      Parameter
      One learned number inside the network — a weight or a bias. Parameter count is a rough proxy for capacity and memory footprint, and a poor proxy for quality, cost or speed.
      Post-training
      Everything done after pre-training to make the model usable: supervised fine-tuning on instructions, preference-based training, safety work, tool-use training. It shapes behavior; pre-training created the capability.
      Precision
      Of the items the system flagged or returned, the fraction that were correct: TP/(TP+FP). The metric that governs wasted effort and eroded trust.
      Pre-training
      The long, expensive first stage: next-token prediction over an enormous text corpus, producing a model with broad capability and no manners. This is where the knowledge comes from, and it ends at a fixed cutoff date.
      Prompt caching
      Reusing the computed state for a stable prefix — typically your system prompt and tool definitions — across many requests, at a reduced price. It rewards putting the unchanging part of your context first.
      Prompt injection
      An attack where instructions hidden in content the model reads — a retrieved document, a web page, a user file — are treated as if they came from you. The defense is least-privilege tools and deterministic authorization, because the model cannot reliably tell instruction from data.
      Quantization
      Storing weights at lower numeric precision, for example 8 or 4 bits instead of 16, to cut memory and increase speed. It usually costs a little quality, and it is how large models fit on smaller hardware.
      RAG
      Retrieval-augmented generation: search your own data for relevant passages at request time and put them in the prompt, so the answer is grounded in current, private, citable text rather than in the model’s frozen memory.
      Recall
      Of the items that should have been found or flagged, the fraction that were: TP/(TP+FN). The metric that governs missed harm and missed value.
      Recall at k
      The retrieval version of recall: of the passages known to be relevant, how many appear in the top k results. Measured on retrieval alone, before the model sees anything.
      Redline
      A behavior the product must never exhibit, defined in advance and enforced deterministically, with a specified fallback and no confidence threshold that allows it. Distinct from a quality target, which is a dial.
      Reranking
      A second, more expensive scoring pass over the top candidates from cheap retrieval, using a model that reads query and passage together. It is the highest-leverage single upgrade in most RAG pipelines, because it fixes precision without re-indexing anything.
      RLHF
      Reinforcement learning from human feedback: humans rank candidate responses, a reward model learns those preferences, and the model is optimized against it. It is where helpfulness, tone and much refusal behavior come from.
      Sampling
      Choosing the next token by drawing from the probability distribution rather than always taking the most likely one. This is the source of the variation you see between identical prompts, and it lives outside the model.
      Semantic search
      Retrieving by meaning rather than by exact words, by embedding the query and finding nearby document vectors. It finds paraphrase, and it can miss the exact product code a keyword index would have caught.
      SFT
      Supervised fine-tuning: training on pairs of input and desired output. The workhorse method for teaching a model a specific format, register or narrow task.
      Skill
      A packaged set of instructions, and often scripts and resources, that a model loads into its context when a task calls for it. It changes no weights — it is text and files delivered at runtime.
      Softmax
      The function that converts a vector of logits into a probability distribution: exponentiate each score, then divide by the total, so all values are positive and sum to one. Because it exponentiates, small logit gaps become large probability gaps.
      Structured output
      Constraining generation so the result is valid JSON matching a schema, often by restricting which tokens are permitted at each step. It turns a parsing problem into a guarantee, and it is how model output becomes safe to hand to code.
      System prompt
      The instructions placed before the conversation that set role, rules, format and available context. It is ordinary tokens in the same window, with more weight by convention and training, not a privileged channel the model cannot ignore.
      Temperature
      A number that divides the logits before softmax. Below one it sharpens the distribution toward the top token; above one it flattens it toward the tail. It changes the shape of a distribution, never the model’s knowledge.
      Token
      The unit a model actually reads and writes: a common word, a word fragment, a punctuation mark. Roughly four characters of English on average. Tokens are the billing unit, the context unit, and the reason the model cannot reliably count letters.
      Tokenizer
      The deterministic component that splits text into tokens from a fixed vocabulary and maps them to integers. It does no learning at runtime, and its quirks explain a surprising share of odd model behavior on numbers, code and non-English text.
      Tool definition
      The name, description and argument schema you give the model for a function it may request. The description is the only thing the model has to decide when to use it, so a vague description is a production bug, not a documentation issue.
      Top-p
      Nucleus sampling: keep only the smallest set of most likely tokens whose probabilities sum to p, then sample from that set. It adapts to the distribution — narrow when the model is confident, wide when it is not.
      Trace
      The full recorded record of one request through your system: prompt, retrieved context, model calls, tool calls, tokens, timings, cost and output. The unit of debugging, and the raw material of new eval cases.
      Transformer
      The architecture underneath modern language models: stacked layers, each combining attention over the whole sequence with a position-wise feed-forward network. Its key property is that all positions are processed in parallel during training.
      Unit economics
      The cost and revenue of a single unit of usage — per request, per user, per resolved case — rather than in aggregate. The number that decides whether a feature can exist on a free tier.
      Vector database
      A store built for approximate nearest-neighbor search over embeddings, with metadata filtering and updatable indexes. Needed because comparing a query against millions of vectors one at a time is too slow to serve.
      VRAM
      The memory on a GPU. It has to hold the weights plus the KV cache for every in-flight request, which is why long contexts and large batches are memory-bound and why quantization matters.
      Weights
      The learned numbers that constitute the model. They are set during training and frozen during inference — the single fact that answers most questions about what a model can remember about you.
      XGBoost
      A widely used gradient-boosted decision tree library, and shorthand for the classical approach to tabular prediction. Fast, cheap, auditable, and still the right answer for churn, fraud and pricing problems with labeled history.
      Zero-shot
      Asking the model to do a task with no examples, relying entirely on the instruction and what pre-training left behind. The baseline you should always measure before adding examples, retrieval or fine-tuning.

      The last thing. Nothing on this page will make you sound experienced, because you cannot fake having shipped. What it does is make you accurate — and an accurate junior who says “I have not measured that, here is how I would” beats a confident one who has memorized a price list. Build the small thing, keep its eval set, and the experience stops being borrowed.
